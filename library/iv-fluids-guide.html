01

Normal Saline

0.9%, Na 154, Cl 154, isotonic

PreferredAvoid / caution
Head injury, metabolic alkalosis, hypercalcemia, with blood transfusionLarge-volume resuscitation, hyperchloremic acidosis

Not actually "normal": its chloride sits far above plasma (plasma Cl is roughly 100-106 mEq/L). The harm from large volumes is dose-dependent, driven by chloride load causing a non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis.

02

Ringer's Lactate

Na 130, K 4, Ca 3, lactate 28 mEq/L, mildly hypotonic

PreferredAvoid / caution
Resuscitation, surgery/trauma, burns, GI lossesHead injury, same line as blood, liver failure

Its potassium content is not a reason to withhold it in hyperkalemia; the added potassium is small relative to the volume given and does not meaningfully worsen hyperkalemia in practice.

Avoid running LR through the same line as a blood transfusion because its calcium can theoretically overcome the citrate anticoagulant in stored blood and promote clotting in the line, though the clinical significance of this is debated; avoid in significant liver failure because lactate metabolism to bicarbonate depends on hepatic function.

03

Plasma-Lyte (/ Kabilyte)

Na 140, K 5, acetate 27, lactate-free, calcium-free

PreferredAvoid / caution
Resuscitation, liver failure, renal transplant, with bloodHead injury, cost/access

Calcium-free, so it can safely run with blood products, and it buffers via acetate metabolized peripherally in muscle rather than lactate metabolized in the liver, so it needs no liver to buffer.

04

5% Dextrose

50 g/L glucose, becomes free water

PreferredAvoid / caution
Hypernatremia, hypoglycemia, free-water deficit, drug diluentResuscitation, hyponatremia, cerebral edema

Not a resuscitation fluid. Once the glucose is metabolized, almost none of the water stays in the intravascular space; it distributes across total body water, making it a poor choice whenever intravascular volume is the goal.

05

Half-Normal Saline (0.45%)

Na 77, Cl 77, hypotonic

PreferredAvoid / caution
Maintenance, hypernatremia, free-water replacementResuscitation, raised ICP, hyponatremia risk

A maintenance fluid. Never use it to resuscitate; its low effective tonicity means much of it moves out of the vascular space quickly.

06

Hypertonic Saline 3%

Na 513, Cl 513, hypertonic

PreferredAvoid / caution
Symptomatic hyponatremia, raised ICP/edemaRapid correction, volume overload

Lift sodium no faster than 8 mEq/L in 24 hours, or risk osmotic demyelination syndrome. This is the standard limit for patients at elevated risk (severe hyponatremia, liver disease, alcoholism, malnutrition); some guidelines allow up to 10-12 mEq/L/24h in average-risk patients, but 8 is the safer number to default to.

07

Albumin

5% iso-oncotic, 20-25% hyperoncotic

PreferredAvoid / caution
Large-volume paracentesis, SBP, hepatorenal syndromeHead injury, routine resuscitation

A targeted drug, not a routine resuscitation fluid. Its role is specific: replacing oncotic pressure lost with large-volume paracentesis, preventing renal impairment after spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, and treating hepatorenal syndrome, not general-purpose volume expansion.

The TBI/sepsis reversal, quantified: a 2020 network meta-analysis across sepsis, surgical, and trauma populations found the "best" fluid actually reverses by population. In sepsis, balanced crystalloids reduced mortality more than saline (OR 0.84) and more than hydroxyethyl starch (OR 0.81). In traumatic brain injury specifically, that flips: saline and starch achieved lower mortality than albumin and balanced crystalloids, with saline significantly outperforming albumin (OR 0.55). This is the trial-level evidence behind the "avoid balanced fluid in head injury" trap in section 13.

The practical read: "always use balanced crystalloid" is a sepsis-shaped default that doesn't transfer to TBI, where saline's higher effective tonicity may matter more than a balanced solution's acid-base profile. Fluid choice is population-specific, not universal.

09

Permissive Hypotension

Bickell et al., NEJM 1994 — the foundational trial: 598 adults with penetrating torso trauma and prehospital SBP ≤90 mmHg, randomized to immediate prehospital/ED fluid resuscitation vs. delayed resuscitation withheld until operative control. Survival to discharge was significantly higher in the delayed group (70% vs. 62%, p=0.04).

The theory: aggressive crystalloid before hemorrhage control raises blood pressure enough to dislodge a forming clot ("popping the clot"), while also driving dilutional coagulopathy, dilutional anemia, and hypothermia — the same lethal triad damage control resuscitation is built to avoid.

Real limitations of the founding trialWhy it matters
Single center, single EMS system, penetrating torso trauma onlyLimited external validity to blunt trauma or systems with different transport times
Used crystalloid, not modern hemostatic/blood-component resuscitationThe comparison being tested has moved on since 1994 — predates the blood-ratio evidence in section 10
Blood pressure was actually similar between groups regardless of allocation A frequently overlooked methodologic detail — raises the question of what, mechanistically, actually drove the mortality difference

Current practice generally applies permissive hypotension (targeting SBP ~80–90 mmHg or MAP ~50 in pure hemorrhagic shock per EAST guidance) until hemorrhage is controlled — with an important carve-out for concurrent traumatic brain injury, where SBP <90 is independently associated with worse outcomes and small aliquots (100–200 mL) to maintain SBP above 90 are favored instead.

10

Blood Product Ratios

PROPPR (Holcomb et al., JAMA 2015): in-hospital massive transfusion trial comparing plasma:platelets:RBC ratios of 1:1:1 vs. 1:1:2. No statistically significant difference in 24-hour or 30-day mortality between ratios, though more patients in the 1:1:1 group achieved hemostasis and fewer died from exsanguination in the first 24 hours.

Despite PROPPR not reaching significance on its primary mortality endpoint, balanced-ratio transfusion (approximating whole blood composition) has been widely adopted as practice — built substantially on observational data with real survivorship-bias risk (patients have to survive long enough to receive the later components of a ratio). A smaller earlier RCT (Nascimento 2013) comparing a 1:1:1 target to standard care actually found higher, though not statistically significant, mortality in the balanced-ratio group — a reminder that the observational enthusiasm for balanced ratios outran the randomized evidence for some time.

Prehospital Plasma: A Genuinely Contradictory Record

This is the section most relevant to evaluating any new plasma product's real-world promise (freeze-dried or otherwise), and it's considerably messier than "prehospital plasma saves lives."

TrialDesignResult
PAMPer (NEJM 2018)Cluster-randomized, air-medical transport, 2 units thawed plasma vs. standard carePositive — reduced 30-day mortality with prehospital plasma
COMBAT (2018)2 units thawed AB plasma prehospital vs. standard crystalloid care, ground EMSNegative — stopped early for futility; no outcome difference; groups imbalanced at baseline (plasma arm sicker)
RePHILL (Lancet Haematology 2022)UK phase 3 RCT, PRBC + lyophilized plasma vs. saline, prehospitalNegative — did not demonstrate improved survival or lactate clearance; stopped early (COVID-19 disruption)

Worth sitting with: RePHILL is the trial that actually tested lyophilized plasma in the prehospital setting, and it was negative on both primary outcomes. This doesn't mean freeze-dried plasma products won't help; RePHILL tested one specific product (Lyoplas) in one health system with its own transport times, and was underpowered by early stopping. But it's a directly relevant data point that a clean "freeze-dried plasma saves lives" framing skips entirely.

A post hoc analysis pooling PAMPer and COMBAT found the prehospital plasma survival benefit was concentrated in patients with transport times longer than 20 minutes — suggesting the effect, where real, may be distance/time-dependent rather than universal. A second lyophilized-plasma trial, PREHO-PLYO (France, 2022, 134 patients), was also negative: median admission INR was 1.21 with lyophilized plasma versus 1.20 with saline.

Prehospital, Transport & Combat Limits

The "right" fluid on paper isn't always the fluid you can actually carry, keep stable, or afford the weight of

The doctrine has already moved past crystalloid for combat hemorrhagic shock. TCCC Guidelines Change 21-01 (2021) designated cold-stored low-titer group O whole blood (CS-LTOWB) as the preferred resuscitation fluid, with fresh LTOWB as the first alternate — and explicitly removed crystalloids and Hextend as recommended options for hemorrhagic shock. The question below isn't really "which crystalloid to carry" at the point of injury anymore; it's "how far down the evacuation chain can a blood product realistically travel before logistics force a fallback to something else."

EchelonStorage realityWhat that means for fluid choice
Ground ambulanceCabin temperatures logged as low as −13.2°C and as high as +50.6°C in real-world services; the vehicle interior exceeded the recommended 25°C storage ceiling for roughly half of total transport time in one study. Bagged crystalloid (saline) held up chemically even under sustained 70°C or cyclic 70/22°C stress testing for a month — sodium/chloride stayed within label range, no precipitation. The bigger unresolved risk is sub-freezing exposure (bag/tubing integrity, IV catheter function), not chemical breakdown from heat. Many progressive EMS systems now carry a small cooler of RBCs/plasma alongside crystalloid, accepting the added cold-chain burden for the survival benefit.
Civilian HEMS (helicopter EMS)Typically a climate-controlled cabin, but payload is constrained by aircraft performance margins — weight and cube compete directly with fuel, crew, and equipment.Increasingly carries low-titer O whole blood or a plasma/RBC combination under dedicated air-medical transfusion programs, precisely because the climate control removes the ambulance's temperature problem — the limiting factor here is payload weight and programmatic cost, not thermal stability.
Military rotary-wing MEDEVAC/CASEVACFaces the ambulance's thermal extremes and the civilian aircraft's payload limits simultaneously, often without the benefit of a fully climate-controlled cabin, plus genuine tactical constraints on dwell time at the landing zone.CS-LTOWB is doctrine-preferred per TCCC 21-01 where the cold chain can be maintained; when it can't, room-temperature- stable freeze-dried plasma and reduced breakage risk (section 11) become the more realistic fallback than frozen components.
Foot / dismounted / light transport (combat medic aid bag)The most severe constraint of all: doctrine explicitly requires equipment be "simple, light, and rugged," carried in a backpack over body armor, often under hostile fire. There is no cold chain at this echelon, full stop.Whole blood is essentially impossible to carry here — no refrigeration, no practical shelf life. This is the exact gap freeze-dried plasma is built to close: no freezer, no thaw cycle, plastic packaging that survives being carried and dropped, reconstituted in about a minute. Crystalloid remains the lightest option by weight but is no longer TCCC's preferred choice for hemorrhagic shock specifically.

Warming matters at every echelon. TCCC recommends a battery-powered warming device delivering fluid at 38°C; ATLS guidance for in-hospital use targets 39°C. In practice, a single 2 L bolus of room-temperature (21°C) crystalloid drops core body temperature by only about 0.3°C in healthy adults — a real but modest effect — so active warming is about avoiding the cumulative, multi-liter scenario and the added hypothermia risk of fluid that has equilibrated to a cold ambient environment (a winter ambulance cabin, a cold LZ), not a single small bolus in isolation.

The throughline across every echelon: the fluid that wins on paper (whole blood, or a balanced crystalloid) isn't always the fluid that survives the storage conditions, fits the weight budget, or can be resupplied at that point in the evacuation chain. Matching fluid choice to the environment is as real a clinical decision as matching it to the patient's physiology in sections 1-8.

13

The Traps

  • Starch (HES): avoid. Increases kidney injury and mortality, confirmed in the CHEST and 6S trials in critically ill and septic patients.
  • D5W for "dehydration": drops sodium. It's mostly free water once metabolized, the wrong fix for volume depletion.
  • Balanced fluid in head injury: use saline instead. The mild hypotonicity of balanced solutions like LR or Plasma-Lyte can worsen cerebral edema — see the quantified trial data in section 8.
  • Hypotonic fluid to resuscitate: won't fill the tank. It distributes out of the vascular space too quickly to restore intravascular volume.

Match the fluid to the patient — and to the environment you're actually operating in.
