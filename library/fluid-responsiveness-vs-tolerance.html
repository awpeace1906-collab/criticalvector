Fluid Responsiveness vs. Fluid Tolerance: Two Different Questions, One Bolus

PLR, PPV, SVV, EEOT, TVC, VExUS — no acronym left undefined. But first, the actual distinction they're all trying to answer.

Original post: definshock. This page is an independent third-party verification, not affiliated with or endorsed by the account.

Verdict: the source post's framing is exactly the distinction that matters most in modern resuscitation, and it's one that's genuinely easy to blur at 3am: "will this fluid increase cardiac output" and "can this patient's body actually accommodate more volume without harm" are two separate questions, answered by two separate categories of test.
01

The Core Distinction

QuestionWhat it measuresTools
Fluid responsivenessWill stroke volume/cardiac output meaningfully increase if I give this fluid? Answers whether the heart is on the steep or flat part of the Frank-Starling curve.Passive leg raise (PLR), pulse pressure variation (PPV), stroke volume variation (SVV), end-expiratory occlusion test (EEOT), tidal volume challenge (TVC)
Fluid toleranceCan the venous/organ system absorb more volume without harmful congestion? Answers whether upstream organs (lungs, liver, kidneys, gut) can accommodate the preload increase.VExUS (venous excess ultrasound), lung ultrasound B-lines, CVP trend, IVC characteristics in context

A fluid-responsive heart in a fluid-intolerant body is exactly the patient who gets a bolus, briefly bumps cardiac output, and then develops pulmonary edema and worsening renal congestion over the following hours — responsiveness answered "yes," tolerance should have answered "no."

02

The Responsiveness Toolkit, Confirmed

TestMechanismPractical note
Passive leg raise (PLR)Auto-transfuses ~150–300 mL of venous blood centrally; a reversible "virtual fluid challenge"Requires real-time cardiac output monitoring (not blood pressure alone) to be reliable; effect is transient (~1 min)
Pulse pressure variation (PPV) / stroke volume variation (SVV)Cyclic changes in venous return from positive-pressure ventilation are exaggerated when the heart is preload-dependentRequires controlled ventilation, regular rhythm, tidal volume ≥8 mL/kg, closed chest — invalid in spontaneous breathing, arrhythmia, low tidal volume lung-protective ventilation, or open chest/abdomen
End-expiratory occlusion test (EEOT)A 15-second end-expiratory hold removes the cyclic impediment to venous return, acting as a mini fluid challengeWorks even in low-tidal-volume ventilation, one of PPV/SVV's main blind spots
Tidal volume challenge (TVC)Transiently increasing tidal volume from 6 to 8 mL/kg amplifies PPV/SVV enough to restore their reliability in lung-protective ventilationA workaround specifically for the low-VT limitation of standard PPV/SVV
03

Why Both Questions Have to Be Asked

Responsiveness without tolerance is how iatrogenic congestion happens

The core argument for pairing these two categories: a positive fluid-responsiveness test tells you the heart can use more volume to raise output right now — it says nothing about whether the venous system, liver, kidneys, and gut can absorb that volume without becoming congested. VExUS grades venous congestion via hepatic, portal, and renal venous Doppler waveforms specifically to answer the tolerance side of the equation, and is a distinct question from anything PLR, PPV, SVV, EEOT, or TVC can tell you.

04

Bottom Line

The source post's framing is the right one to internalize: "are they fluid responsive" is only half the resuscitation decision. The full question every bolus should answer is responsiveness (will output actually go up) and tolerance (can the body afford it) — treating them as one question is exactly the pattern that produces over-resuscitated, congested ICU patients even when every individual bolus was given to a technically fluid-responsive heart.
