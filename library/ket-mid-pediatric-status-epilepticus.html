Ket-Mid: Should Ketamine Be First-Line, Not Rescue, in Pediatric Status Epilepticus?

Othman AA, et al. Combined Ketamine and Midazolam Versus Midazolam Alone for Initial Treatment of Pediatric Generalized Convulsive Status Epilepticus (Ket-Mid Study): A Randomized Controlled Trial.

Pediatric Neurology. June 2025. · Sohag University Hospital, Egypt, March 2023–August 2024 · Original social summary: RebelEM (Drs. Brendan Freeman & Katherine Zabinski), Aug 14

Verdict on the source post: RebelEM's numbers are correct and its "promising, not practice-changing" framing matches the trial's own stated conclusion. This audit adds the single-center caveat that determines how far this should travel outside its original setting.
01

The Setup

About a third of pediatric GCSE isn't controlled by first-line benzodiazepines. Ket-Mid asks whether ketamine belongs in that first dose, not the third.

Ketamine's current place in most status epilepticus protocols is refractory/super-refractory therapy — reached for after benzodiazepines and a second-line agent (levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, valproate per ESETT) have already failed, often 30–60+ minutes into the seizure. The Ket-Mid investigators' rationale: synaptic GABA-A receptors internalize and NMDA receptors upregulate as seizure duration lengthens, which is exactly why benzodiazepine efficacy falls off the longer a seizure runs. Their hypothesis was that adding an NMDA antagonist (ketamine) from the first dose, rather than waiting for BDZ monotherapy to fail, could pre-empt that receptor shift instead of chasing it.

02

Design

DesignSingle-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled superiority trial
Population144 children, 6 months–16 years, generalized convulsive status epilepticus, no prior ASM given for the current episode
GroupsKet-Mid: IV ketamine 2 mg/kg + IV midazolam 0.2 mg/kg (n=72) · Pla-Mid: placebo + IV midazolam 0.2 mg/kg (n=72)
Primary outcomeClinical seizure cessation at 5 minutes
Secondary outcomesCessation at 15/35/55 min, need for a second midazolam bolus, 24h seizure control, adverse effects, intubation
EtiologyPredominantly febrile seizures and known epilepsy; 11% CNS infection, 4% other/unknown
03

Primary & Key Secondary Results

OutcomeKet-MidPla-MidEffect
Seizure cessation at 5 min76%21%RR 3.7 (95% CI 2.3–5.9), P<.001
Endotracheal intubation4.2%20.8%≈5× fewer intubations

Cessation rates favored Ket-Mid at every subsequent checkpoint too (15, 35, and 55 minutes), and the Ket-Mid group required fewer additional midazolam doses to achieve control. The effect was described as most pronounced in patients whose GCSE had already run longer than 30 minutes — consistent with the receptor-kinetics rationale behind the trial.

04

The Caveat the Post Didn't Flag

A single 144-patient trial from one center is strong signal, not a protocol change.

Single-center, single-country, first-of-its-kind

The trial's own authors, and the independent reviews of it (St Emlyn's, SGEM #482), are explicit that this is the first RCT testing this specific combination as first-line therapy, conducted at one hospital in Egypt. There is no confirmatory data yet from a second site, a different healthcare system, or a US/European pediatric ED population. RebelEM's "promising, not practice-changing" line reflects exactly this: strong effect size, narrow evidentiary base.

Worth noting separately: ketamine's evidence base looks different in adults than in children for status epilepticus more broadly. A 2025 REBEL EM review of out-of-hospital ketamine-after-midazolam protocols (Zitek et al., Ann Emerg Med 2024) found the benefit skewed toward adult patients, with a smaller effect in the pediatric subgroup — a useful cross-check when reading a pediatric-specific trial like Ket-Mid in isolation.

05

Practical Barriers Beyond the Evidence

  • Ketamine is not currently stocked or protocolized as a first-line seizure agent in most EDs or EMS formularies — adoption requires a pharmacy and protocol change, not just a clinician's individual choice.
  • Dissociative and psychomimetic effects of ketamine, while generally well-tolerated in this dose range, are a different clinical picture for nursing and family communication than a benzodiazepine-only course.
  • The trial used a fixed 2 mg/kg IV ketamine dose; dose-response, alternative routes (IM/IN for pre-IV-access patients), and different formulations remain unstudied per the authors' own stated next steps.
06

Bottom Line

The numbers in the source post are accurate and the effect size is genuinely large: 3.7× more 5-minute seizure cessation and roughly a fivefold reduction in intubation with ketamine added to first-line midazolam. But this is one trial, at one center, in one country, and it is the first of its kind — ketamine plus midazolam is a promising signal for pediatric GCSE, not yet an evidence base that should move it ahead of established first- and second-line agents in your own department's protocol without confirmatory multicenter data.
