AI at the Bedside: Three Jobs, Three Evidence Bars

A CHEST 2026 recap argues that "it uses AI" says nothing until you know whether the tool drafts, predicts or recommends. Every number checks out, and one omitted finding makes the post's point stronger.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · October 2026. Original post: @defineshock, "CHEST2026 in review: AI at the bedside" (10 slides; slide 9 not captured in the screenshots reviewed). This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the original creator.

Verdict: all three cited studies say what the post says, and each limitation is stated honestly. The AI-scribe figure (13.4 fewer EHR minutes per 8 patient-hours) is the published difference-in-differences estimate. The GPT-4 trial numbers (76% vs 74%) are exact. The VentPilot figure (3.1 more ventilator-free days) is the more conservative of the paper's two validation cohorts. The one addition worth making: in the GPT-4 trial, the model alone outscored the physicians who had it.

01

Three jobs

The post's central frame, laid out with the error each job makes.

Generates
Drafts a summary or note

Error mode: omission, invented detail, wrong timeline.

Safeguard: the clinician who signs it reads it.

Evidence so far: observational time savings in ambulatory care.

Predicts
Estimates deterioration risk

Error mode: false alarms, silence when it matters, alerts nobody acts on.

Safeguard: a defined responder and action.

Evidence needed: improved outcomes after deployment, not just discrimination.

Recommends
Proposes a treatment change

Error mode: a confident wrong setting acted on.

Safeguard: expertise, time and authority to override.

Evidence needed: prospective, ideally randomized testing.

The higher the autonomy, the higher the evidence bar. A draft you review fails safely when the reviewer has time to read it. A recommendation that changes treatment fails in the patient.

03

The result the post left out

It supports the argument.

In the Goh trial the language model working alone scored 16 percentage points higher than the conventional-resources group (95% CI 2 to 30). Physicians who had the model available did no better than those who didn't. The tool was more capable than the way it was used. That is the post's "the clinician using the tool is part of what needs evaluating" point, with a number attached.

Two cautions keep this from becoming "the AI beats doctors." These were written vignettes (up to six per physician in 60 minutes), not live patients, and the trial ran in late 2023 with a model that has since been superseded. The finding is about how clinicians used the tool in that setup, not a ceiling on what the tool can do.

04

Reading VentPilot carefully

Why a concordance analysis can flatter a recommender.

Comparing outcomes by how closely clinicians' choices matched the model is vulnerable to confounding by indication. Patients who are improving are easy to manage by the book, so their settings will tend to match a model trained to favor ventilator-free days. Patients who are deteriorating get unusual settings because they are deteriorating. Inverse probability weighting adjusts for measured differences, not for the bedside judgment of how the patient is trending. The authors call for further clinical evaluation, which is the right conclusion: a prospective trial where some patients' clinicians receive the recommendations and others don't.
