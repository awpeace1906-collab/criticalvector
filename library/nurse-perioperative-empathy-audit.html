NURSE in the Preop Bay: The Table Lost Its E

An ASA table of NURSE empathic statements for patients facing cancer surgery. The examples are well chosen. The fifth row is mislabeled, and one Understanding example is really an Exploring question.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · November 2026. Original post: @asa_hq, NURSE table accompanying Drs. Justin Kim and Sandra Sacks, November 2026 ASA Monitor. This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the original creator.

Verdict: the E row repeats the S header (“Supporting”). E stands for Exploring. Everything else matches the NURSE framework of Back et al. (2005), and the perioperative examples are good enough to use as written.

NURSE, with the E restored

LetterSkillPerioperative example (from the post, lightly edited)
NNaming: say the emotion you see“I can hear how overwhelming this is: having cancer and now facing an operation feels like so much at once.”
UUnderstanding: show that it makes sense to you“It makes sense that you’re worried about what they’ll find. Anyone would be.”
RRespecting: credit the patient’s effort and coping“It takes real courage to ask the questions you’re asking. You’re advocating for yourself.”
SSupporting: commit to staying with them“Our whole perioperative team is with you before, during and after this surgery. You won’t go through it alone.”
EExploring: ask about the emotion directly“Is there anything you’ve been holding onto that you haven’t felt able to bring up yet?”

NURSE statements are responses to emotion, not information. The usual failure in a preoperative visit is answering a feeling (“I’m scared I won’t wake up”) with data (“the risk is under one in a hundred thousand”). One NURSE statement first, then the facts, tends to land better. You don’t need all five letters in every conversation.

Where NURSE comes from

Lineage

Smith and Hoppe described patient-centered interviewing skills for responding to emotion in 1991. Back, Arnold, Baile, Tulsky and Fryer-Edwards set out NURSE alongside “ask-tell-ask” and “tell me more” in their 2005 oncology communication paper, which is the citation most teaching programs, including VitalTalk, trace it to. Pollak and colleagues later audio-recorded oncology visits and found clinicians often missed or redirected patients’ expressions of emotion, which is the gap the mnemonic is meant to close.
