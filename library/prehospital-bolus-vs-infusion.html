Bolus-Only Prehospital Sedation: What the Undersedation Data Actually Shows

Sheridan B, Griggs J, Mondim P, Ter Avest E, Lyon R. Maintenance of prehospital anaesthesia using an intermittent bolus regime in blunt trauma patients with a high GCS and hemodynamic reserve: a retrospective cohort study.

Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med. 2026 Jun 25;34(1):136. doi:10.1186/s13049-026-01654-w · PMID: 42351216 · Single-center, Air Ambulance Charity Kent Surrey Sussex (KSS), UK · Original social summary: The Prehospitalist (@the.prehospitalist), critical care flight paramedic and prehospital educator. This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by The Prehospitalist.

Verdict on the source post: the caption's framing is accurate and understated, if anything — "a paper that should prompt consideration of a shift to infusions" undersells how stark the actual numbers are. This audit pulls the specific dosing figures the caption doesn't quote, and flags the one limitation worth knowing before treating this as definitive.
01

The Setup

In-hospital anesthesia maintenance is almost universally continuous infusion. Prehospital maintenance after RSI has stayed bolus-based by convention — and until this paper, nobody had actually measured what that convention delivers.

Bolus administration of sedatives/analgesics post-RSI carries an inherent structural risk: dosing can be delayed or entirely omitted amid competing clinical priorities, and most services provide no protocol for bolus intervals or doses — drug choice, dose, and timing are left to individual clinician discretion. This study set out to quantify, not just theorize about, the practice effect of that discretion in a population specifically selected to need reasonable sedation: hemodynamically stable, GCS ≥9 blunt trauma patients without severe TBI.

02

Design

DesignSingle-center retrospective observational cohort, UK HEMS (KSS)
PeriodJan 1, 2019 – Feb 28, 2023
Population101 blunt trauma patients, age ≥12, pre-induction GCS 9–15, pre-induction SBP ≥100 mmHg, pre-induction shock index ≤1.0 (i.e., hemodynamically reserved, not exsanguinating, not severely head-injured)
Primary objectiveDescribe practice effect of intermittent-bolus maintenance dosing (midazolam, ketamine, fentanyl, rocuronium), standardized to weight and time (dose/kg/h)
Induction100% received ketamine + rocuronium; 92% also received fentanyl

What that translates to for an average patient

For an 80 kg patient (this cohort's median weight), the study's own stated bottom line is that the average patient would receive only 2.2 mg of midazolam and 51.2 mcg of fentanyl in one hour — well below prior published guideline recommendations (e.g., the German Society of Anaesthesiology's 2016 out-of-hospital guidance: midazolam 3–5 mg and fentanyl 150 mcg repeated every 20 minutes). Eight patients (8%) received no maintenance sedative or analgesic at all, with anesthesia lasting 48–74 minutes — and in six of those eight cases, the chart showed no physiological reason for the omission.

04

The Finding That Rules Out the Charitable Explanation

Maybe clinicians were just titrating carefully to each patient. The data says otherwise.

If under-dosing reflected careful individualized titration, you'd expect maintenance dose to correlate with something — patient physiology, induction dose, or at minimum, transport duration (more time, more cumulative drug). None of that held. After Bonferroni correction across 20 comparisons, maintenance dosing showed no significant association with age, weight, shock index, GCS, heart rate, blood pressure, or induction dose. Total cumulative dose also showed no relationship with anesthesia duration for any of the four drugs (fentanyl r=-0.04, ketamine r=0.07, midazolam r=0.01, rocuronium r=0.15; all P>.19) — clinicians gave roughly the same total drug regardless of whether transport took 20 minutes or 100. The authors' own read: this pattern is harder to explain as deliberate precision dosing and easier to explain as a byproduct of cognitive load and competing priorities during a demanding, understaffed (two-clinician) prehospital resuscitation.

05

The Limitation Worth Knowing

  • No timing data. The dataset can't tell you when each bolus was actually given relative to a patient's physiology at that moment — only the cumulative total. It's possible some "under-dosed" patients were, in fact, adequately sedated by well-timed smaller boluses; the paper can't rule this out, and says so explicitly.
  • Single-center, single-country. This is one UK HEMS service's practice pattern over one four-year window — generalizing the specific dose figures to other services' bolus cultures should be done cautiously, even though the structural argument (bolus regimens lack standardization and drift toward under-dosing) is broadly consistent with the wider literature the authors cite (South African EMS, German OOH guidelines).
  • No outcome data. This study measures dosing, not patient-centered outcomes like awareness, PTSD, or hemodynamic events attributable to under-sedation — the harm is inferred from known physiology (awareness risk, hyperadrenergic stress response), not directly demonstrated in this cohort.
06

Bottom Line

The source post's framing holds up well against the primary data, and if anything is conservative: an intermittent-bolus-only regimen, without standardized dosing intervals, produced doses well below published guideline recommendations in a substantial minority of patients, with no evidence the shortfall reflected deliberate clinical titration. The paper doesn't prove infusions are safer or more effective — that's explicitly flagged as an area needing further research — but it makes a strong, quantified case that unstructured bolus dosing has a real, measurable ceiling problem, particularly under the cognitive load of a two-person prehospital trauma team.
