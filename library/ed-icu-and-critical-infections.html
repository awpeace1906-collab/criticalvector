ED/ICU & Critical Infections

The infections that generate the 2am page, across all three pathogen classes, plus the rare-but-lethal presentations where recognition — not the antibiotic itself — is the rate-limiting step. Companion file covers board-tested outpatient/floor presentations.

Regimens reflect standard empiric teaching (IDSA-pattern reasoning) for study and clinical reasoning — confirm against current guidelines and local antibiogram before treating an actual patient. Doses are intentionally omitted; this is a bug/drug/why reference.

01

ED / ICU — Bacterial

Source usually presumed before it's proven; empiric coverage broadened deliberately, de-escalation planned from the first order.

InfectionEmpiric AbxAbx RationaleSymptom ProfileClinical Result ProfileNotes
Sepsis / Septic Shocksource unknown Piperacillin-tazobactam + vancomycin± antifungal if high-risk (recent surgery, TPN, prolonged ICU) Broadest reasonable single-pass coverage (Gram-positive incl. MRSA, Gram-negative incl. Pseudomonas, anaerobes) while source is being localized — narrow within 48–72h on culture data. Fever/hypothermia, tachycardia, tachypnea, altered mentation, hypotension unresponsive to fluid. Lactate elevated, blood cultures x2 before abx if it doesn't delay therapy, qSOFA/SOFA scoring. Antibiotics within 1 hour of recognition; 30mL/kg crystalloid bolus; source control identified in parallel.
Severe CAPICU-level Ceftriaxone + macrolide or fluoroquinolone+ vancomycin/linezolid if MRSA risk (post-influenza, cavitary) Combination covers typical + atypical pathogens simultaneously; ICU-level disease no longer permits narrow empiric guessing before culture return. Respiratory failure or septic shock from pneumonia; one major or ≥3 minor IDSA/ATS severity criteria. CXR: multilobar infiltrates; hypoxemia, elevated procalcitonin, positive urinary pneumococcal/Legionella antigen if available. Consider MRSA/Pseudomonas coverage in structural lung disease, recent hospitalization, or post-influenza necrotizing pneumonia.
HAP / VAP Cefepime/pip-tazo/meropenem + vancomycin± second anti-pseudomonal agent if high resistance risk Hospital-acquired flora skews toward MDR Gram-negatives (Pseudomonas, resistant Enterobacterales) and MRSA — outpatient CAP regimens are inadequate. New/worsening infiltrate + fever, purulent secretions, leukocytosis, worsening oxygenation ≥48h after admission or on a ventilator. CXR/CT new infiltrate; respiratory culture (endotracheal aspirate or BAL) to guide de-escalation. Base regimen on unit-specific antibiogram; de-escalate aggressively once cultures return.
Aspiration Pneumonia Ampicillin-sulbactamAlt: clindamycin (PCN-allergic) Oropharyngeal flora includes anaerobes and mixed Gram-positive/negative organisms; anaerobic coverage is the key addition versus standard CAP. Witnessed aspiration event or risk factors (altered LOC, dysphagia, seizure) with subsequent fever, cough, foul-smelling sputum. CXR: infiltrate in dependent lung segments (right lower lobe most common, supine → posterior segments). Routine anaerobic coverage of chemical pneumonitis alone (no true infection) is often unnecessary and overused.
Urosepsiscomplicated UTI Ceftriaxone or pip-tazo (if resistant flora risk) IV therapy with reliable Gram-negative and renal-tract penetration; broaden for prior resistant organisms, indwelling catheter, or recent instrumentation. Fever, flank pain, hemodynamic instability layered on lower-tract symptoms; may present as delirium in elderly without classic symptoms. UA with pyuria/bacteriuria, blood cultures, lactate; renal ultrasound/CT if obstruction suspected. Obstructing stone with infected urine = urologic emergency — needs decompression, not just antibiotics.
Intra-abdominal Infectionperforation / abscess Piperacillin-tazobactamAlt: ceftriaxone + metronidazole Polymicrobial source (enteric Gram-negatives + anaerobes) requires combined or single broad-spectrum agent with both aerobic and anaerobic activity. Diffuse or localized abdominal pain, fever, rebound/guarding, distension. CT abdomen/pelvis: free air, abscess, fat stranding; leukocytosis, lactate elevation with ischemia. Source control (drainage/surgery) is the primary intervention — antibiotics alone will not cure an undrained abscess.
Necrotizing Fasciitis Vancomycin + pip-tazo + clindamycinSurgical Emergency Broad coverage for polymicrobial/mixed disease plus clindamycin specifically to suppress exotoxin production (protein synthesis inhibition, not just bacterial killing). Pain out of proportion to exam, rapidly spreading erythema, crepitus, bullae, systemic toxicity. CT/plain film may show soft tissue gas; markedly elevated CK, LRINEC score; definitive diagnosis is surgical exploration. Emergent surgical debridement is the definitive treatment — antibiotics alone are not curative.
Febrile Neutropenia Cefepime or pip-tazo monotherapy+ vancomycin if line infection/skin-soft tissue signs, instability Pseudomonas coverage is non-negotiable given rapid, fulminant course in the neutropenic host; monotherapy preferred unless specific risk factors argue for additions. Fever (single temp ≥38.3°C or ≥38°C x1h) with ANC <500 in a chemotherapy/hematologic malignancy patient. Blood cultures from all lines and peripheral, CBC with differential, CXR. Antibiotics within 1 hour is a hard quality metric here — do not wait for source to declare itself.
Adult Bacterial MeningitisICU / immunocompromised Vancomycin + ceftriaxone + ampicillin+ dexamethasone Ampicillin added over the standard adult regimen to cover Listeria in age >50 or immunocompromised hosts; steroid given with/before first antibiotic dose. Fever, headache, nuchal rigidity, altered mentation; may be blunted in elderly/immunocompromised. LP: neutrophilic pleocytosis, low glucose, high protein, elevated opening pressure; CT before LP if focal deficit, papilledema, or altered mentation. Do not delay antibiotics for imaging — draw blood cultures and start empiric therapy immediately if LP delayed.
Infective Endocarditisacute, native valve Vancomycin + ceftriaxoneProsthetic valve: add gentamicin + rifampin Empiric coverage for S. aureus (incl. MRSA), streptococci, and enterococci pending blood culture speciation; narrow once organism/sensitivities return. Fever, new murmur, embolic phenomena (Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, Roth spots, splinter hemorrhages). Blood cultures x3 from separate sites before antibiotics; echocardiogram (TTE → TEE) for vegetation; Duke criteria. IVDU history strongly suggests right-sided (tricuspid) S. aureus endocarditis with septic pulmonary emboli.
CLABSI Vancomycin + cefepime (or pip-tazo) Coverage for coagulase-negative staph/MRSA (most common) plus Gram-negatives, since line infections can seed systemically before organism is known. Fever, chills, hemodynamic instability in a patient with a central line, without another identified source. Paired blood cultures (peripheral + through-line) with differential time to positivity; culture line tip if removed. Line removal is generally required for S. aureus, Candida, or persistent bacteremia despite therapy.
Diabetic Foot Infectionmoderate–severe Piperacillin-tazobactam (± vancomycin if MRSA risk) Chronic wounds are typically polymicrobial (Gram-positive, Gram-negative, anaerobes); broad coverage warranted once infection extends beyond superficial skin. Wound with purulence plus ≥2 signs of inflammation; deep/large ulcers with systemic signs in severe disease. Probe-to-bone test, plain films/MRI for osteomyelitis, wound culture (deep tissue preferred over surface swab). Osteomyelitis changes duration of therapy significantly — image early if bone is a concern.
Toxic Shock Syndromestaphylococcal / streptococcal Vancomycin (or oxacillin) + clindamycin Clindamycin suppresses toxin (TSST-1/exotoxin) synthesis via protein synthesis inhibition independent of bacterial killing — critical adjunct beyond the beta-lactam/glycopeptide alone. High fever, diffuse macular erythroderma, hypotension, multi-organ involvement; desquamation 1–2wk later. Clinical diagnosis (CDC criteria); blood cultures often negative in staphylococcal TSS (toxin-mediated, low bacteremia). Identify and remove the source (retained tampon, wound packing, abscess) — source control is essential.
02

ED / ICU — Fungal

Fungal disease that behaves like an ICU emergency: neutropenic, transplant, and DKA hosts where the antifungal has to start before speciation.

InfectionEmpiric AntifungalRationaleSymptom ProfileClinical Result ProfileNotes
Candidemia / Invasive Candidiasisseptic ICU patient Echinocandin (micafungin/caspofungin) Fungicidal activity and coverage of azole-resistant species make it preferred first-line in the unstable ICU patient pending speciation. Persistent fever/shock unresponsive to broad-spectrum antibacterial therapy in a line-bearing, post-op, or TPN-dependent ICU patient. Blood cultures positive for Candida species; dilated fundoscopic exam to exclude endophthalmitis. Source control (line removal/exchange) is essential; step down to fluconazole once susceptible and stable.
Invasive Pulmonary Aspergillosis VoriconazoleAlt: isavuconazole (fewer interactions/QT effects) Voriconazole is first-line per IDSA guidance for reliable Aspergillus activity and CNS penetration. Fever, cough, hemoptysis, pleuritic chest pain in a neutropenic, transplant, or chronic-steroid ICU patient. CT: halo sign or air-crescent sign; serum galactomannan (+); BAL galactomannan more sensitive than serum. Mortality remains high even with treatment; early initiation on radiographic suspicion improves outcomes.
Mucormycosisrhino-orbital-cerebral Liposomal amphotericin B (high-dose)+ Emergent Surgical Debridement Amphotericin remains the most reliably active agent against Mucorales; azoles (fluconazole, voriconazole) have no activity — a critical distinction from Aspergillus. Facial pain/swelling, black necrotic eschar on palate/nasal turbinates, proptosis, cranial nerve deficits in poorly-controlled DKA or profound neutropenia. Biopsy: broad, non-septate, right-angle branching hyphae; CT/MRI shows sinus/orbital invasion. Surgical debridement is co-equal with antifungal therapy — delay in either drives mortality.
Pneumocystis Pneumonia (PJP) TMP-SMX (high-dose)+ corticosteroids if PaO2 <70 or elevated A-a gradient TMP-SMX remains first-line with the best-evidenced outcomes; steroids blunt the inflammatory response to organism die-off that otherwise worsens hypoxemia. Subacute dyspnea, dry cough, fever in AIDS (CD4 <200) or other profound immunosuppression. CXR/CT: bilateral ground-glass infiltrates; elevated LDH; BAL silver stain or PCR for Pneumocystis jirovecii. Steroids must start with or before the first antimicrobial dose in moderate-severe hypoxemia, not after.
03

ED / ICU — Viral

Viral disease severe enough to need ICU-level supportive care, with the narrow set of cases where an antiviral genuinely changes the outcome.

InfectionEmpiric Antiviral / MgmtRationaleSymptom ProfileClinical Result ProfileNotes
Severe Influenzaviral ARDS Oseltamivir (IV peramivir if enteral route unusable)+ lung-protective ventilation Antiviral therapy is indicated regardless of symptom-onset timing once hospitalized with severe disease; the IV formulation covers patients who can't absorb enterally. Rapid progression to hypoxemic respiratory failure, bilateral infiltrates, hemodynamic instability during flu season/outbreak. PCR respiratory panel (+) for influenza; CXR/CT bilateral ground-glass/consolidative infiltrates. Watch for post-influenza necrotizing S. aureus (incl. MRSA) pneumonia — add empiric MRSA coverage if suspected.
HSV / VZV EncephalitisICU IV Acyclovir (high-dose) — start empiricallyTreat Before Confirmation Untreated mortality is extremely high, and the drug's toxicity profile is low enough to justify empiric use on suspicion alone — same principle as the outpatient/floor entry. Fever, altered mentation, seizures, focal deficits; consider in any ICU patient with unexplained encephalopathy plus fever. MRI temporal lobe changes (HSV); CSF PCR for HSV/VZV; EEG may show PLEDs in HSV. Monitor renal function and hydration closely — acyclovir crystallizes in renal tubules at high doses.
Severe COVID-19pneumonia / ARDS Remdesivir + dexamethasone± immunomodulator (tocilizumab/baricitinib) per severity Remdesivir modestly reduces recovery time in hospitalized hypoxemic patients; dexamethasone reduces mortality specifically in patients requiring supplemental oxygen or ventilation by dampening the hyperinflammatory phase. Progressive dyspnea, hypoxemia, bilateral infiltrates roughly 7–10 days into the illness course (inflammatory phase). PCR (+) for SARS-CoV-2; CXR/CT bilateral peripheral ground-glass opacities; elevated CRP, ferritin, D-dimer. Timing matters — steroids given early in the viral-replication phase (before hypoxemia) may worsen outcomes.
CMV Reactivationcritically ill / transplant ICU Ganciclovir (or valganciclovir PO if tolerating enteral route) Viral kinase-dependent activation targets actively replicating CMV; treatment is reserved for end-organ disease or high viral load in the immunosuppressed/transplant ICU population, not incidental low-level viremia. Unexplained fever, worsening pulmonary status (pneumonitis), or GI bleeding/diarrhea (colitis) in a transplant or critically ill immunosuppressed patient. CMV PCR viral load trending; tissue biopsy with immunohistochemistry for definitive end-organ disease diagnosis. Ganciclovir causes significant myelosuppression — monitor counts closely, especially with other marrow-suppressive agents.
04

Rare & Time-Critical

Low pretest probability, high consequence for missing the window — including select bioterrorism-relevant pathogens where recognition, not the antibiotic itself, is the rate-limiting step.

InfectionEmpiric AbxAbx RationaleSymptom ProfileClinical Result ProfileNotes
Anthraxcutaneous Ciprofloxacin or doxycycline B. anthracis remains fluoroquinolone/tetracycline-sensitive; oral therapy sufficient for localized cutaneous disease without systemic toxicity. Painless papule → vesicle → black eschar with surrounding non-pitting edema; history of animal hide/wool/livestock exposure. Gram stain/culture of lesion: large Gram-positive boxcar-shaped rods; PCR confirmatory. Do not debride the eschar — it typically resolves with antibiotics alone; systemic spread is the real risk.
Anthraxinhalational / systemic Ciprofloxacin (or levofloxacin) + linezolid + a third agent — multi-drug IVAdd antitoxin (raxibacumab/obiltoxaximab) if available Systemic disease requires bactericidal + protein-synthesis-inhibiting combination to both kill the organism and halt ongoing toxin production; antitoxin neutralizes circulating lethal/edema toxin the antibiotics can't touch. Biphasic: flu-like prodrome → abrupt respiratory failure, mediastinal widening, shock. GI form: severe abdominal pain, bloody diarrhea after contaminated meat. CXR/CT: widened mediastinum (hemorrhagic mediastinitis), pleural effusions; blood cultures, PCR. Report to Public Health Suspected bioterrorism exposure — near-uniform fatality without early recognition and treatment.
Plaguebubonic Streptomycin (or gentamicin)Alt: doxycycline, fluoroquinolone Aminoglycosides remain first-line for Y. pestis given rapid bactericidal activity needed to outpace the organism's fulminant course. Sudden fever, chills, and an intensely painful, swollen lymph node (bubo) — history of flea bite/rodent exposure (Southwestern US, endemic regions). Gram stain of bubo aspirate: bipolar "safety-pin" staining Gram-negative coccobacillus; culture/PCR/serology confirms. Untreated bubonic plague can progress to secondary septicemic or pneumonic plague — treat empirically on suspicion.
Plaguepneumonic Streptomycin/gentamicin or fluoroquinolone IV Same rationale as bubonic form, but pneumonic disease is uniformly and rapidly fatal without treatment and is the only form transmissible person-to-person via respiratory droplets. Fulminant pneumonia: fever, cough, hemoptysis, respiratory failure within 1–2 days of exposure. Sputum Gram stain/culture: bipolar-staining Gram-negative rod; CXR shows rapidly progressive consolidation. Droplet Isolation Only form transmissible human-to-human — implement droplet precautions immediately, notify public health.
Botulism Botulinum antitoxin — antibiotics are not primary treatment Disease is toxin-mediated, not infection-mediated; antitoxin neutralizes circulating unbound toxin. Aminoglycosides are specifically avoided — they potentiate neuromuscular blockade. Descending, symmetric flaccid paralysis: diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia progressing to respiratory failure; afebrile, no sensory deficit. Clinical diagnosis; toxin assay (mouse bioassay or PCR) of serum/stool/food source; EMG shows characteristic incremental response. Avoid Aminoglycosides Infant botulism (honey exposure) uses human botulism immune globulin, not equine antitoxin.
Tularemia Streptomycin (or gentamicin)Alt: doxycycline, ciprofloxacin for milder disease Francisella tularensis is an intracellular organism requiring an agent with good intracellular penetration and bactericidal activity for severe/systemic disease. Ulceroglandular form: painful skin ulcer + tender regional lymphadenopathy after tick/rabbit/deer-fly exposure; can also present as pneumonic or typhoidal illness. Serology (most common confirmatory method); culture is hazardous to lab personnel — notify lab of suspicion. Classic exposure: skinning/handling rabbits or other wild game; consider in hunters/trappers.
MeningococcemiaWaterhouse-Friderichsen Ceftriaxone (empiric, immediately)+ hydrocortisone if adrenal hemorrhage/shock N. meningitidis progresses from petechiae to purpura fulminans within hours; immediate ceftriaxone reduces mortality dramatically, and stress-dose steroids address adrenal hemorrhage-driven shock. Fever, rapidly spreading petechial/purpuric rash, hypotension, DIC; can progress to full circulatory collapse within hours. Blood cultures (do not delay treatment to obtain); adrenal hemorrhage on CT in Waterhouse-Friderichsen; DIC labs (low platelets, elevated INR/PTT, low fibrinogen). Treat Before Confirmation Give antibiotics on clinical suspicion — do not wait for LP or culture results.
Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever Doxycycline — start empirically, at any age Doxycycline is the only agent proven to reduce mortality; delay while awaiting serology (often negative early) significantly worsens outcomes — this overrides usual pediatric tetracycline avoidance. Fever, headache, myalgias, followed by a maculopapular rash starting on wrists/ankles and spreading centripetally to palms/soles. Serology often negative in the first week (retrospective diagnosis); low platelets, low sodium, elevated LFTs are supportive clues. Rash may be absent or atypical early — treat empirically based on exposure history and clinical suspicion alone.
LeptospirosisWeil's disease Doxycycline (mild) or penicillin G / ceftriaxone (severe) Both drug classes remain reliably active against Leptospira; severity dictates the switch from oral tetracycline to IV beta-lactam coverage. Biphasic illness: fever, myalgias, conjunctival suffusion → severe form (Weil's disease) with jaundice, renal failure, hemorrhage after freshwater/animal urine exposure. Serology (MAT) confirmatory but slow; elevated bilirubin with only mildly elevated transaminases (distinguishes from viral hepatitis), elevated CK, renal dysfunction. Consider in returning travelers with fever after freshwater exposure (rafting, flooding) in endemic regions.
Rabiespost-exposure prophylaxis Rabies immune globulin + vaccine series — not an antibiotic; prevention only Immune globulin provides immediate passive antibody at the wound site while active vaccination builds durable immunity before the long incubation period ends — once symptoms begin, disease is essentially uniformly fatal. Once symptomatic: agitation, hydrophobia, hypersalivation, autonomic instability progressing to paralysis and death. Diagnosis (if symptomatic) via saliva PCR, skin biopsy (nuchal), or serology — pre-symptomatic PEP decision is clinical/exposure-based, not lab-based. Prevention Is the Treatment PEP decision hinges on animal type, provocation, and local rabies prevalence — infiltrate immune globulin directly into and around the wound.
Viral Hemorrhagic FeverEbola / Marburg Supportive care ± investigational monoclonal antibodies (Ebola) — no antibiotic role These are viral, not bacterial, pathogens; management is aggressive supportive care (fluid/electrolyte repletion) plus, for Ebola specifically, monoclonal antibody therapy where available. Fever, severe malaise, GI symptoms progressing to hemorrhagic manifestations (bleeding gums, petechiae, GI bleeding) in a patient with relevant travel/exposure history. PCR confirmatory (send to reference lab under strict biosafety protocol); marked thrombocytopenia, elevated transaminases, coagulopathy. Isolate + Notify Public Health Immediately Strict isolation (PPE, negative pressure) before any further workup — this is a diagnosis made by travel history first.
