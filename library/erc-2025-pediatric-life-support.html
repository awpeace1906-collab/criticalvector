2025 Pediatric Resuscitation: What Actually Changed

European Resuscitation Council Guidelines 2025 Paediatric Life Support — the changes that matter at the bedside, not just the citation.

Djakow J, Turner NM, Skellett S, et al; ERC Paediatric Life Support Writing Group. Resuscitation. 2025;215(Suppl 1):110767. doi:10.1016/j.resuscitation.2025.110767 · Original social summary: hagahmedmd, "5 high-yield updates emergency clinicians need to know"

Note on the source post: only the title card was available to audit against — the specific "5 updates" hagahmedmd picked weren't visible. Rather than guess at their five, this is a full breakdown of the guideline's actual highest-yield changes from the previous 2021 edition, organized by category, each checked directly against the primary document and independent summaries (Resuscitation Council UK, Don't Forget the Bubbles).
01

The Headline: BLS Didn't Change

If you do nothing else with this update, know this: basic life support technique is unchanged. The changes are almost entirely in advanced care.

Paediatric Basic Life Support carries no technique changes from 2021 — compression rate, ratio, and rescue breathing recommendations are unchanged. The one clarification: use newborn life support (NLS) during neonatal/maternity unit stays and, out-of-hospital, up to 24 hours of life; after that, use paediatric BLS (PBLS).

02

Airway: Hard Numbers Where There Used to Be Judgment Calls

ChangeDetail
Apnoeic oxygenationConsider apnoeic oxygenation or high-flow nasal oxygen to avoid hypoxia during intubation
Time limit per attemptLimit each intubation attempt to 30–60 seconds; re-ventilate immediately if desaturation or bradycardia occurs
Attempt ceilingMaximum of 4 intubation attempts, then pivot to oxygenation (face mask or supraglottic airway) and call for anaesthetics/PICU support

This is a genuine shift from "use clinical judgment" to explicit numeric limits — useful for exactly the moment judgment is hardest to exercise well.

03

Circulation: The Fluid Bolus Cap Just Changed for Teens

The number worth memorizing

Fluid boluses at 10 mL/kg can now be given up to 500 mL in adolescents (14+), in line with adult practice — increased from the previous 250 mL cap. Ideally a balanced crystalloid (Plasma-Lyte 148 or Hartmann's/lactated Ringer's).

  • IV access time limit: no more than 5 minutes attempting IV access before inserting an IO; provide effective analgesia for IO placement unless the child is comatose.
  • POCUS for IV cannulation: competent providers should use point-of-care ultrasound to guide difficult IV placement — a new explicit recommendation, not just a nice-to-have.
  • Vasoactive drug first-line agents, now specified: noradrenaline (norepinephrine) first-line vasopressor; adrenaline (epinephrine) first-line inotrope; milrinone first-line inodilator. The choice between vasopressor and inotrope is left to clinical judgment.
  • TBI-specific target: in traumatic brain injury, titrate fluids and vasoactives to a MAP at or above the 50th centile for age (to protect cerebral perfusion pressure); 0.9% saline may be preferred over balanced crystalloids here specifically, to drive mild hypernatremia for neuroprotection — a deliberate exception to the "balanced crystalloid by default" rule elsewhere in the guideline.
04

Drugs: The Obesity-Dosing Fix

Use ideal body weight, not actual body weight, in high-BMI patients

Most resuscitation drugs are hydrophilic and don't distribute into fatty tissue — dosing off actual body weight in a high-BMI child risks overdosing, including exceeding adult maximum doses. This is a small line in the guideline with real overdose-prevention consequences.

05

Shockable Arrest: New Energy Ceiling and Pad Placement Logic

Parameter2025 recommendation
Initial shock energy4 J/kg, not exceeding adult max (120–200 J, manufacturer dependent)
Refractory VT/VF (>5 shocks)Escalate stepwise to a maximum of 8 J/kg
Re-arrest after ROSC in VT/VFUse the energy that previously achieved ROSC — don't reset to initial dose
Pad placement, infants/children <25 kgAntero-posterior preferred (easier to achieve without pad-to-pad contact)
Pad placement, larger childrenAntero-lateral (less CPR interruption); avoid breast tissue in adolescents
Monitored shockable arrestUp to 3 stacked shocks at 4 J/kg with rhythm/pulse checks between; if refractory, give adrenaline + amiodarone after 5 shocks, further amiodarone after 7

Note the drug-timing sequence differs from adult ALS, where amiodarone follows immediately after stacked shocks — don't cross-apply the adult sequence to a pediatric arrest.

New invasive monitoring target: if an arterial line is in place during CPR, target diastolic pressure >25 mmHg in infants or >30 mmHg in children/adolescents — coronary filling happens in diastole, so this is a direct proxy for whether compressions are actually generating adequate coronary perfusion.

06

Special Circumstances: Adult-Level Specificity, Now Explicit in Peds

Little of this is conceptually new — the shift is from vague/implicit to explicitly stated, matching existing adult guidance.

  • Septic shock: blood cultures/PCR if feasible, broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour; if refractory to fluids and first-line vasoactives (or in adrenal insufficiency), consider hydrocortisone 1–2 mg/kg IV (max 100 mg) — consistent with Surviving Sepsis Campaign, now explicit in the pediatric-specific document.
  • Massive hemorrhage: tranexamic acid ASAP, within 3 hours of injury — 15–20 mg/kg IV bolus (max 1 g), then infusion 2 mg/kg/hr for at least 8 hours (max 1 g in 8h). Limit crystalloid to 20 mL/kg max before moving to blood products. In TBI or refractory shock, target MAP >50th centile for age.
  • Traumatic cardiac arrest: explicit HOTT principles (Hypovolemia, Oxygenation, Tension, Tamponade) take priority over AED/pad placement, run concurrently with compressions, unless a shockable rhythm is especially likely (electrocution, commotio cordis).
  • Hyperkalemia: confirmed severe (>6.5 mmol/L, or >7.0 in neonates <4 days) → insulin 0.1 unit/kg (max 10 units) in 5 mL/kg 10% glucose (max 250 mL) over 30 min, glucose/K+ monitoring q15min × 4h. Do not give calcium during cardiac arrest for hyperkalemia — explicitly stated not to improve survival and possibly worse in some studies; focus on potassium-lowering agents instead.
  • Obstructed cardiac shunt: genuinely new content — specific guidance for ductus stents, post-Norwood/Glenn physiology; maximize oxygenation, fluids for preload, heparin anticoagulation (50–100 unit/kg bolus), early specialist involvement.
07

Bottom Line

The single most useful framing for this update: pediatric BLS is unchanged, so don't relearn anything there. Almost everything new sits in advanced care and special circumstances — numeric caps replacing judgment calls (intubation attempts/timing, defibrillation energy escalation, the adolescent 500 mL fluid bolus cap), first-line vasoactive agents now explicitly named, and adult-level specificity finally written into the pediatric document for septic shock, massive hemorrhage, and traumatic arrest. The genuinely new material, not just a restatement of adult practice, is the obstructed cardiac shunt section and the diastolic-pressure CPR-quality target.
