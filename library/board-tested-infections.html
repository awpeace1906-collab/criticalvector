The Critical Vector · Empiric Therapy Guide, Part I of II

Board-Tested Infections

Bacterial, fungal, and viral pathogens COMLEX Level 2-CE reliably tests: the classic symptom-triad vignette, the signature lab or imaging finding, and the one empiric regimen the question wants you to name. Companion file covers ED/ICU presentations and rare, time-critical infections.

Regimens reflect standard board-pattern empiric teaching for study and clinical reasoning — confirm against current guidelines and local antibiogram before treating an actual patient. Doses are intentionally omitted; this is a bug/drug/why reference, not a dosing card.

Bacterial

InfectionAbxAbx RationaleSymptom ProfileClinical Result ProfileNotes
Group A Strep Pharyngitis Penicillin V or amoxicillinAlt: cephalexin, azithromycin if PCN-allergic S. pyogenes remains universally PCN-sensitive; narrow-spectrum first-line prevents rheumatic fever without broad collateral coverage. Sudden sore throat, fever, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical nodes, absence of cough/coryza. Centor criteria 3–4 → rapid strep/culture; (+) rapid antigen or culture confirms. Treat to prevent rheumatic fever, not primarily to shorten symptoms. Avoid amoxicillin if mono is on the differential (rash).
CAP — Typicaloutpatient, healthy Amoxicillin (high-dose)Alt: doxycycline S. pneumoniae is the dominant typical pathogen; narrow coverage preferred in low-risk outpatients per stewardship principles. Acute fever, productive cough, pleuritic chest pain, rigors. CXR: lobar consolidation. Elevated WBC with left shift. CURB-65 to triage site of care. Macrolide monotherapy now limited by rising pneumococcal resistance.
CAP — AtypicalMycoplasma / Chlamydophila / Legionella Azithromycin or doxycycline Atypicals lack a cell wall (Mycoplasma) or are intracellular; beta-lactams are ineffective, macrolides/tetracyclines cover intracellular replication. "Walking pneumonia": gradual onset, dry cough, headache, malaise out of proportion to exam. CXR: patchy interstitial infiltrate, worse than exam suggests. Cold agglutinins (+) in Mycoplasma. Legionella: hyponatremia + GI symptoms + confusion is the classic triad; urine antigen test.
Uncomplicated Cystitis Nitrofurantoin or TMP-SMXAlt: fosfomycin single-dose Both concentrate in urine with minimal systemic exposure and collateral resistance pressure; first-line per stewardship guidance for uncomplicated lower-tract disease. Dysuria, urgency, frequency, suprapubic discomfort; afebrile, no flank pain. UA: leukocyte esterase (+), nitrites (+, E. coli), pyuria. Avoid nitrofurantoin if suspected early pyelo (poor tissue penetration) or CrCl <30.
Pyelonephritisoutpatient-appropriate Fluoroquinolone (cipro/levofloxacin)Alt: ceftriaxone (single dose) → oral step-down Requires an agent with reliable renal parenchymal and blood-level penetration, unlike nitrofurantoin. Fever, chills, flank pain, CVA tenderness, +/- nausea/vomiting; cystitis symptoms often present. UA: pyuria, WBC casts (specific for pyelo). Urine culture to confirm/de-escalate. Admit and use IV therapy if unable to tolerate PO, pregnant, or systemically ill.
Acute Otitis Media Amoxicillin (high-dose)Alt: amox-clavulanate if recent abx/conjunctivitis S. pneumoniae, nontypeable H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis; high-dose overcomes intermediate pneumococcal resistance. Ear pain, fever, irritability (peds); recent URI. Otoscopy: bulging, erythematous, non-mobile TM; +/- effusion. Watchful waiting appropriate in select low-risk children per age/severity criteria.
Bacterial Sinusitis Amoxicillin-clavulanate Added beta-lactamase coverage over amoxicillin alone addresses H. influenzae/M. catarrhalis co-infection typical of true bacterial sinusitis. Symptoms >10 days without improvement, "double-worsening," or severe onset with fever >39°C + purulent discharge. Clinical diagnosis; imaging not routine. CT if complication suspected (orbital, intracranial). Most sinusitis is viral — reserved for the bacterial criteria above, not routine URI congestion.
Bacterial Meningitisneonate Ampicillin + cefotaximeor ampicillin + gentamicin Ampicillin specifically covers Listeria and Group B Strep, unique to the neonatal period; cefotaxime preferred over ceftriaxone (bilirubin displacement risk). Cefotaxime's US availability has been limited; many centers substitute ceftazidime or cefepime. Fever or hypothermia, poor feeding, lethargy, bulging fontanelle, irritability. LP: high WBC (neutrophil-predominant), low glucose, high protein. GBS, E. coli, Listeria monocytogenes are the classic neonatal triad.
Bacterial Meningitischild / adult Vancomycin + ceftriaxone+ dexamethasone before/with first dose Vancomycin covers resistant S. pneumoniae; ceftriaxone covers N. meningitidis and susceptible pneumococcus; steroid blunts inflammatory hearing-loss/mortality in pneumococcal disease. Fever, nuchal rigidity, headache, photophobia, altered mental status; petechial rash if meningococcal. LP: neutrophilic pleocytosis, glucose <40% of serum, elevated protein, elevated opening pressure. Add ampicillin if age >50 or immunocompromised (Listeria coverage). Do not delay abx for imaging/LP.
Cellulitis / Erysipelas Cephalexin or dicloxacillin+ TMP-SMX/doxycycline if MRSA risk or purulence Non-purulent cellulitis is overwhelmingly streptococcal/MSSA; anti-staphylococcal beta-lactam covers both without unneeded MRSA spectrum unless risk factors present. Erythema, warmth, tenderness, poorly demarcated (cellulitis) vs. sharply demarcated raised border (erysipelas); +/- fever. Clinical diagnosis; cultures low-yield unless purulent, abscess, or systemic toxicity. Erysipelas is classically GAS and more superficial/lymphatic; mark borders to track response.
Lyme Diseaseearly localized DoxycyclineAlt: amoxicillin, cefuroxime (pregnancy/peds) Borrelia burgdorferi is exquisitely doxycycline-sensitive; also covers coincident anaplasmosis from the same tick exposure. Erythema migrans ("bullseye") rash, flu-like symptoms, recent endemic-area tick exposure. Clinical diagnosis in endemic areas with classic rash; serology (ELISA → Western blot) if uncertain or disseminated. Late disease (carditis, arthritis, neuroborreliosis) escalates to IV ceftriaxone.
Pertussis Azithromycin Macrolide reduces transmissibility and shortens the catarrhal phase if given early; largely supportive once paroxysmal phase established. Catarrhal (URI-like) → paroxysmal (severe coughing fits, inspiratory "whoop," post-tussive emesis) → convalescent. NP PCR/culture for Bordetella pertussis; marked lymphocytosis on CBC. Treatment mainly limits spread once paroxysmal — abx doesn't shorten established cough.
C. difficile Colitis Oral vancomycin or fidaxomicinMetronidazole reserved for mild disease/low-resource settings Oral vancomycin acts locally within the gut lumen where C. diff resides — IV vancomycin does not reach the colon in adequate concentration. Watery diarrhea, abdominal cramping, recent/current antibiotic exposure, fever. Stool PCR/toxin EIA (+) for C. diff toxin; leukocytosis often marked. Fulminant disease (ileus, megacolon, shock) → oral + rectal vancomycin + IV metronidazole; surgery eval.
H. pylori Quadruple: PPI + bismuth + metronidazole + tetracyclineTriple (PPI+amox+clarithro) if low local resistance Rising clarithromycin resistance has shifted first-line to bismuth quadruple therapy in most regions. Epigastric pain, peptic ulcer pattern (duodenal: relieved by food; gastric: worsened by food). Urea breath test or stool antigen (hold PPI ~2wk prior); biopsy with rapid urease test if endoscopy performed. Confirm eradication with test-of-cure 4+ weeks after completing therapy.
Gonorrhea / Chlamydiacervicitis / urethritis Ceftriaxone (single IM) + doxycycline Empiric dual coverage given frequent co-infection; ceftriaxone dose increased given rising gonococcal resistance, doxycycline replaced azithromycin as chlamydia first-line. Purulent discharge, dysuria; often asymptomatic, especially chlamydia in women. NAAT of urine/cervical/urethral swab for both organisms. Treat partners; test for other STIs including HIV/syphilis. Disseminated gonococcemia → migratory polyarthralgia + dermatitis.
Syphilisprimary / secondary / early latent Benzathine penicillin G (single IM)Late latent/tertiary: weekly x3 Treponema pallidum has no documented penicillin resistance; long-acting benzathine form maintains treponemicidal levels for weeks. Primary: painless chancre. Secondary: diffuse rash incl. palms/soles, condyloma lata. Tertiary: gummas, aortitis, neurosyphilis. Nontreponemal (RPR/VDRL) screen → treponemal (FTA-ABS) confirm; titers trend response. PCN-allergic pregnant patients require desensitization — no substitute is acceptable in pregnancy.

Fungal

InfectionAntifungalRationaleSymptom ProfileClinical Result ProfileNotes
Oropharyngeal / Esophageal Candidiasis Nystatin swish-and-swallow (mild oral)Fluconazole (systemic) for esophageal disease Esophageal involvement implies deeper mucosal invasion requiring systemic azole rather than topical polyene. White plaques scraping off oral mucosa; odynophagia if esophageal extension. Clinical diagnosis; endoscopy with brushings if refractory to empiric fluconazole. Esophageal candidiasis in a young patient is AIDS-defining — check HIV status.
Vulvovaginal Candidiasis Fluconazole (single oral dose)Alt: topical azole (clotrimazole/miconazole) Fluconazole's oral convenience and reliable efficacy against C. albicans make it first-line for uncomplicated disease. Thick "cottage cheese" discharge, vulvar pruritus/erythema, dyspareunia. Wet mount: pseudohyphae/budding yeast; vaginal pH normal (<4.5), distinguishing from BV/trichomonas. Recurrent disease (≥3/yr, per CDC 2021) warrants evaluation for uncontrolled diabetes.
Invasive Candidiasis / Candidemia Echinocandin (micafungin/caspofungin) Echinocandins are fungicidal, active against azole-resistant Candida (incl. glabrata/auris), and preferred empirically in critically ill/ICU patients. Persistent fever unresponsive to broad-spectrum antibiotics in a line-bearing, ICU, or TPN patient. Blood cultures (+) for Candida species; dilated eye exam to rule out endophthalmitis. Remove/exchange central lines when feasible — source control is essential, not optional.
Cryptococcal Meningitis Liposomal amphotericin B + flucytosine (induction)→ fluconazole consolidation/maintenance Combination fungicidal induction clears organism burden faster and reduces mortality versus fluconazole monotherapy; maintenance suppresses relapse in ongoing immunosuppression. Subacute headache, fever, altered mentation in an immunocompromised (classically AIDS, CD4 <100) host. LP: markedly elevated opening pressure; India ink or cryptococcal antigen (+) in CSF/serum. Serial therapeutic LPs to manage elevated opening pressure are often as important as the antifungal itself.
Histoplasmosisdisseminated / severe pulmonary Liposomal amphotericin B → itraconazole step-down Amphotericin provides rapid fungicidal control in severe/disseminated disease; itraconazole maintains suppression with better tolerability for prolonged courses. Fever, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly (disseminated); cough/dyspnea after exposure to bird/bat droppings (caves, Ohio/Mississippi River valleys). Urine/serum Histoplasma antigen most sensitive; CXR may show diffuse nodular infiltrates or calcifications. Mild pulmonary disease in immunocompetent hosts is often self-limited and observed.
Coccidioidomycosissevere / disseminated Fluconazole (or itraconazole)Amphotericin B for severe/disseminated or pregnancy Azole therapy is generally sufficient given the organism's susceptibility profile; amphotericin reserved for severe/CNS disease or pregnancy (azole teratogenicity). "Valley fever": fever, cough, arthralgias, erythema nodosum after Southwestern US (San Joaquin Valley) dust exposure. Serology (complement fixation/EIA) for Coccidioides; CXR may show consolidation, cavitary lesions. Most cases in immunocompetent hosts are self-limited and don't require antifungals.

Viral

InfectionAntiviral / MgmtRationaleSymptom ProfileClinical Result ProfileNotes
Influenza OseltamivirWithin 48h of onset, or any point if hospitalized/high-risk Neuraminidase inhibition blocks viral release from infected cells, shortening illness most effectively when started early in the replication curve. Abrupt fever, myalgias, headache, dry cough, prostration — more systemic than typical URI. Rapid antigen or PCR nasal swab; leukopenia common. Benefit is greatest within 48h of onset but still indicated later in hospitalized/high-risk patients.
HSVgenital / oral Valacyclovir or acyclovir Nucleoside analog terminates viral DNA chain elongation via HSV thymidine kinase activation — no cure, but shortens outbreaks and reduces shedding. Painful grouped vesicles on erythematous base; primary episode often with fever/lymphadenopathy. Clinical diagnosis; PCR or viral culture from unroofed vesicle confirms. Suppressive daily therapy reduces transmission and recurrence frequency in frequent recurrers.
HSV Encephalitis IV Acyclovir — start empiricallyTreat Before Confirmation Untreated HSV encephalitis carries ~70% mortality; empiric treatment on suspicion alone is standard given the cost of delay versus the low toxicity of a short acyclovir course. Fever, altered mental status, focal neuro deficits, seizures — classically temporal lobe. MRI: temporal lobe edema/hemorrhage. CSF PCR for HSV (may be falsely negative very early). Start acyclovir before LP/imaging results return if HSV encephalitis is on the differential.
VZVvaricella / zoster Valacyclovir (within 72h of rash) Same nucleoside-analog mechanism as HSV; efficacy is time-dependent, so treatment is only meaningfully effective started early in the vesicular phase. Varicella: diffuse vesicles "in different stages." Zoster: dermatomal, painful vesicular rash, often with prodromal neuralgia. Clinical diagnosis; Tzanck smear shows multinucleated giant cells (not specific to VZV vs HSV). Zoster ophthalmicus (nasociliary involvement, Hutchinson's sign) requires urgent ophtho referral.
EBV Mononucleosis Supportive care — no specific antiviral indicated EBV is self-limited in immunocompetent hosts; antivirals do not alter course. Management is symptomatic + activity restriction. Fever, exudative pharyngitis, posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, fatigue, splenomegaly. Heterophile (Monospot) antibody (+); atypical lymphocytosis on smear; transaminitis common. Avoid amoxicillin/ampicillin — near-universal morbilliform rash in EBV infection. Avoid contact sports (splenic rupture) x3–4wk.
CMVimmunocompromised: retinitis, colitis Ganciclovir (or valganciclovir PO)Foscarnet for ganciclovir-resistant disease Ganciclovir requires viral kinase for activation and is reserved for end-organ disease in immunocompromised hosts, where untreated CMV causes irreversible organ damage (blindness, colitis). Retinitis: floaters, vision loss (AIDS, CD4 <50). Colitis: diarrhea, abdominal pain in transplant/AIDS patients. CMV PCR viral load; retinitis diagnosed by dilated fundoscopic exam (granular hemorrhagic pattern). Immunocompetent CMV mono-like illness is self-limited and does not require treatment.
HIVacute retroviral syndrome Refer for prompt ART initiation (regimen per specialist) Early ART initiation reduces viral reservoir size, reduces transmission risk, and improves long-term outcomes — treat as soon as diagnosis is confirmed, regardless of CD4 count. Mono-like: fever, pharyngitis, diffuse lymphadenopathy, maculopapular rash, myalgias 2–4wk post-exposure. 4th-gen Ag/Ab combo test (detects p24 antigen); confirm with HIV RNA viral load if acute infection suspected (antibody may be falsely negative in the window period). Consider PEP for any recent high-risk exposure presenting within 72h.
Hepatitis C Direct-acting antivirals (sofosbuvir-based combinations) DAAs target specific HCV viral enzymes (NS3/4A protease, NS5A, NS5B polymerase), achieving >95% cure rates versus older interferon-based regimens. Often asymptomatic acutely; chronic infection may present with fatigue, or incidentally with elevated LFTs. Anti-HCV antibody screen → HCV RNA (viral load) confirms active infection; pangenotypic regimens mean genotyping is often unnecessary before treatment. Universal one-time screening now recommended for all adults regardless of risk factors.
