1. The Big Picture

Mechanical ventilation exists to buy time — for oxygenation, for ventilation, for work-of-breathing relief — while the underlying process is treated. Every setting on the vent is a tradeoff between supporting gas exchange and causing iatrogenic lung injury (volutrauma, barotrauma, atelectrauma) or diaphragm injury (VIDD) in the process.

The core loop of vent management: pick a mode → set initial parameters → read the waveform and loops continuously → adjust for the lung mechanics and gas exchange you're actually seeing, not the ones you assumed → reassess daily whether the patient still needs full support at all.

Owens frames this as "The Eleven Commandments of Mechanical Ventilation" in his book — a philosophy more than a checklist, built around the idea that most critical care success comes from disciplined execution of the basics, not exotic maneuvers.

2. Modes

Every ventilator manufacturer uses different names for the same underlying physiology — this is the single most common source of cross-institution confusion. Every mode, regardless of brand name, answers the same three questions.

The three questions every mode answers:

1. What does the vent guarantee? Volume? Pressure? Both, via a dual-target hybrid? (It's never truly both simultaneously — one is always the controlled variable, the other the dependent one.)

2. Who's in control? Does the ventilator control everything (mandatory), does the patient trigger every breath (spontaneous/assisted), or is it shared?

3. How does the breath end? Time-cycled, flow-cycled, or volume-cycled?

Every proprietary brand name is a remix of the answers to these three questions — useful to run through when a mode name on an unfamiliar ventilator doesn't map cleanly onto anything in the table below.

Volume-controlled (VC): set tidal volume is guaranteed; airway pressure varies with compliance and resistance. Consistent VT delivery; risk of barotrauma if compliance falls.

Pressure-controlled (PC): set inspiratory pressure is guaranteed; tidal volume varies with lung mechanics. Limits peak pressure; risk of hypoventilation if compliance worsens.

Adaptive / dual-target (PRVC and equivalents): a pressure-controlled breath that auto-adjusts pressure breath-to-breath to hit a target VT — called PRVC, AC-VC+, Volume Control, APVcmv, or POVAc depending on manufacturer.

SIMV: set number of mandatory breaths with spontaneous breathing permitted between — largely supplanted by assist-control plus pressure support in modern practice. Owens notes the theoretical appeal (preserved spontaneous effort) is undercut in practice by imposed work of breathing on the spontaneous breaths themselves.

Pressure support ventilation (PSV): every breath is patient-triggered and pressure-limited, with no backup rate — comfortable and physiologic, but unsafe without a reliable respiratory drive, since apnea produces no rescue breath until the alarm sounds.

Biphasic / APRV: two alternating pressure levels (Phigh/Plow) with spontaneous breathing permitted throughout; evidence base largely built on preserving spontaneous ventilation and improving V/Q matching in ARDS.

2.1 — APRV: Initial Setup

APRV setup varies more across centers and ventilator platforms than any other mode — here's a reasonable starting point for the four parameters and how to titrate them.

The four parameters: Phigh (high pressure) — set to the plateau pressure measured on the prior conventional mode, not exceeding 30 cmH2O. Plow (low/release pressure) — commonly set to 0 cmH2O to maximize expiratory flow (this does not create zero PEEP; the release time is too brief for full lung emptying, so meaningful intrinsic PEEP persists even at a Plow of 0). Thigh (time at high pressure) — typically 4–6 seconds in patients with reasonably preserved lungs; longer Thigh generally improves oxygenation. Tlow (release time) — the most consequential and most poorly standardized parameter, commonly set to terminate the release when expiratory flow has fallen to 50–75% of the peak expiratory flow rate, targeting roughly 10–14 releases per minute.

Titrating for gas exchange: for hypoxemia, increase Phigh and/or Thigh (and FiO2) before reaching for other changes. For hypercapnia, shorten Thigh to increase release frequency, or lighten sedation to allow more spontaneous breathing between releases — spontaneous effort is central to how APRV is intended to work, not incidental to it.

APRV protocols vary enough across centers and ventilator platforms that the published literature itself is difficult to pool — increasing Plow above 0, for instance, has been shown to unpredictably raise both intrinsic PEEP and delivered tidal volume even when the pressure difference (Phigh − Plow) is held constant. Treat any single center's APRV protocol as one implementation among several, not a universal standard.

2026 update: a meta-analysis of 9 studies (1,921 patients) found APRV/BiVent improved oxygenation and increased ventilator-free days compared to conventional ventilation, but showed no mortality benefit. Worth noting for CO2 clearance specifically: the brief Tlow release is the only point in the cycle where bulk CO2 clearance happens, and one study found APRV actually improved CO2 clearance at a lower minute ventilation — but in patients with very stiff lungs or high CO2 production, short release times can make CO2 management genuinely difficult. The TCAV protocol (time-controlled adaptive ventilation) refines APRV by personalizing Tlow to the patient's own expiratory flow curve rather than a fixed target — promising physiologically, but unproven in large RCTs.

2.2 — VC vs. PC and the SIMV Question: What the Evidence Actually Shows

Volume control vs. pressure control — the great non-debate: no RCT has ever shown a mortality difference between VC and PC. A Cochrane review found no difference in barotrauma, ventilator duration, or ICU stay between the two. A randomized comparison of pressure-control with spontaneous breathing (PC-SV) vs. volume-control assist-control in ARDS patients found no mortality difference between strategies, though PC used less sedation with otherwise similar outcomes. The mode doesn't matter nearly as much as the settings — low tidal volume, low plateau pressure, and appropriate PEEP achieve lung protection in either VC or PC.

SIMV — the mode that won't die, and the data against it: the theoretical appeal of SIMV (mandatory breaths plus preserved spontaneous effort between them) runs into a practical problem: between mandatory breaths, the patient does all the work on unsupported spontaneous breaths unless pressure support is added — potentially exhausting an already sick patient and prolonging weaning.

Ortiz et al.'s propensity-matched secondary analysis compared 350 patients ventilated with SIMV plus pressure support against 1,228 patients on assist-control (1,578 patients total). After adjustment for propensity score, the difference in in-hospital mortality was not significant (OR 1.04) — SIMV offered no measurable advantage over assist-control. SIMV was designed for an earlier era of weaning practice; a structured spontaneous breathing trial protocol now accomplishes the same goal more directly.

Reading these two findings together: the choice between VC and PC is genuinely inconsequential as long as the lung-protective guardrails in section 6 are followed, while the choice to use SIMV specifically has actual negative evidence behind avoiding it, not just a theoretical preference.

2.3 — Advanced & Closed-Loop Modes

PRVC / VC+ / PCV-VG — the hybrid (also called Volume Target, AutoFlow, APVcmv): you set a target tidal volume; the ventilator delivers pressure-controlled breaths but adjusts the pressure level breath-by-breath to hit that VT target — combining decelerating flow with a volume guarantee. A meta-analysis of 17 studies found lower peak pressures and better compliance compared to pure volume-controlled ventilation in the operating room, though no ICU mortality data exists for this specific comparison.

The catch: if the patient takes a large spontaneous breath, the ventilator may interpret this as improved compliance and decrease support accordingly — this can lead to under-support during genuinely high patient demand, precisely when more support might be needed.

NAVA (Neurally Adjusted Ventilatory Assist, Maquet/Getinge) — triggers off neural signal, not flow or pressure: a specialized nasogastric catheter with electrodes detects the electrical activity of the diaphragm (Edi, measured in microvolts). The ventilator delivers pressure proportional to that signal: PIP = NAVA level (cmH2O/µV) × (Edi max − Edi min) + PEEP. Turning up the NAVA level is like turning up a gain knob — the harder the diaphragm signals, the more support the ventilator delivers, instantly.

This is the best-synchrony mode available, since it triggers off neural effort rather than flow or pressure change, and may reduce over-assistance and diaphragm atrophy as a result. A meta-analysis of 7 randomized/crossover trials (693 patients) found NAVA improved weaning success compared with other partial-support modes (OR 1.93), reduced duration of mechanical ventilation (MD −2.63 days), reduced hospital mortality (OR 0.58), and increased ventilator-free days (MD +3.48) relative to comparator modes.

Real limitations: it requires a special, comparatively expensive Edi catheter, is only available on Maquet/Getinge platforms, and titrating the NAVA level takes practice — set too high and you risk over-assistance, too low and the patient does the work alone. Watch the Edi trend: if Edi falls as NAVA level rises, the ventilator is unloading the diaphragm appropriately; if Edi stays high despite escalating support, the patient still needs more.

PAV+ (Proportional Assist Ventilation Plus, Puritan Bennett/Medtronic) — amplifies the patient's own effort by a fixed percentage: the ventilator continuously measures respiratory system compliance and resistance in real time (via brief end-inspiratory occlusions roughly every 10–15 breaths, applying the equation of motion), then delivers pressure assistance proportional to the patient's own instantaneous inspiratory effort. A 70% gain setting means the ventilator does 70% of the calculated work.

Earlier meta-analyses suggested better patient-ventilator synchrony and weaning success compared to pressure support. The PROMIZING trial (Bosma et al., NEJM 2025) — 573 patients across 23 centers in 7 countries, the largest RCT of PAV+ to date — found no significant difference between PAV+ and PSV in time to successful liberation from mechanical ventilation, reintubation rate, ventilator-free days, or 90-day mortality (29.6% vs. 26.6%). Patients on PAV+ required less sedation, though this was not a prespecified outcome. Clinicians in the trial frequently reported unfamiliarity with PAV+, and often switched patients to PSV as a result — a real-world adoption barrier that may partly explain the gap between the physiologic promise and the trial result.

Bottom line: PAV+ works as designed and monitors patient effort well at the bedside, but does not clearly outperform PSV when weaning is already protocolized and structured — which most of the benefit in earlier, smaller trials may have reflected rather than the mode itself.

ASV / IntelliVent-ASV (Adaptive Support Ventilation, Hamilton Medical) — the autopilot: you enter the patient's weight and a target percentage of predicted minute ventilation; the ventilator uses the Otis equation to calculate the tidal volume/rate combination that minimizes calculated work of breathing, then auto-adjusts pressure to reach it. IntelliVent-ASV extends this further, also auto-adjusting FiO2 and PEEP based on SpO2 and EtCO2.

Compared with SIMV specifically, ASV has shown shorter mechanical ventilation duration and lower peak airway pressures across several studies and a pooled meta-analysis (11 studies, 627 patients). Compared with PSV specifically, meta-analyses have generally found no significant difference in weaning outcomes — the advantage over SIMV doesn't necessarily translate into an advantage over already-good manual weaning practice.

The real critique: ASV has a "black box" feel — it can be genuinely difficult to know exactly what the ventilator is doing and why at any given moment, and it may not be well suited to complex ARDS physiology where the underlying assumptions behind the Otis equation optimization break down.

None of these four modes has proven a mortality advantage over the three workhorse modes (VC-AC, PC-AC, PSV) in ICU practice specifically, with the partial exception of NAVA's positive meta-analytic signal. The unifying lesson: fancier does not reliably mean better, and every one of these should be judged on the actual trial evidence for the specific comparison and population in question, not on how sophisticated the underlying physiology sounds.

3. Initial Settings & the Waveform

Reasonable starting point (non-ARDS): tidal volume ~8 mL/kg predicted body weight per Owens' general approach (narrower to 6 mL/kg PBW specifically once ARDS is identified — see section 6), rate 12–16/min, PEEP 5 cmH2O, FiO2 titrated to target SpO2, check an arterial blood gas 15–20 minutes after initiating ventilation.

4. Ventilator Loops

A loop plots two variables against each other for a complete breath, and the resulting shape — its area, its width, where it bends — encodes information numbers alone don't: work of breathing, hysteresis, the point where you're overdistending rather than recruiting.

Pressure-volume (P-V) loop: pressure on the x-axis, volume on the y-axis. Traces counterclockwise — inspiration up, expiration down. Area inside the loop = work of breathing; width of the loop = hysteresis; slope = compliance.

Flow-volume (F-V) loop: flow on the y-axis, volume on the x-axis. Expiratory limb typically drawn above baseline, inspiratory limb below — shape changes here identify airway obstruction and restriction.

The physiology behind "beaking": once alveoli are near their elastic limit, additional pressure buys essentially no additional volume — the loop's upper limb flattens into a shape classically compared to a penguin's beak or a duck's bill. This is the direct visual correlate of volutrauma/barotrauma risk building in real time.

A P-V loop can also reveal patient effort: a clockwise deflection just before a mechanical breath starts (rather than the expected counterclockwise trace) indicates the patient is actively triggering, not just being passively ventilated — useful for spotting dyssynchrony that a numeric readout won't show.

Because it's the inspiratory limb of a volume-controlled breath that's fixed by the ventilator, the F-V loop's diagnostic information for airway resistance lives almost entirely in the expiratory limb — that's the side to watch.

4.3 — Clinical Applications of Loops

Detecting overdistension before it's obvious elsewhere: a beaking P-V loop can flag volutrauma risk before plateau pressure alone tells the full story, especially in a patient whose compliance is changing breath to breath.

Titrating PEEP and recruitment maneuvers: comparing pre- and post-recruitment P-V loops — leftward shift with improved compliance is the visual confirmation a maneuver worked, rather than inferring it from oxygenation alone.

Catching auto-PEEP and air trapping: an F-V loop that doesn't return to baseline, or a P-V loop with a rightward creep breath over breath, both flag dynamic hyperinflation — worth a direct look whenever a patient is tachypneic on the vent or hemodynamics are drifting for no clear reason.

Detecting circuit leaks: a P-V loop that fails to close — a visible break rather than a continuous trace — indicates a leak in the circuit or around the cuff, worth checking before troubleshooting anything more complex.

None of this replaces the numbers (Pplat, driving pressure, compliance) from section 3 — loops are the visual layer that makes those numbers easier to trust or to catch drifting, especially breath to breath at the bedside rather than on a delayed chart review.

5. Capnography on the Vent

Capnography measures CO2 partial pressure continuously through the respiratory cycle, producing a waveform (capnogram) with four distinct phases per breath. EtCO2 — the value most people quote — is just the number read at the end of the plateau, phase III.

Normal Capnogram

I (dead space)Start of exhalation — CO2-free gas from the anatomic dead space (conducting airways) is expelled first.
II (alveolar upstroke)CO2-rich alveolar gas begins mixing with the dead-space gas; the steep rise.
III (alveolar plateau)Relatively uniform alveolar gas is exhaled; EtCO2 is read at the very end of this phase, the point closest to alveolar (and by extension, arterial) CO2.

Normal values (adult): EtCO2 35–45 mmHg. PaCO2 typically exceeds EtCO2 by 2–5 mmHg (the a-ET gradient) due to dead-space ventilation — a gradient that widens meaningfully in conditions that increase dead space, like PE or severe COPD.

EtCO2 is a ventilation, perfusion, and metabolism monitor simultaneously — a sudden change can mean any of the three shifted, which is exactly why the waveform shape matters as much as the number.

The dangerous confusion: a flat capnography line does not automatically mean esophageal intubation. If the tube was confirmed correctly placed moments earlier, an abrupt flatline is a circulatory arrest until proven otherwise — chest compressions restore some waveform even in a correctly placed tube, so a flat line during ongoing CPR (rather than at a pulse check) is a different problem than a flat line right after intubation.

5.3 — Capnography in Cardiac Arrest

ETCO2 during CPR is primarily a marker of pulmonary blood flow, and therefore a real-time, breath-by-breath readout of compression quality and cardiac output — arguably its single highest-yield use in resuscitation.

5.4 — Clinical Applications by Setting

Traumatic brain injury: target EtCO2 35–40 mmHg. Avoid hyperventilation outside of brief, deliberate use for acute herniation with Cushing's reflex — cerebral vasoconstriction from over-ventilation can worsen ischemia in an already injured brain.

Post-ROSC: target EtCO2 35–45 mmHg — avoid both hyper- and hypoventilation in the immediate post-arrest period, when cerebral autoregulation is impaired.

Sepsis / DKA / other compensatory tachypnea: match the ventilator to what the patient's own respiratory drive is already doing rather than "normalizing" a compensatory hyperventilation — a metabolic acidosis being compensated by a low PaCO2 needs that low PaCO2 maintained, not corrected.

Procedural sedation: capnography detects hypoventilation before it becomes hypoxemia — often the earliest available warning sign, arriving before the pulse oximeter changes at all, especially with supplemental oxygen running.

If you're bagging a patient without capnography, you're ventilating blind — the waveform (or its absence) is the fastest confirmation of both airway position and adequacy of ventilation available at the bedside.

6. Lung-Protective Ventilation & ARDS

Berlin definition of ARDS: onset within 1 week of a known clinical insult; bilateral opacities not fully explained by effusion, collapse, or nodules; respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload; PaO2/FiO2 categorized mild (200–300), moderate (100–200), or severe (<100), all with PEEP ≥5 cmH2O.

6.1 — The ARDSNet Protocol, Step by Step

1. Calculate predicted body weight (PBW) — see the exact formulas in section 10.

2. Select any assist-control mode (volume or pressure).

3. Set initial tidal volume to 8 mL/kg PBW.

4. Reduce VT by 1 mL/kg at intervals ≤2 hours until VT = 6 mL/kg PBW.

5. Set initial rate to approximate baseline minute ventilation, not to exceed 35/min.

6. Adjust VT and rate to achieve pH and plateau pressure goals below.

Neurally-adjusted ventilatory assist (NAVA) and other neural-signal-driven modes use diaphragmatic electrical activity to trigger and size each breath — a genuinely different triggering paradigm, not covered in depth here given its lower general-reference yield relative to the modes in section 2.

6.4 — What the Later Trials Actually Found: Recruitment, PEEP Titration, HFOV, ECMO

Several ARDS interventions that read as intuitively appealing — aggressive recruitment, individualized PEEP titration, high-frequency oscillation — have since been tested in large trials with results that complicate or outright contradict the textbook chapters written about them, including chapters in Owens' own book. Worth knowing precisely because the intuition and the evidence point different directions here.

OSCILLATE (Ferguson et al., NEJM 2013)High-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) targeting lung recruitment vs. ARDSNet low-VT/high-PEEP control, in moderate-severe ARDSStopped early for harm — in-hospital mortality 47% (HFOV) vs. 35% (control), RR 1.33. HFOV required significantly more vasopressor support, suggesting hemodynamic compromise from the sustained high airway pressures.
OSCAR (Young et al., NEJM 2013)HFOV vs. usual ventilatory care, broader ARDS population, concurrent with OSCILLATENo significant difference in 30-day mortality either direction (41.7% vs. 41.1%) — no benefit, but also no harm signal, in contrast to OSCILLATE's more protocolized, higher-pressure approach.
EOLIA (Combes et al., NEJM 2018)Early veno-venous ECMO vs. conventional lung-protective ventilation with ECMO available as rescue, in very severe ARDS Stopped early for futility at 249/331 planned patients. 60-day mortality 35% (ECMO) vs. 46% (control), a large absolute difference that did not reach significance (p=0.09) — underpowered by early stopping, and confounded by 28% crossover to ECMO in the control arm. Widely interpreted as supportive of early ECMO despite the non-significant p-value, given the effect size and crossover contamination.

The pattern across all six trials: the interventions that sound most aggressive or most physiologically elegant (maximal recruitment, HFOV) either caused harm or showed no benefit, while the more individualized or conservative approaches (esophageal manometry-guided PEEP, severity-stratified higher PEEP, early ECMO in genuinely refractory cases) show a more nuanced, patient-specific signal. Routine high-frequency oscillation and aggressive stepwise recruitment to very high pressures are not supported by current evidence as default ARDS strategies, regardless of how they're described in older textbook chapters.

6.5 — Patient-Ventilator Dyssynchrony

Dyssynchrony between the patient's own respiratory effort and the ventilator's delivered breath is common, frequently under-recognized, and directly relevant to both comfort and lung-protective goals — a mistimed breath can deliver an effectively much larger tidal volume than the number on the screen suggests.

TypeMechanismConsequence
Ineffective triggeringPatient effort occurs but fails to trigger a breath — most often because auto-PEEP raises the trigger threshold beyond what a weakened effort can overcomeMissed breaths, increased work of breathing, often under-recognized without close waveform review
Double triggeringPatient's neural inspiratory time outlasts the ventilator's set inspiratory time, triggering a second breath before full exhalation The two breaths can functionally "fuse" into an effective tidal volume far larger than either individual breath — a hidden driving-pressure and volutrauma risk not visible on the standard VT readout
Premature cyclingMechanical inspiration ends before the patient's neural inspiration doesIncreases risk of double triggering as the patient tries to keep inhaling against a closing valve
Delayed cyclingMechanical inspiration continues after the patient has already begun exhalingContributes to air trapping and subsequent ineffective triggering from insufficient expiratory time
Flow starvationSet inspiratory flow rate is too low to meet patient demandVisible as a concave "scooping" on the pressure-time waveform during inspiration — patient appears to be "fighting" the ventilator

Most dyssynchrony is a waveform-recognition problem before it's anything else — the pressure-time and flow-time traces (section 3) and the loops (section 4) usually show it plainly once you know the pattern to look for, well before it becomes clinically obvious as agitation or a rising sedation requirement.

The fix isn't always more sedation. A patient fighting the ventilator, treated reflexively with escalating sedation, often ends up on more sedation, more delirium, and a longer ICU stay without the underlying dyssynchrony ever being addressed. Before reaching for another dose, try: adjusting trigger sensitivity; switching to a pressure mode for better flow matching (section 2); increasing the inspiratory flow rate if in volume control; or simply asking the respiratory therapist what they're already seeing at the bedside — often the fastest diagnostic step of all.

6.6 — Status Asthmaticus: A Distinct Ventilator Strategy

Severe asthma on the ventilator is a fundamentally different problem than ARDS — the goal is not lung-protective low-pressure ventilation for a stiff lung, but deliberately minimizing minute ventilation to give an obstructed lung enough time to fully exhale before the next breath.

The core strategy — controlled hypoventilation with permissive hypercapnia:

Tidal volume 5–8 mL/kg (lower than typical, to limit the volume that must be exhaled). Respiratory rate 10–14/min (deliberately low — this is the opposite instinct from most hypoxemic respiratory failure). High inspiratory flow rate (60–100 L/min) to shorten inspiratory time and maximize the time available for exhalation. Permissive hypercapnia, generally tolerated as long as pH remains >7.2, occasionally to extreme degrees in case reports (PaCO2 >200 mmHg, pH <7.0) without apparent harm when oxygenation is maintained.

Monitoring toolWhat to watch for
Flow-time / flow-volume waveformExpiratory flow failing to return to zero before the next breath — direct visual confirmation of incomplete exhalation (section 4)
Plateau pressureStill the best surrogate for alveolar pressure — keep it in view even while accepting a higher PIP from airway resistance
End-inspiratory lung volume / auto-PEEPDirect markers of dynamic hyperinflation, checked via expiratory hold maneuver

Sedation, occasionally with neuromuscular blockade, is often necessary to prevent the patient from fighting a deliberately hypoventilating strategy and to reduce barotrauma/accidental extubation risk. No randomized trial has established the efficacy of permissive hypercapnia in status asthmaticus specifically — the evidence base is case series and physiologic reasoning, not RCT data, but it remains the standard approach given the clear mechanistic rationale for avoiding dynamic hyperinflation over achieving normocapnia.

6.7 — Invasive Ventilation in COPD

Distinct from status asthmaticus in its target, though the underlying obstructive-physiology reasoning overlaps: the goal in COPD is not a fixed normal PaCO2, but returning the patient toward whatever PaCO2 they were chronically compensated at before this admission.

Permissive hypercapnia to the patient's own baseline: a COPD patient with a chronic PaCO2 of 60 mmHg does not need to be ventilated to 40 — pushing toward a "normal" number in a chronically compensated patient risks post-hypercapnic alkalosis and doesn't reflect their actual physiologic target.

Matching extrinsic PEEP to intrinsic auto-PEEP: in a patient with significant auto-PEEP from dynamic hyperinflation, setting extrinsic (ventilator) PEEP to roughly 70–85% of the measured intrinsic PEEP eases the work of triggering a breath without meaningfully worsening hyperinflation — a counterintuitive move (adding PEEP to a hyperinflated lung) that specifically targets the trigger threshold, not the total lung volume.

Same monitoring principles as status asthmaticus apply — watch the flow-volume waveform for failure to return to baseline, and prioritize adequate expiratory time over chasing a normal blood gas.

6.8 — Recruitment Maneuver Technique (Where Still Used Selectively)

Section 6.4 covered why the ART trial's maximal stepwise recruitment (PEEP to 45, peak to 60) caused harm. That doesn't mean all recruitment technique is abandoned — a more judicious maneuver, followed by a decremental PEEP trial to find the lowest PEEP that preserves the recruitment benefit, remains in selective use.

TechniqueHow it's done
Staircase recruitment maneuverStepwise PEEP increases (e.g., 4→10 cmH2O for several breaths, 10→15 for several breaths, 15→20 for a longer run), holding peak pressure well below the 60 cmH2O used in ART — commonly capped around 40–50 cmH2O.
Decremental PEEP trialAfter recruitment, PEEP is reduced stepwise (e.g., by 2 cmH2O every few minutes) while monitoring oxygenation or compliance, identifying the lowest PEEP level at which the recruitment benefit is lost — that level, plus a small margin, becomes the working PEEP.

The distinction that matters clinically: technique and magnitude, not the concept of recruitment itself, are what ART's harm signal targets. A brief, moderate-pressure maneuver followed by a decremental PEEP trial is a materially different intervention from sustained ventilation at 45–60 cmH2O, and the evidence against the latter shouldn't be read as evidence against the former.

6.9 — Intraoperative Protective Ventilation (A Different Population)

Everything above this point in section 6 concerns critically ill ICU/ED patients. Protective ventilation in the operating room, for patients without ARDS, is a related but distinct evidence base — worth including given how directly relevant it is to anesthesiology practice.

TrialFinding
IMPROVE (Futier et al., NEJM 2013)400 intermediate/high-risk patients undergoing major abdominal surgery, randomized to lung-protective ventilation (VT 6–8 mL/kg PBW, PEEP 6–8 cmH2O, recruitment maneuvers every 30 minutes) vs. a non-protective strategy (VT 10–12 mL/kg, no PEEP). The protective strategy reduced major postoperative pulmonary and extrapulmonary complications and improved other clinical outcomes — the trial that established protective ventilation as relevant beyond critical illness.
PROVHILO (Lancet 2014)900 patients undergoing open abdominal surgery, both arms already using low tidal volume, randomized to high PEEP (12 cmH2O) with recruitment maneuvers vs. low PEEP (≤2 cmH2O) without. No difference in postoperative pulmonary complications (40% vs. 39%) — the high-PEEP/recruitment group had more intraoperative hypotension and higher vasopressor requirements. High PEEP with recruitment added risk without added benefit once low tidal volume was already the baseline.

Reading IMPROVE and PROVHILO together: low tidal volume appears to be the component doing the real work. PEEP and recruitment maneuvers added on top, at least in the routine open-abdominal-surgery population studied in PROVHILO, added hemodynamic cost without a demonstrated complication-rate benefit. This mirrors, in a lower-acuity population, the same pattern from section 6.4: aggressive add-on maneuvers don't consistently outperform doing the fundamentals well.

Later trials (PROBESE in obese surgical patients, individualized PEEP-titration approaches like iPROVE-OLV in one-lung ventilation) suggest the PEEP/recruitment question may still have a role in specific subgroups — obesity, thoracic surgery, video-assisted approaches — even where the unselected open-abdominal-surgery population in PROVHILO showed no benefit. This remains an active area rather than a fully settled one.

7. NIV, CPAP, BiPAP & HFNC

Why HFNC actually works — the mechanism behind the trial results: dead space washout (high flow clears CO2-laden exhaled gas from the nasopharynx, replacing it with fresh gas, improving ventilatory efficiency without necessarily changing tidal volume); a modest, flow-dependent positive airway pressure effect (roughly 1 cmH2O per 10 L/min of flow with the mouth closed, far short of CPAP but non-trivial); improved humidification and mucociliary clearance; and reduced work of breathing from not having to generate inspiratory flow against a fixed low-flow cannula. These mechanisms are additive rather than any single one explaining the whole effect.

TrialFinding
3CPO (Gray et al., Lancet 2008)CPAP and NIV both relieved dyspnea and metabolic derangement faster than standard oxygen in acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema, with no significant difference in short-term mortality between CPAP and BiPAP/NIV specifically — either is a reasonable first-line noninvasive strategy for this indication.
Brochard et al., NEJM 1995NIV in acute COPD exacerbations reduced intubation rate, length of stay, and in-hospital mortality compared to standard therapy — foundational evidence for NIV as first-line in COPD exacerbation with respiratory acidosis.
FLORALI (Frat et al., NEJM 2015)In acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, high-flow nasal cannula reduced 90-day mortality compared to standard oxygen or NIV in the overall trial population, with the mortality benefit concentrated in patients with more severe hypoxemia (P/F ≤200).
ROX index (Roca et al., J Crit Care 2016)(SpO2/FiO2) ÷ respiratory rate, calculated at set intervals on HFNC in pneumonia-related hypoxemic respiratory failure. A ROX ≥4.88 at 2, 6, or 12 hours predicted HFNC success; lower values predicted impending HFNC failure and the need for escalation to intubation.

When NOT to reach for NIV: altered mental status without a reversible cause, inability to protect the airway, hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled vomiting, facial trauma/surgery precluding a mask seal, or a patient who simply isn't tolerating it — these are reasons to move toward intubation rather than escalating NIV settings.

CPAP and BiPAP are functionally distinct even though both are "NIV": CPAP delivers one continuous pressure (primarily an oxygenation/afterload-reduction tool, classic for cardiogenic pulmonary edema), while BiPAP alternates inspiratory and expiratory pressure levels (adding real ventilatory support, classic for COPD/hypercapnic failure). Reach for the one that matches the physiologic problem — hypoxemic vs. hypercapnic — not out of habit.

8. Weaning & Extubation

Screening criteria to attempt a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT): adequate oxygenation (P/F >150–200, FiO2 ≤0.4–0.5, PEEP ≤5–8), hemodynamic stability (no or minimal vasopressor support, no active ischemia), adequate mentation (arousable, able to initiate a breath), afebrile without major metabolic derangement, and an improving underlying cause of respiratory failure.

SBT tolerance criteria (T-piece, CPAP 5, or low-level pressure support 5–8 cmH2O for 30–120 minutes): RR <35, SpO2 >90%, HR <140 or <20% change from baseline, SBP 90–180 or <20% change from baseline, no significant increased work of breathing (accessory muscles, thoracoabdominal paradox), no diaphoresis or agitation.

Rapid Shallow Breathing Index (RSBI / Tobin Index): measured during the first minute of unassisted breathing, before fatigue sets in.

RSBI = respiratory rate (breaths/min) ÷ tidal volume (L). RSBI <105 predicts a good likelihood of successful extubation; ≥105 predicts substantially higher weaning-failure risk.

RSBI is one data point, not a standalone extubation order — integrate it with SBT tolerance, secretion burden, cough strength, and overall trajectory. Controlled mechanical ventilation causes ventilator-induced diaphragm dysfunction (VIDD) at roughly 6% atrophy per day of full support; serial diaphragm ultrasound (thickening fraction, excursion) can flag this developing before it shows up as a failed SBT, and preserving partial spontaneous effort (pressure support rather than full control) mitigates it.

8.1 — Classifying Weaning Difficulty (Boles/ERS-ATS 2007)

CategoryDefinition
Simple weaningSuccessful extubation after the first SBT attempt, no further difficulty
Difficult weaningRequires up to 3 SBTs or up to 7 days from the first SBT to achieve successful extubation
Prolonged weaningRequires more than 3 SBTs or more than 7 days of weaning after the first SBT

This classification, from the Sixth International Consensus Conference on Intensive Care Medicine (Boles et al., Eur Respir J 2007), is what most later weaning outcome studies are stratified by — worth knowing the terms even if the exact cutoffs vary slightly between references.

8.2 — The ACCP/AARC/SCCM Evidence-Based Guidelines

MacIntyre et al.'s 2001 collaborative task force (American College of Chest Physicians, American Association for Respiratory Care, American College of Critical Care Medicine) remains the foundational evidence-based framework most SBT protocols descend from.

Core recommendations: assess readiness daily using objective criteria rather than clinical impression alone; conduct the SBT itself as the primary diagnostic test of extubation readiness rather than relying on weaning predictors like RSBI in isolation; use a standardized approach (protocol-driven, often nurse- or respiratory-therapist-initiated) rather than ad hoc physician judgment alone, since standardized approaches shorten ventilator duration in trial data.

ConsiderationEvidence
Failed extubation consequencesEpstein et al. found reintubation itself is associated with worse outcomes independent of the underlying reason for extubation failure — failed extubation isn't a neutral "try again" event.
Following commands pre-extubationNot an absolute requirement — King et al. found some patients unable to reliably follow commands can still be extubated successfully, so this criterion should inform rather than gate the decision alone.

8.3 — Sedation & the ABCDEF Bundle: Getting to a Screenable Patient

Everything in sections 8.1–8.2 assumes a patient who is arousable enough to screen and trial in the first place — how you manage sedation is what actually gets a patient to that point, and it's arguably the single biggest lever on ventilator duration overall.

TrialFinding
ABC Trial (Girard et al., Lancet 2008)Pairing daily spontaneous awakening trials (sedation interruption) with daily spontaneous breathing trials produced more ventilator-free days, shorter ICU and hospital stay, and improved 1-year mortality compared to a standard sedation protocol with SBT alone. Coordinating both interventions outperformed either one in isolation.
ABCDE bundle implementation studyA prospective before-after study found bundle implementation produced roughly 3 additional ventilator-free days versus usual care, plus reduced delirium and increased likelihood of early mobilization — consistent with the individual-component RCT evidence it's built from.

The ABCDEF bundle in brief: Assess/prevent/manage pain · Both SAT and SBT, coordinated daily · Choice of sedation/analgesia (favoring lighter, non-benzodiazepine sedation where possible) · Delirium: assess, prevent, manage · Early mobility and exercise · Family engagement and empowerment.

Real-world implementation of paired SAT/SBT remains inconsistent despite the trial evidence — workload and staffing constraints are the most commonly cited barriers, not disagreement with the evidence itself. Deep sedation early in an ICU course is an independently modifiable risk factor for worse outcomes; targeting lighter sedation from the start, not just interrupting it later, is the more current framing.

8.4 — Post-Extubation Support: Cuff Leak and Prophylactic NIV/HFNC

A passed SBT answers the breathing question. Two more questions — airway patency and reintubation risk — determine what happens in the minutes and hours immediately after the tube comes out.

Cuff leak test: in patients at risk of laryngeal edema, deflate the cuff and assess for an audible or measured air leak around the tube before extubating. A reduced or absent leak (a "failed" cuff leak test) flags elevated risk of post-extubation stridor and reintubation.

A meta-analysis found that giving IV corticosteroids at least 4 hours before extubation in patients with a failed cuff leak test reduced post-extubation stridor and reintubation — benefit confined to this high-risk subgroup, not a reason to steroid-treat every extubation.

Prophylactic NIV or HFNC after extubation: in patients at high risk for reintubation (age >65, heart failure as the indication for ventilation, moderate-severe COPD, APACHE II >12 at extubation, BMI >30, difficult/prolonged prior weaning, ≥2 comorbidities, or ventilation >7 days), applying prophylactic respiratory support immediately after extubation — rather than waiting to see if the patient fails — reduces reintubation.

Hernández et al. (JAMA 2016) found HFNC non-inferior to NIV for this purpose in high-risk patients, and generally better tolerated. A separate Hernández trial (JAMA 2016) found HFNC also outperformed conventional oxygen in low-risk patients. A post hoc analysis found patients with ≥4 risk factors did better specifically on NIV (23.9% vs. 45.7% reintubation) — the non-inferiority of HFNC may not hold at the highest end of the risk spectrum.

The practical framing: extubate high-risk patients directly onto prophylactic HFNC as the new default, reserving NIV preferentially for the hypercapnic/COPD/heart-failure phenotypes or for patients with a very high burden of risk factors — rather than waiting reactively for post-extubation failure to declare itself before acting.

8.5 — Tracheostomy Timing

StudyFinding
TracMan (Young et al., JAMA 2013)909 patients randomized to tracheostomy within 4 days vs. after 10 days (if still indicated). No significant difference in 30-day mortality, ICU stay, or hospital stay — early tracheostomy did not clearly improve outcomes in this largest trial to date.
Later pooled meta-analyses (umbrella reviews, 2024-2025)Some subsequent meta-analyses of pooled RCT data suggest a modest mortality and VAP-incidence benefit with early tracheostomy, but authors of the umbrella reviews describe the overall evidence quality as weak, given the inherent difficulty of predicting up front which patients will actually require prolonged ventilation.

Practical synthesis: there's no strong mandate to rush tracheostomy at a fixed early timepoint based on current evidence, but a reasonable, well-supported approach is to perform it once prolonged ventilation is genuinely anticipated (commonly cited as feasible in the 7–10 day range) rather than adhering to either extreme dogmatically.

9. Troubleshooting, Alarms & VAP Prevention

DOPES — the crashing ventilated patient:

Displaced ETT / cuff not inflated · Obstruction of the tube · Pneumothorax · Equipment malfunction · Stacked breaths (auto-PEEP)

DOTTS — the response: Disconnect from the ventilator · Oxygenate with a bag-valve-mask, feeling for resistance · Tube position/function (check for migration, kinking, plugging) · Tweak the vent settings once stable · Sono (ultrasound) or needle decompression if pneumothorax is suspected

Ensuring adequate ventilation and oxygenation is the priority over diagnosis when an alarm fires and the cause isn't immediately obvious — disconnect and bag first if the patient is deteriorating, diagnose second.

The differentiating maneuver for a high-pressure alarm: an inspiratory pause hold to check Pplat. High PIP with a correspondingly high Pplat is a lung or chest-wall compliance problem (ARDS, pneumothorax, mainstem intubation, atelectasis) — this single maneuver is the fastest branch point in the entire troubleshooting tree.

9.2 — VAP Prevention Bundle

Prevention rather than troubleshooting, but it belongs alongside alarm management as a daily bedside habit — the IHI Ventilator Bundle remains the most widely implemented framework, though one of its original components has since been walked back.

The IHI Ventilator Bundle, current core elements:

1. Head-of-bed elevation 30–45°. 2. Daily sedation interruption ("sedation vacation") coordinated with daily extubation-readiness/SBT assessment — see section 8.3. 3. Peptic ulcer disease prophylaxis. 4. DVT prophylaxis. 5. Daily oral care.

Correction to the original bundle: daily oral care with chlorhexidine was added as a fifth IHI element in 2010, but a 2022 practice update reversed this specifically for general ICU patients — current guidance is to not use chlorhexidine for routine oral decontamination outside of cardiac surgery. Oral care itself (mechanical cleaning, tooth brushing) remains recommended; the chlorhexidine-specific component does not.

Additional measureRole
Subglottic secretion drainageContinuous or intermittent suctioning of secretions pooling above the ETT cuff, below the vocal cords — a major aspiration reservoir, not part of the original four-element bundle but widely added since

Bundles work as a package — reviews consistently find that reliable, combined application of multiple elements produces a larger VAP reduction than any single element in isolation, which is the entire logic of treating this as an "all-or-nothing" daily checklist rather than a menu of optional items.

VAE surveillance (CDC framework) — not the same thing as clinical "VAP": current national surveillance runs on a tiered definition rather than the older clinical-pneumonia criteria. VAC (ventilator-associated condition): a sustained rise in FiO2 or PEEP after a period of stability/improvement. IVAC (infection-related VAC): a VAC plus abnormal temperature/WBC plus a new antimicrobial started. PVAP (possible VAP): an IVAC plus microbiologic or histopathologic evidence of pneumonia. Most VAEs are not infectious — atelectasis, pulmonary edema, and ARDS drive the majority of cases, with VILI as the underlying mechanism connecting an oxygenation-requirement spike back to the ventilator itself.

10. Equations & Calculations

Every number on the ventilator display or blood gas panel traces back to one of these. Collected here in one place rather than scattered across sections.

10.1 — Predicted Body Weight & Tidal Volume

Predicted body weight (PBW), ARDSNet formula
Male (in) = 50 + 2.3 × [height(in) − 60]
Female (in) = 45.5 + 2.3 × [height(in) − 60]
Male (cm) = 50 + 0.91 × [height(cm) − 152.4]
Female (cm) = 45.5 + 0.91 × [height(cm) − 152.4]
Tidal volume target
VT = 6 mL/kg × PBW (lung-protective / ARDS)
VT = 8 mL/kg × PBW (general starting point, non-ARDS, per Owens)

Use actual measured height where available; estimated height from demi-arm span (right arm extended horizontal, measured from the base of the fingers where middle and ring finger meet to the sternal notch) is an accepted substitute when accurate height isn't available. PBW equations differ meaningfully between ARDSNet, actuarial, and Stewart formulas — ARDSNet is the recommended standard since it's the formula tied to the trial that established the 6 mL/kg target in the first place.

10.2 — Ventilation

Minute ventilation (VE)
VE = Respiratory Rate × Tidal Volume
Alveolar minute ventilation (VA)
VA = Respiratory Rate × (Tidal Volume − Dead Space)
Anatomic dead space (estimate)
VDanat ≈ 1 mL per pound of ideal body weight
Physiologic dead space fraction (Bohr-Enghoff equation)
VD/VT = (PaCO2 − EtCO2) ÷ PaCO2

An elevated dead space fraction reflects alveoli that are ventilated but not perfused — worth calculating in unexplained hypercapnia or when PE is on the differential in a ventilated patient.

10.3 — Mechanics: Compliance, Resistance, Pressures

Static compliance
Cstat = VT ÷ (Pplat − PEEP)
Driving pressure
ΔP = Pplat − PEEP
Airway resistance
Raw = (PIP − Pplat) ÷ Flow
Time constant (τ)
τ = Compliance × Resistance
(One time constant ≈ 63% of exhalation complete; 3–5 time constants for near-complete passive exhalation — useful for judging whether expiratory time is adequate.)
Mean airway pressure (Paw)
Paw = [(Inspiratory Time × Respiratory Rate) ÷ 60] × (PIP − PEEP) + PEEP
Work of breathing (WOB)
WOB = ΔPressure × ΔVolume (the area enclosed by the pressure-volume loop, section 4)
Mechanical power (simplified, Gattinoni)
MP (J/min) = 0.098 × RR × VT × (Ppeak − ΔP ÷ 2)
A single energy-based metric combining rate, volume, and pressure into one number — a more complete VILI risk estimate than driving pressure alone, since it captures the contribution of respiratory rate and flow that driving pressure doesn't. Higher mechanical power is associated with mortality independent of its individual components, in observational cohort data.

The threshold, and what actually drives it: a 2026 meta-analysis of 34 studies found mechanical power was consistently higher in nonsurvivors (mean difference 1.91 J/min), with each 1 J/min increase independently associated with higher adjusted mortality (adjusted OR 1.04 per J/min). A mechanical power above 17 J/min specifically was associated with substantially greater mortality (OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.34–1.91) — a threshold echoed in several independent single-center cohorts. Despite mechanical power's multi-variable formula, subsequent analyses have found that driving pressure and respiratory rate are the individual variables most predictive of the mortality signal — meaning the extra complexity of the full mechanical power calculation may not be adding much practical value over watching those two numbers directly.

10.4 — Oxygenation

P/F ratio
P/F = PaO2 ÷ FiO2 (the ratio used directly in the Berlin ARDS definition)
Alveolar gas equation (PAO2)
PAO2 = FiO2 × (Patm − 47) − (PaCO2 ÷ R)
where Patm = atmospheric pressure (760 mmHg at sea level), 47 = water vapor pressure at body temperature, R = respiratory quotient (0.8)
A-a gradient
P(A-a)O2 = PAO2 − PaO2
a/A ratio
a/A = PaO2 ÷ PAO2
Oxygenation Index (OI)
OI = (FiO2 × Mean Airway Pressure × 100) ÷ PaO2
Arterial oxygen content (CaO2)
CaO2 = (Hb × 1.34 × SaO2) + (PaO2 × 0.003)

Oxygenation Index is the pressure-aware sibling of the P/F ratio — it incorporates how much airway pressure is being spent to achieve a given PaO2, which the P/F ratio alone doesn't capture. Both a rising OI and a falling P/F ratio point the same direction: worsening oxygenation efficiency.

10.5 — Weaning

Rapid Shallow Breathing Index (RSBI / Tobin Index)

RSBI <105 favors successful extubation; ≥105 favors weaning failure
