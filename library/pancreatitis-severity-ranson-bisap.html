Pancreatitis Severity: Ranson vs BISAP

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole

Two scores, one question: who will get sick? Ranson needs 48 hours of data; BISAP needs one set of labs and a chest film. Both are now framed by the definition that actually decides severity, persistent organ failure.

01

Correction to the circulating chart

"Ranson >6 → 100% mortality" is a 1970s number. The original Ranson cohorts showed very high mortality in the top score band, but those figures predate modern critical care. Quoting 100% today overstates risk. The chart itself notes Ranson "likely overestimates mortality," and that is the reason.

The rest of the chart is accurate: Ranson requires serial data and is delayed by design; BISAP trades completeness for speed; both perform similarly for early risk stratification.

02

What "severe" means now

Scores predict. The Revised Atlanta classification defines, and it defines severity by organ failure and its duration (modified Marshall score ≥2 in respiratory, cardiovascular, or renal systems).

MildNo organ failure, no local or systemic complications.
Moderately severeOrgan failure that resolves within 48 hours, and/or local or systemic complications.
SeverePersistent organ failure beyond 48 hours.

This is why neither score settles the question at hour 24: the defining feature of severe disease, persistence, can't be known until hour 48.

03

At the bedside

  • Use BISAP early for triage; a score of 3 or more is a reason for closer monitoring or ICU review.
  • Reassess repeatedly. Persistent SIRS beyond 48 hours and a rising BUN over the first 24 hours both track with worse outcomes.
  • Classify with Revised Atlanta once the 48-hour course is known.
  • Don't let a low early score close the question; organ failure can develop after the score was calculated.
  • Don't chase scores with fluid. Aggressive early fluid caused harm without preventing severe disease (WATERFALL; see waterfall-fluids-pancreatitis).
