Aortic regurgitation

Early diastolic decrescendo murmur at the left sternal border, loudest sitting forward in held expiration. A collapsing (water-hammer) pulse and a wide pulse pressure. An Austin Flint rumble at the apex in severe AR.

Corrigan'sVisible carotid distention and collapse
de Musset'sHead bobbing with each beat
Quincke'sNail-bed capillary pulsation
Traube's"Pistol-shot" sound over the femoral artery
Müller'sPulsatile uvula
Duroziez'sTo-and-fro femoral bruit with compression

Severity tracks pulse pressure and murmur duration, not loudness. In acute severe AR the murmur can be short and soft, because LV and aortic diastolic pressures equalize quickly. A quiet murmur in a sick patient is a trap. Most eponymous signs are classic exam material but have modest sensitivity; echo decides.

02

Right-sided valves

Tricuspid regurgitation

Holosystolic murmur, left lower sternal border, louder with inspiration (Carvallo's sign).

Large v waves in the JVP; pulsatile, tender liver; RV heave.

Tricuspid stenosis

Rare, usually rheumatic and usually with mitral stenosis. Diastolic murmur at the left lower sternal border, louder with inspiration; giant a wave in the JVP.

Pulmonary stenosis

Usually congenital. Ejection systolic murmur at the left upper sternal border; widely split S2 with a soft P2; RV heave.

Pulmonary regurgitation

Usually functional, from pulmonary hypertension. Early diastolic murmur at the left upper sternal border, louder with inspiration (the Graham Steell murmur).

Inspiration increases venous return to the right heart, so right-sided murmurs get louder. One exception: the pulmonic ejection click of valvular pulmonary stenosis softens with inspiration.

03

Mitral valve prolapse

Mid-systolic click, often followed by a late systolic murmur, best at the apex. Anything that makes the LV smaller moves the click earlier and lengthens the murmur.

05

Prosthetic valves

Mechanical

Crisp opening and closing clicks; the closing click is normally the louder. A soft ejection systolic flow murmur in the aortic position is normal.

Bioprosthetic

Sounds like a native valve. A soft ejection murmur (aortic) or early diastolic flow murmur (mitral) can be normal. A new diastolic murmur in the aortic position or a new holosystolic murmur in the mitral position is not.

A softened or absent closing click on a mechanical valve is an emergency until echo excludes thrombosis or pannus.
06

Maneuvers

Correction to the circulating carousel. It groups "hand-grip / squatting" and says both soften aortic stenosis. They don't behave the same. Squatting raises venous return, so the aortic stenosis murmur gets louder. Hand-grip raises afterload, so it gets softer.

Valsalva and standing are the cleanest way to separate HOCM from aortic stenosis: HOCM gets louder, aortic stenosis gets softer. Hand-grip softens both, so it can't separate them.

07

Escalate now

  • Syncope, angina, or heart failure in aortic stenosis: prognosis falls sharply once symptomatic.
  • A new S3 in mitral regurgitation: think decompensation.
  • Wide pulse pressure with chest pain and a new AR murmur: think aortic dissection.
  • A soft or absent murmur in acute severe MR or AR: quiet can mean worse.
  • New atrial fibrillation in significant mitral stenosis: high embolic risk; anticoagulate.

The exam misses a substantial share of significant valve disease (Part 1 covers the primary-care data). Keep the threshold for echo low; the exam tells you what to look for.
