Extubation Strategy

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole

Extubation is a planned procedure with its own failure modes. Most problems start after the tube comes out, and the patients most likely to fail are the ones who were hardest to intubate.

VERIFIEDDAS extubation guidelines 2012ASA NMB guideline 2023

First decide the risk. Then decide the depth.

Default · and the only choice when the airway is at risk
Awake

Reflexes back, following commands, breathing on their own. Protective tone returns before the tube leaves.

Cost: coughing, bucking, a sympathetic surge with raised heart rate, blood pressure, and intracranial and intraocular pressure.
Selected low-risk patients only
Deep

Tube out in a deep plane, before reflexes return, for cases where coughing would damage the repair (intracranial, ophthalmic, some ENT and plastic surgery).

Cost: obstruction, hypoventilation, and no reflex protection until emergence. Not for difficult airways, full stomachs, or obesity with OSA.

01

Low risk or at risk

The DAS guidelines sort every extubation into one of two categories before anything else happens. The category decides which technique is allowed.

At risk if any of these applyWhy it matters
Known or suspected difficult intubation or mask ventilationReintubation will be at least as hard as the first time, often harder
Restricted airway access: head and neck surgery, halo, wired jaw, cervical fixationRescue options are physically limited
Airway edema, hematoma, or surgical change to the anatomyPatency after extubation is uncertain
Obesity, OSA, aspiration risk, limited respiratory reserveLittle tolerance for apnea or obstruction
Residual neuromuscular blockade, hypothermia, cardiovascular instabilityGeneral factors that make any extubation fail
At risk means awake. Deep extubation is reserved for patients with an easy airway and no aspiration risk, in the hands of someone ready to rescue it.
02

Four steps

1
Plan. Classify risk before induction is over. State the rescue plan out loud: who reintubates, with what, and where the front-of-neck kit is.
2
Prepare. Optimize the patient (oxygenation, hemodynamics, temperature, acid-base, analgesia) and the setting (equipment, skilled help, monitoring).
3
Perform. Preoxygenate, suction under direct vision, place a bite block, and remove the tube with positive pressure applied so the first breath clears secretions out rather than in.
4
Post-extubation care. Oxygen, monitoring, and staff who can recognize obstruction. Hand over the airway risk explicitly.

A bedside version of the readiness check:

RRespiratory: adequate spontaneous ventilation and oxygenation
EEvaluate: awake, following commands (if awake technique)
AAirway: suctioned, no significant edema
DDrugs: TOF ratio ≥0.9; steroid if edema risk
YYou are ready: stable, positioned, rescue plan stated
03

Reversal is a number

TOFR ≥ 0.9
Measured at the adductor pollicis before extubation
Quantitative
ASA 2023: use quantitative monitoring, not tactile or visual fade

Below a train-of-four ratio of 0.9, pharyngeal function and airway protection remain impaired even when the patient looks strong. Subjective assessment cannot reliably detect fade above about 0.4, which is why the 2023 ASA guideline recommends quantitative monitoring over clinical signs such as head lift or grip.

04

At-risk techniques

When a plain awake extubation isn't enough.

TechniqueWhat it does
Airway exchange catheterLeft in the trachea after extubation as a guide for reintubation; oxygen can be insufflated through it with care
Supraglottic airway exchangeSwap the tube for a supraglottic airway under deep anesthesia, then wake the patient on the SGA to avoid coughing on the tube
Remifentanil infusionBlunts coughing and the hemodynamic surge while the patient wakes fully on the tube
Postpone extubationStay intubated in ICU until edema or the underlying problem resolves
TracheostomyWhen the airway is unlikely to be safe for days
05

After the tube

  • Laryngospasm: jaw thrust, CPAP with 100% oxygen, deepen with propofol; small-dose succinylcholine if it doesn't break.
  • Upper airway obstruction or stridor: airway adjuncts, positioning, nebulized epinephrine; reintubate early rather than late.
  • Hypoventilation: residual opioid or blockade until proven otherwise; check the TOF and the capnogram.
  • Aspiration: head-up, suction, and a low threshold for imaging if oxygen needs rise.

The take-home. Awake extubation is the default. Deep extubation is a choice made for a specific reason in a specific low-risk patient. In a high-risk airway, the plan for failure matters as much as the plan for success.
