Extubation Decision Protocol

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole

Two decisions share one name. At the end of an anesthetic, the question is how to take the tube out safely. In the ICU, it's whether the patient can stay extubated, and what support follows. Both end with a rescue plan.

Track A · OR emergence
01

The gate

Every item is checked before the tube moves. The first three are hard stops.

  • Quantitative train-of-four ratio is 0.9 or higher (adductor pollicis)
  • Adequate spontaneous ventilation and oxygenation
  • Hemodynamically stable
  • Normothermic, with metabolic problems corrected
  • Pain is adequately controlled

Reversal is a number. Subjective fade can't be detected above a train-of-four ratio of about 0.4, so the ASA 2023 guideline calls for quantitative monitoring and a ratio of at least 0.9 before extubation.

02

Airway risk decides depth

The DAS guidelines classify every extubation as low risk or at risk before choosing a technique. Any one of these makes it at risk:

  • Known or suspected difficult intubation or mask ventilation
  • Restricted airway access (head or neck surgery, halo, wired jaw, cervical fixation)
  • Airway edema, hematoma, or changed anatomy
  • Aspiration risk (full stomach, delayed gastric emptying)
  • Obesity or obstructive sleep apnea
  • Limited respiratory or cardiovascular reserve
ClassificationTechnique
At riskAwake only. Consider an airway exchange catheter, supraglottic airway exchange, remifentanil infusion, or postponing extubation. State the rescue plan out loud.
Low risk, coughing harmful to the repairAwake by default; deep extubation acceptable in a patient with an easy airway, no aspiration risk, and a clinician ready to rescue it.

Track B · ICU liberation
03

Readiness screen

Daily. Pair it with a sedation interruption; protocolized sedation minimization is an ATS/ACCP recommendation.

  • The cause of respiratory failure is improving
  • PaO2/FiO2 is 150 or higher on PEEP of 8 or less and FiO2 of 0.5 or less
  • Hemodynamically stable (no vasopressor or a low dose)
  • Breathing on their own with sedation minimized
04

Spontaneous breathing trial

PS 5–8
cmH₂O of inspiratory support for the initial SBT (ATS/ACCP 2017)
30 min
PS 8 for 30 min vs T-piece for 2 h: more successful extubations, 82.3% vs 74.0% (Subirà 2019)

Stop the trial for any of:

  • respiratory rate above 35
  • SpO2 below 90%
  • heart rate above 140 or a sustained change of more than 20%
  • systolic pressure above 180 or below 90
  • agitation, sweating, or rising work of breathing

A rapid shallow breathing index under 105 (Yang & Tobin) predicts success, but no single index should decide extubation on its own.

05

Airway protection and stridor

Passing an SBT proves the patient can breathe, not that they can protect the airway. Check:

  • Effective cough
  • Secretions manageable (suctioned 2 times or fewer in the last 8 hours)
  • Follows commands or protects their airway

Cuff-leak test, for patients at high risk of post-extubation stridor: traumatic or difficult intubation, intubation for more than 6 days, a large tube, female sex, or reintubation after an unplanned extubation.

Failed leak (a leak below 110 mL or below 12 to 24% of the delivered tidal volume): give systemic steroids at least 4 hours before extubation (for example methylprednisolone 20 mg IV 12 hours before, then every 4 hours); a repeat leak test is not required.

06

Reintubation risk

Hernández 2016: any one factor defines high risk. Count them; more factors, more risk.

The ≥1 threshold puts most ICU patients in the high-risk group, so the count matters more than the label.

Prevention, not rescue. NIV started for established post-extubation respiratory failure did not prevent reintubation and was associated with higher ICU mortality, likely from delayed reintubation (Esteban 2004). If they're failing, reintubate.
