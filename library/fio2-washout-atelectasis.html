Washing Out With 70% Instead of 100% Oxygen at Extubation

Paschold B-S, Braeuer BLP, Santer P, et al. Impact of high versus low fraction of inspired oxygen for anaesthetic washout and extubation on postoperative pulmonary atelectasis formation and oxygenation: a randomised controlled trial.

Br J Anaesth. 2026 Aug;137(2):591–600. doi:10.1016/j.bja.2026.04.056. NCT06538740 · Single-center RCT, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston · Original social summary: British Journal of Anaesthesia (Facebook)

Verdict on the source post: the clinical claim is accurate and matches the published trial. One small thing worth flagging if you're citing this: the Facebook caption spells the first author "Pashold" — the correct spelling in the published paper is Paschold (Béla-Simon Paschold).
01

The Setup

Giving 100% oxygen right before extubation feels like the safe move — maximize the margin against desaturation. This trial tests whether that habit is actually working against you.

Clinicians commonly deliver FiO2=1.0 during anaesthetic washout and extubation specifically to optimize oxygen delivery and buy safety margin. But absorption atelectasis is a well-described phenomenon: pure oxygen in poorly-ventilated alveoli gets absorbed into the bloodstream faster than nitrogen would, causing those alveoli to collapse. This trial directly tests whether that theoretical risk translates into measurable lung volume loss and worse post-extubation oxygenation — and whether there's a "sweet spot" FiO2 that avoids both hyperoxia-driven atelectasis and hypoxia risk.

02

Design

DesignSingle-center RCT, three-arm (100% vs. 70% vs. 40% FiO2)
Population48 adults, elective surgery under general anesthesia with tracheal intubation, 1–5 hour anticipated duration
Groups24 received 100% FiO2 during washout/extubation; 24 received lower FiO2 (12 at 70%, 12 at 40%)
Primary outcomeAtelectasis formation, measured 1 min after extubation via electrical impedance tomography (EIT): center-of-ventilation shift and change in end-expiratory lung volume (EELV)
Secondary outcomesSpO2 trajectory around extubation, lowest SpO2, hypoxemia (SpO2<90%) incidence, PACU clinical outcomes

Reading the pattern correctly

The atelectasis-prevention benefit of avoiding pure oxygen held whether patients got 70% or 40% — there was no significant difference between those two lower-FiO2 groups on lung volume. But 70% specifically produced the best oxygenation trajectory in the 10 minutes after extubation. Put together: 40% avoids atelectasis just as well as 70%, but 70% is the concentration that also keeps SpO2 highest and most stable — which is why the trial's own conclusion lands on 70% as the practical target, not the lowest FiO2 that still avoids atelectasis.

04

The Physiology, Confirmed

This isn't a new mechanism — it's a well-established one getting its first dedicated washout/extubation-specific RCT.

Absorption atelectasis from high FiO2 is established physiology, and prior trials have tested FiO2 during other phases of anesthesia (recruitment maneuvers, intraoperative maintenance, TAVI procedures) with generally consistent findings that lower FiO2 reduces atelectasis risk. What's genuinely new here is the specific focus on the washout-to-extubation window — the exact moment many clinicians default to 100% oxygen for a safety margin — and using EIT (bedside, continuous, radiation-free) rather than CT to quantify the atelectasis directly at the bedside.

05

Limitations Worth Knowing

  • Small trial: 48 patients total, 24 per high/low FiO2 arm, split further into 12 and 12 for the 70%/40% comparison — underpowered to detect anything but a fairly large effect, and not powered at all for clinical outcomes like pneumonia or reintubation.
  • Single center: one academic Boston hospital; local extubation practices, patient mix, and staffing may not generalize directly.
  • Surrogate outcomes: EIT-measured lung volume and center-of-ventilation shift are validated atelectasis surrogates, but this trial doesn't report postoperative pulmonary complications, length of stay, or other patient-centered endpoints — the case for changing practice rests on physiological plausibility plus this signal, not yet on outcome data.
  • The effect was short-lived: a second journal-club summary of the full paper reports that the between-group differences had disappeared by 1 hour in PACU, with no difference in PACU hypoxemia, Air-Test results, or reintubation. These details are not in the abstract; confirm them against the full text.
  • Who wasn't studied: the same summary lists exclusions of BMI above 40, compromised airways, and thoracic or cardiac surgery. Those are the patients whose apnea reserve makes 100% oxygen at extubation most defensible.
06

Bottom Line

The trial supports its own conclusion: avoiding pure oxygen during washout preserved end-expiratory lung volume, and post-extubation SpO₂ was steadiest with 70%. Those are EIT surrogates measured in the first minutes after extubation, in 48 low-risk elective patients, and the reported differences faded within an hour without a difference in clinical outcomes. Reasonable to adopt 70% for routine low-risk extubations; not a reason to withhold 100% oxygen from the obese, the difficult airway, or anyone with little apnea reserve.
