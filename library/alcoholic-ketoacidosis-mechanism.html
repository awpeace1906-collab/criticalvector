Alcoholic Ketoacidosis: The Metabolic Shift, Confirmed

Why AKA isn't just "low glucose plus alcohol" — the NADH/NAD+ mechanism that actually drives it.

Original post: FOAMfrat (@foamfrat · foamfrat.com), co-founded by Tyler Christifulli & Samuel Ireland. This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by FOAMfrat.

Verdict on the source post: accurate, standard biochemistry throughout. The NADH/NAD+ mechanism, the gluconeogenesis and Krebs cycle inhibition, and the resulting ketogenesis are all correctly sequenced and correctly explained.
01

The Core Mechanism, Confirmed

Two enzymes, one shared cofactor, and that's the whole story.

StepReaction
1Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) converts ethanol → acetaldehyde, reducing NAD+ to NADH in the process
2Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) converts acetaldehyde → acetate, again reducing NAD+ to NADH
NetTwo sequential NAD+-consuming reactions sharply raise the hepatic NADH/NAD+ ratio

This is exactly right, and it's the hinge the entire pathophysiology turns on: ethanol metabolism doesn't just burn calories, it specifically depletes the liver's NAD+ pool relative to NADH — and NAD+ is a required cofactor for several key steps elsewhere in intermediary metabolism.

02

Why a High NADH/NAD+ Ratio Shuts Down Gluconeogenesis and the Krebs Cycle

Both downstream effects are correctly attributed to the same upstream cause.

  • Gluconeogenesis inhibition: converting lactate to pyruvate, and oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate via the malate shuttle, both require adequate NAD+. A high NADH/NAD+ ratio pushes these reactions toward their reduced forms (pyruvate→lactate accumulates instead), starving gluconeogenesis of substrate flow and contributing to the hypoglycemia or low-normal glucose classically seen in AKA — consistent with the post's framing that this is a metabolic shift, not simply "low glucose."
  • Krebs cycle inhibition: pyruvate dehydrogenase's conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA requires NAD+ as an electron acceptor; with NAD+ scarce, this step slows, acetyl-CoA entry into the Krebs cycle drops, and the cycle itself (which also depends on NAD+ at isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase) is further suppressed.
03

Why the Acetyl-CoA Has to Go Somewhere: Ketogenesis

This is the logical payoff of the mechanism, and the post gets the sequencing right.

With the Krebs cycle backed up and unable to fully process incoming acetyl-CoA (itself also increasingly supplied by lipolysis, which is favored under the fasting/starvation state typical of a binge-then-stop drinking pattern with poor oral intake), the liver diverts that excess acetyl-CoA toward ketone body synthesis. The dominant ketone produced in this NADH-excess state is specifically beta-hydroxybutyrate, not acetoacetate — because the high NADH/NAD+ ratio favors the reduction of acetoacetate to beta-hydroxybutyrate. This is an important, correctly-implied detail: standard urine ketone dipsticks primarily detect acetoacetate, not beta-hydroxybutyrate, which is one reason AKA (and DKA in states of severe NADH excess) can under-register on dipstick testing relative to a serum beta-hydroxybutyrate level.

04

Bottom Line

Every mechanistic step in the source post is accurate biochemistry, correctly sequenced: ethanol metabolism raises NADH/NAD+ → that ratio inhibits gluconeogenesis and the Krebs cycle → the liver shifts toward lipolysis and ketogenesis → beta-hydroxybutyrate accumulates, producing the acidotic state. The one clinically useful addition: because this pathway specifically favors beta-hydroxybutyrate over acetoacetate, a standard urine ketone dipstick can under-represent the true ketone burden — a serum beta-hydroxybutyrate level is the more reliable test if AKA is suspected and the dipstick is unimpressive.
