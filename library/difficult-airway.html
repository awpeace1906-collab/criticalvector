The Difficult Airway: A Framework for When Plan A Fails

Anticipate, optimize, recognize failure, rescue, and escalate. A working mental model for the ICU airway, built around the DAS 2025 algorithm and the Vortex approach.

The FrameworkEvery ICU airway has three possible outcomes

You pushed the meds. You inserted the blade. You cannot pass the tube. SpO2 is falling. What happens next depends on which of three outcomes you are in, not on trying the same thing harder.

1

Plan A works

Video laryngoscopy with a stylet, tube in, done. This happens roughly 85% of the time with VL.

2

Plan A fails but you can oxygenate

You have time. Stop. Think. Regroup.

3

Nothing works and you cannot oxygenate

Cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate (CICO). The emergency inside the emergency.

The Difficult Airway Society (DAS) 2025 guidelines keep a linear structure across four plans:

PlanApproach
ATracheal intubation
BSupraglottic airway (SGA) ventilation
CFace mask ventilation
DEmergency front of neck airway (eFONA)
Key paradigm shift, 2025

Maximize first attempt success. Do not just manage failure after it happens.

Before You StartAnticipate

The best time to identify a difficult airway is before you push the meds. The MACOCHA score (0 to 12) was built specifically for the ICU, not the OR.

Threshold

A score of 3 or higher predicts a difficult airway. Verbalize it. Prepare for it.

16% / 29%
The ID-ACCT study of 1,965 UK ICU patients found 16% had a known difficult airway, but only 29% of those were clearly identifiable at the bedside.

If MACOCHA is high and CICO risk is high, consider awake intubation.

Plan AOptimized first attempt

Make the first attempt your best attempt. DAS 2025, Gottlieb (BMJ 2026), and Freund (Intensive Care Medicine 2026) all agree that video laryngoscopy is first line for most intubations, not just the anticipated difficult ones.

85.1% vs 70.8%
The DEVICE trial (NEJM, 1,417 patients across 17 EDs and ICUs) found first pass success of 85.1% with video laryngoscopy versus 70.8% with direct laryngoscopy (P < 0.001).

Optimize everything before the blade goes in:

  • Positioning: ear to sternal notch, head of bed elevated
  • Full neuromuscular blockade confirmed
  • Video laryngoscopy with a styletted ETT, bougie at hand
  • Best available operator on the first attempt
  • Suction ready, EtCO2 connected
Why this matters

Each failed attempt roughly doubles the complication rate. First pass success is the goal. Everything after is rescue.

The Practice GapWe know what to do. We are not always doing it.

A 2026 Australia and New Zealand survey of 211 ICU clinicians (Toolis et al.) found a gap between equipment availability and consistent execution.

  • VL availability: 100%, up from 2019
  • VL as first line choice: 82%
  • Consistently use a bougie or stylet: only 15%
  • Familiar with any airway guideline: only 54%
  • Formal airway training in the past 3 years: under 50%

We have the equipment. We have the evidence. We have the guidelines. What is missing is consistent implementation.

Plan A FailsThe most important 10 seconds

First attempt failed. SpO2 is holding. Do not immediately reinsert the blade. Ask three questions.

1

Can I oxygenate?

BVM or SGA. If yes, you have time.

2

Why did it fail?

Poor view? Could not pass the tube? Secretions?

3

What will I change?

Different blade, bougie, or a different operator.

The 3-attempt rule (CAFG)

Maximum of 3 total intubation attempts. Each attempt must be different from the last. After 3, declare failed intubation and move to Plan B.

Do not repeat the same technique hoping for a different result. That is perseveration. That is how people die.

Plan BSupraglottic airway

Failed intubation but you can oxygenate: place an SGA. It is your bridge. It buys time, restores oxygenation, and gives you options.

  • Ventilate through the SGA while you regroup
  • Intubate through it: pass a flexible bronchoscope through the SGA. Know how to do this before you need it.
  • Call for help: anesthesia, ENT, whoever your institution's airway expert is

Second generation SGAs (i-gel, LMA Supreme) are preferred for their better seal and gastric drainage port.

DAS sequence

SGA has been Plan B, ahead of face mask, since the DAS 2015 guideline, reflecting its reliability as a rescue device. The 2025 update keeps that order.

The SGA is not a failure. It is a lifeline. Use it.

Plan CFace mask: the fork in the road

If the SGA fails, optimize face mask ventilation.

  • Two person technique: one seals, one squeezes
  • Jaw thrust, head tilt, OPA or NPA
  • Confirm neuromuscular blockade: a tense patient makes mask ventilation impossible
The critical decision point

Can you oxygenate via face mask?

Yes: you have time. Regroup, call for help, and consider intubation through the SGA with a bronchoscope, an awake approach, or elective FONA.

No: you are in a CICO emergency. Declare it. Say the words out loud: "This is a CICO emergency."

This is the fork in the road. One path has time. The other does not. Know which one you are on.

Plan DCICO → eFONA

Cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate. Emergency front of neck airway. Three things happen simultaneously.

1

Declare CICO out loud

Everyone in the room needs to hear it.

2

Confirm or establish full neuromuscular blockade

Give rocuronium if not already paralyzed. A tense patient makes FONA harder.

3

One final attempt at any untried technique

Only if it has not been tried yet.

If that fails

Scalpel to neck. Immediately. No more delays.

~0.5%
CICO occurs in roughly 0.5% of difficult airways. Rare, but when it happens, hesitation kills.

eFONAScalpel, bougie, tube

The recommended technique per DAS 2025, CAFG, and ASA. DAS 2025 standardized a vertical skin incision.

1

Stabilize the larynx with the non-dominant hand.

2

Vertical skin incision, roughly 4 cm, over the cricothyroid membrane.

3

Horizontal stab through the cricothyroid membrane.

4

Turn the scalpel 90 degrees, sharp edge caudal, maintain traction.

5

Slide the bougie through the incision, feeling for tracheal ring clicks.

6

Railroad a 6.0 cuffed ETT over the bougie.

7

Inflate the cuff, confirm with EtCO2, secure.

Why scalpel-bougie-tube: higher success under stress, a definitive airway immediately, and fewer failure points than a cannula technique.

83% vs 18%
In one porcine model, scalpel-bougie technique achieved 83% rescue oxygenation versus 18% with cannula in thick pretracheal tissue.

The VortexA crisis mental model

Traditional algorithms are flowcharts. In a crisis, it is hard to read a flowchart. The Vortex is a visual cognitive aid built for real time use.

  • Green zone: the patient is oxygenated. You are safe.
  • The funnel: three lifelines back to green: ETT, SGA, face mask

Each lifeline gets a best effort. If it fails, move to the next.

Bottom of the funnel

All three lifelines exhausted. CICO → eFONA.

The Vortex does not tell you how to intubate. It tells you when to stop trying and move on. That is the part we are bad at.

Human FactorsSafety-I to Safety-II

The most common human factors in airway disasters are behavioral, not technical.

  • Perseveration: repeating the same failed technique 4, 5, 6 times
  • Failure to declare CICO: everyone knows it is a crisis but nobody says the words
  • Reluctance to cut: "I've never done a cric," so you keep trying things that are not working

Endlich et al. (2026) applied a Safety-II framework to DAS 2025. Traditional airway management focuses on preventing failure (Safety-I). DAS 2025 legitimizes escalation as an adaptive response, not a failure.

Escalation is not failure. It is adaptation under pressure.

The Team BriefOut loud before every intubation

"This is a low, moderate, or high risk airway.
Plan A is video laryngoscopy with a stylet. Bougie is at the bedside.
Plan B is the i-gel, sized by ideal body weight.
Plan C is face mask ventilation.
Plan D is a surgical airway. Cric kit is on the cart.
We have all of our equipment and adjuncts at hand.
Any questions before we start?"

This takes 30 seconds. It prevents chaos.

An Uncomfortable Truth

Emergency cricothyrotomy is needed in under 1% of difficult airways. It is rare. But when it is needed and not performed, or performed too late, people die.

The NAP4 audit found that failure to perform timely cricothyrotomy was a recurring theme in airway related ICU deaths.

The biggest barrier is not skill. It is psychological.

  • "I've never done one."
  • "Maybe one more attempt will work."
  • "Someone else should do this."

The algorithm gives you permission to cut. Training and simulation give you the skill. If your institution has a Difficult Airway Response Team, utilize it early. But you have to make the decision.

Cheat SheetQuick reference

Before
MACOCHA score. If ≥ 3, prepare for difficulty. If CICO risk is high, consider awake intubation.
Plan A
VL with stylet, bougie at hand. Optimize positioning, NMB, suction. Best operator available. Max 3 attempts, each different.
Plan B
SGA (i-gel or LMA). Ventilate, regroup. Consider intubation through the SGA.
Plan C
Face mask ventilation. Two person technique. OPA/NPA. Optimize NMB.
Plan D (CICO)
Declare it. Confirm NMB. One final untried technique. If still CICO, scalpel-bougie-tube, now.

Equipment for Plan D: #20 scalpel, bougie, 6.0 cuffed ETT, 10 mL syringe. Know where it is.

Closing Thoughts

The difficult airway algorithm is not about memorizing a flowchart. It is about building a mental model so that when Plan A fails, and it will eventually, you do not freeze.

Anticipate (MACOCHA) → Optimize (first pass success) → Recognize failure (3-attempt rule) → Rescue (SGA → BVM) → Escalate (eFONA)

Escalation is not failure. It is adaptation under pressure. The best airway experts are not the ones who never fail. They are the ones who recognize failure early and act decisively.
