One Pancreas, Three Units: Necrotizing Pancreatitis from the Ambulance to the Vent

A seven-slide case: a 23-year-old with RLQ pain and shock who turns out to have necrotizing pancreatitis. Every pearl checks out. What it leaves out is the stuff that kills these patients on day 3 and day 30.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · October 2026. Original post: @scope_education_ (Advanced Medical Education; author not named on the post). This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the account.

Verdict: accurate throughout. Lactated Ringer's over saline, moderate rather than aggressive fluids, necrosis not meaning infection, lung-protective ventilation, and ARDS without pneumonia all match current guidelines and trials. Three things a reader would want next are missing: abdominal compartment syndrome, the timeline for infected necrosis and intervention, and the etiology workup that changes management in a young obese patient. One minor production note: the slide footers are numbered out of 8 while the carousel holds 7, and the order is shuffled, so slide "1/8" isn't in the post.

The case arc

Same patient, three teams, three questions.

EMS · first look
Recognize hypoperfusion early

Tachycardic, tachypneic, pale, cool, clammy, soft pressures. The post's pearl: shock is a perfusion problem, not a blood-pressure problem.

CV adds: the skin and the respiratory rate told the story before the cuff did.

ED · differential + fluids
Treat the physiology, find the cause

RLQ pain plus shock: perforation, ischemia, infection, hemorrhage, inflammatory catastrophe. Don't anchor on appendicitis. LR, reassess, avoid overload. CT shows necrotizing pancreatitis.

CV adds: a lipase and a triglyceride level reorganize this differential within an hour.

MICU · pressors + lungs
Optimize perfusion, anticipate failure

Norepinephrine for vasodilatory shock, trend perfusion markers, re-examine the shock type. ARDS: 6 mL/kg PBW, plateau ≤30, prone when indicated.

CV adds: measure bladder pressure. The "obstructive" box on slide 6 has a pancreatitis-specific answer.

The fluid slide, with numbers

"Moderately aggressive" now has a trial behind it.

WATERFALL (NEJM 2022, 18 centers, LR in both arms)AggressiveModerate
Protocol20 mL/kg bolus, then 3 mL/kg/h10 mL/kg bolus only if hypovolemic, then 1.5 mL/kg/h
Moderately severe or severe pancreatitis22.1%17.3%
Fluid overload20.5%6.3%
Median hospital stay6 days5 days

The trial was stopped at the first interim analysis (249 of a planned 744 patients) for the safety difference. Both arms were reassessed at 12, 24, 48 and 72 hours, so "moderate" meant goal-directed, not fixed. One caveat for this case: WATERFALL excluded patients who already had organ failure or shock at enrollment, so it informs the early ED phase better than the shocked patient on slide 4. Once a patient is in shock, the dynamic responsiveness-and-tolerance approach the post recommends is the right frame.

04

What's missing: abdominal compartment syndrome

The organ failure that pancreatitis and fluids create together.

Necrotizing pancreatitis puts inflammatory fluid in the retroperitoneum and gut wall, causes ileus and ascites, and then gets liters of crystalloid. Intra-abdominal pressure climbs. The result looks like several things on the post's slides at once: rising pressor need, falling urine output, worsening oxygenation with high plateau pressures. The post asks the reader to reassess shock mechanism and lists "obstructive"; in this disease, the bladder pressure is how you check.

WSACS 2013 definitionThreshold
Intra-abdominal hypertensionSustained or repeated IAP ≥12 mmHg
Abdominal compartment syndromeSustained IAP >20 mmHg with new organ dysfunction
Escalation, least invasive first

Nasogastric and rectal decompression, adequate sedation and analgesia, a fluid-neutral or negative strategy once resuscitated, avoiding tight dressings and head-of-bed angles that raise pressure, percutaneous drainage of large ascites, neuromuscular blockade, and decompressive laparotomy as the last step. A bladder pressure in a ventilated patient with severe pancreatitis is a cheap measurement with a big payoff.

Obesity matters twice for this patient: it raises baseline intra-abdominal pressure, and it stiffens the chest wall, which can make a plateau pressure look worse than the lung's actual stress.

What's missing: etiology and severity

Two questions that change the plan for this specific patient.

Why is a 23-year-old obese man's pancreas on fire?

Gallstones and alcohol are the common answers. In a young obese patient, check triglycerides early: hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis (typically TG >1,000 mg/dL) changes management, and it can travel with undiagnosed diabetes or DKA. Also check calcium, a right upper quadrant ultrasound for gallstones, and a medication and alcohol history. ERCP is for cholangitis or persistent obstruction; ACG 2024 suggests medical therapy over early ERCP in biliary pancreatitis without cholangitis.

How sick, by definition?

Revised Atlanta 2012Criteria
MildNo organ failure, no local or systemic complications
Moderately severeTransient organ failure (<48 h) and/or local complications such as necrosis
SeverePersistent organ failure, >48 h

This patient has necrosis already, so he is at least moderately severe. Whether the shock resolves within 48 hours decides whether he is severe.

Feeding

Enteral nutrition over parenteral, and oral intake once tolerated. In PYTHON (NEJM 2014), early nasoenteric tube feeding within 24 hours was not better than on-demand feeding at 72 hours in predicted-severe pancreatitis, so the goal is enteral, not necessarily immediate.

07

The lungs, one refinement

The post's vent settings are right. One detail matters for this patient.

The ventilator on slide 7 shows a tidal volume of 420 mL, which is 6 mL/kg for a predicted body weight of about 70 kg, roughly a 5'9" man. That works only if he's 5'9". Predicted body weight comes from height and sex, never from actual weight, and obese patients are the ones most often over-ventilated by mistake. Prone positioning in PROSEVA was for P/F <150 on FiO2 ≥0.6 and PEEP ≥5, for at least 16 hours a day.

08

Archive flag

Found while checking this post against earlier CV material.

Board-prep differential tables (BIG DDx)

The acute pancreatitis rows read "Aggressive IV fluids (LR preferred)." Since WATERFALL and ACG 2024, that should read "moderately aggressive, goal-directed fluids, LR preferred; reassess at 12, 24, 48 and 72 hours." Older question banks may still reward "aggressive," so a note on both answers is worth keeping for COMLEX.
