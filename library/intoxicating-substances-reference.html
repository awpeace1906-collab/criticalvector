Drug Class

Stimulants

Agents that increase synaptic monoamine tone — dopamine, norepinephrine, and (variably) serotonin — producing a shared sympathomimetic toxidrome: tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, mydriasis, diaphoresis, and agitation. The route, the specific transporter pharmacology, and the co-released neurotransmitter burden determine which complications dominate.

Stimulant · Tropane Alkaloid

Cocaine

Benzoylmethylecgonine

CokeBlowSnowYayoYeyo Crack (freebase)RockSpeedball (+ opioid)Pink cocaine / Tusi (adulterated mix)
DEA Schedule II

Mechanism of Action

Cocaine is a competitive inhibitor of the presynaptic monoamine transporters — dopamine transporter (DAT), norepinephrine transporter (NET), and serotonin transporter (SERT) — blocking reuptake and flooding the synaptic cleft with dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin. The dopaminergic surge in the mesolimbic reward pathway (nucleus accumbens) drives euphoria and reinforcement; the noradrenergic surge drives the peripheral sympathomimetic toxidrome.

Cocaine is also a class Ic-like sodium-channel blocker (local anesthetic property, the basis of its historical surgical use), which underlies its proarrhythmic and pro-convulsant potential — wide-complex dysrhythmias mimic tricyclic antidepressant overdose. Chronic use produces coronary vasospasm and accelerated atherosclerosis via endothelin-1 upregulation, platelet activation, and impaired nitric-oxide-mediated vasodilation, independent of any acute overdose event.[1,2]

Urine drug screen: cocaine immunoassays target benzoylecgonine specifically — there is essentially no cross-reactive substance in modern assays, so a positive result is almost never a true false positive. That reliability does not extend to attributing every symptom in front of you to the positive result.[4]

Symptomology

Classic sympathomimetic triad: tachycardia, hypertension, and mydriasis, layered with diaphoresis, agitation, and — in heavy or binge use — paranoia and formication-type tactile hallucinations. Chest pain and palpitations are common presenting complaints even without infarction.

Pathognomonic Clue

Painful retiform purpura with a predilection for the ears, cheeks, and nose, occurring alongside agranulocytosis/neutropenia and high-titer dual p-ANCA/c-ANCA positivity, is essentially diagnostic of levamisole-adulterated cocaine toxicity — an adulterant present in up to 70–80% of the current US supply. Seeing bilateral ear necrosis in a young patient should trigger this diagnosis before an autoimmune vasculitis workup.[7]

Cocaine-induced midline destructive lesions (CIMDL) — septal perforation with palatal/nasal cartilage destruction from chronic insufflation — mimic granulomatosis with polyangiitis on exam and imaging, but lack the pulmonary-renal involvement of true GPA.

At the Bedside — Front Line

  • Benzodiazepines are first-line for agitation, hypertension, tachycardia, and seizure prophylaxis — they address the shared adrenergic driver.
  • Active external cooling for hyperthermia; treat as excited delirium physiology, not infectious fever — antipyretics do not work and are not indicated.
  • Chest pain: treat as possible ACS until proven otherwise. ECG, troponin, aspirin if no contraindication, nitroglycerin/calcium-channel blockers for vasospasm.
  • Avoid unopposed beta-blockade in the acutely intoxicated patient — pure beta-blockade can leave alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed, worsening hypertension and coronary vasospasm. If beta-blockade is needed for rate control, a combined alpha/beta agent (labetalol) is the accepted approach per AHA/ACC guidance, after benzodiazepines and vasodilator therapy.[5,6]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Wide QRS or ventricular dysrhythmia → sodium bicarbonate (treat as sodium-channel blockade, akin to TCA overdose); avoid lidocaine and class Ia/Ic antiarrhythmics, which are synergistically pro-convulsant/pro-arrhythmic.
  • Refractory agitation/excited delirium → ketamine with airway readiness; ICU maintenance with dexmedetomidine to prevent recrudescent agitation.
  • Rhabdomyolysis, DIC, and AKI are downstream of uncontrolled hyperthermia and muscular hyperactivity — aggressive IV fluids and early cooling prevent the cascade rather than treating it after the fact.
  • Aortic dissection and cardiomyopathy are on the differential for any cocaine-associated chest pain or heart failure presentation — imaging per usual AD/heart-failure pathways once ACS is excluded.[6]
Practice Shift

Believe the positive UDS, but don't anchor on it. The route of use predicts the complication — always ask how the patient uses. And once acute intoxication resolves, don't reflexively withhold guideline-indicated beta-blocker therapy (e.g., HFrEF, prior MI) on the basis of remote cocaine history alone.

Stimulant · Phenethylamine

Amphetamine / Dextroamphetamine

Mixed amphetamine salts (Adderall), lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse, a prodrug)

AddiesDexiesUppersStudy drugSpeedBennies
DEA Schedule II (Rx) — widely diverted/misused

Mechanism of Action

Amphetamine is primarily a transporter-mediated releaser, not just a reuptake blocker. It is taken up into the presynaptic terminal via DAT and NET, then reverses their normal direction of transport (via interaction with VMAT2 and phosphorylation of the transporters), causing dopamine and norepinephrine to be released into the synapse independent of neuronal firing. It also inhibits monoamine oxidase (MAO) at higher concentrations, further prolonging the extracellular monoamine surge.[1]

Lisdexamfetamine is inactive until cleaved by red-blood-cell peptidases to release dextroamphetamine, which blunts the abuse-deterrent kinetics somewhat (slower onset, harder to achieve an IV/insufflation rush) compared to immediate-release salts.

Pharmacokinetics

Oral bioavailability is high with peak plasma levels around 3 hours (immediate-release) to 7 hours (extended-release/lisdexamfetamine). Elimination half-life is 10–13 hours in adults and is pH-dependent — acidic urine accelerates renal elimination, alkaline urine prolongs it. Misuse routes include oral supratherapeutic dosing, insufflation of crushed IR/ER tablets, and rarely IV injection of dissolved tablets (which carries embolic risk from tablet fillers/binders).

Symptomology

Mydriasis, tremor, agitation, hyperreflexia, combative or paranoid behavior, and — at high doses — hallucinations, delirium, and choreoathetoid movements. Insomnia and anorexia dominate at prescribed doses; the picture escalates toward the full sympathomimetic toxidrome only in supratherapeutic misuse.

No True Pathognomonic Sign

Amphetamine intoxication has no exam finding that distinguishes it from methamphetamine or other stimulant toxicity — the toxidrome, dosing thresholds, and management are shared across the class. The clinical distinguishing feature is context: a prescribed ADHD medication history, a crushed/insufflated tablet, or an adolescent/young-adult diversion pattern.

At the Bedside

  • Benzodiazepines first-line for agitation and sympathomimetic excess — same approach as cocaine and methamphetamine.
  • Cardiac monitoring; treat hypertension/tachycardia symptomatically rather than with a beta-blocker alone.
  • Activated charcoal reasonable if presenting early after a large oral ingestion and airway is protected.

Critical Care Escalation

  • Seizures and dysrhythmias take priority in severe overdose — benzodiazepines for seizures; sodium bicarbonate if QRS widening is present.
  • Management is otherwise supportive and mirrors the broader sympathomimetic toxidrome pathway — there is no specific antidote.[2]
Practice Shift

Prescription stimulant misuse is a legal-substance problem with an illegal-substance toxidrome. Screen for diversion in any patient with a new sympathomimetic presentation and an ADHD diagnosis — theirs, a sibling's, or a classmate's.

Stimulant · Phenethylamine

Methamphetamine

N-methylamphetamine

MethCrystalIceGlassTinaCrankTweak
DEA Schedule II

Mechanism of Action

Methamphetamine shares amphetamine's transporter-reversal mechanism (DAT/NET-mediated dopamine and norepinephrine release, with weaker serotonergic activity) but is more lipophilic, crosses the blood-brain barrier faster, and is a more potent CNS stimulant with greater neurotoxic potential. Chronic high-dose use depletes dopaminergic terminals and is associated with methamphetamine-induced psychosis and long-term neurocognitive deficits distinct from acute intoxication.[1,2]

Pharmacokinetics & Route

Oral onset in 2–4 hours; smoking, snorting, and injection produce effects within minutes. Elimination half-life is markedly longer than cocaine's — 6–15 hours (commonly cited as 11–12 hours) versus cocaine's ~1 hour — which is why methamphetamine intoxication and its downstream complications (agitation, psychosis) tend to be more prolonged and resource-intensive in the ED. Metabolized via CYP2D6 to active amphetamine plus inactive p-OH-amphetamine and norephedrine; renal excretion is enhanced by urinary acidification.[1]

Clinical Toxidrome

Sympathomimetic toxidrome across cardiovascular (tachycardia, hypertension, dysrhythmia), CNS (agitation, psychosis, seizures, intracranial hemorrhage), renal (rhabdomyolysis-driven AKI), and GI systems. >150,000 US ED visits annually are attributed to methamphetamine toxicity.[2]

Symptomology

Acute intoxication produces the standard sympathomimetic picture — tachycardia, hypertension, mydriasis, agitation, and psychosis — but methamphetamine's chronic-use stigmata are unusually distinctive and often visible before a history is even obtained.

Pathognomonic Clue

"Meth mouth" — rampant interproximal and buccal caries at the cementoenamel junction with a blackened, crumbling appearance, combined with severe xerostomia and bruxism/trismus — is a chronic-use finding specific enough that dental literature uses it as a recognition tool independent of history.[4]

Formication ("crank bugs," "meth mites") — the tactile hallucination of insects crawling under the skin — drives compulsive skin-picking with characteristic linear excoriations concentrated on the face, forearms, and lower legs. The combination of meth mouth plus formication-pattern excoriations in the same patient is highly specific for chronic methamphetamine use.[5]

At the Bedside

  • Benzodiazepines remain first-line for agitation, hypertension, and seizure prevention; titrate to effect, doses are often higher than typical due to tolerance.
  • Active cooling for hyperthermia; IV fluids for volume status and to support renal perfusion given rhabdomyolysis risk.
  • Screen for heavy-metal contamination from illicit synthesis byproducts in atypical presentations.

Critical Care Escalation

  • Refractory agitation/excited delirium → consider ketamine; have airway management ready.
  • Severe oral ingestions: whole-bowel irrigation can be considered for body packers, though controlled outcome data is lacking.
  • Seizures and dysrhythmias managed per standard sympathomimetic-overdose algorithms; sodium bicarbonate for QRS widening.[3]
Practice Shift

Methamphetamine's long half-life means the agitation and hemodynamic instability you're managing at hour 1 may still be there at hour 8 — plan disposition and staffing accordingly rather than expecting a cocaine-length course.

Stimulant-Entactogen · Substituted Amphetamine

MDMA / MDA

3,4-Methylenedioxymethamphetamine / 3,4-Methylenedioxyamphetamine

MollyEcstasyXESassRolls
DEA Schedule I

Mechanism of Action

MDMA is a potent serotonin releaser (SERT-mediated, analogous to amphetamine's DAT/NET mechanism) with additional dopamine and norepinephrine release — roughly one-tenth the pure CNS stimulant potency of amphetamine but a much larger serotonergic signature. Direct 5-HT2A agonism contributes to the perceptual/empathogenic effects. MDMA also stimulates arginine vasopressin (ADH) release, promoting free-water retention — a mechanism with no real analog in cocaine or methamphetamine and the direct cause of MDMA's signature hyponatremia risk, especially compounded by compulsive water intake at events.[1,2]

A subset of users are CYP2D6 poor metabolizers (or co-ingest a CYP2D6 inhibitor, e.g., ritonavir), leading to markedly prolonged and intensified exposure and higher risk of severe toxicity.

Pharmacokinetics

Oral onset 20–60 min, peak ~2 hours, duration 4–6 hours; half-life ~8–9 hours for a single dose, but repeated dosing autoinhibits its own metabolism, prolonging and intensifying toxicity ("stacking"/"boofing" behavior at multi-day events).

Clinical Toxidrome

Mixed adrenergic-serotonergic picture: hypertension, tachycardia, mydriasis, diaphoresis, bruxism/trismus, plus hyperthermia and, when severe, frank serotonin syndrome (hyperthermia, clonus, hyperreflexia, altered mental status). The two dominant causes of MDMA death are hyperthermia and hyponatremia — not cardiac events, which distinguishes it from cocaine/methamphetamine mortality patterns.[1]

Symptomology

Bruxism and trismus (jaw clenching) are so consistent that users at raves and festivals often carry pacifiers or chewing gum specifically to manage it — a near-universal exam finding rather than an incidental one.

Pathognomonic Clue

The combination of hyperthermia and hyponatremia in the same patient — rather than either alone — is distinctive for MDMA/MDA among the stimulants: no other agent on this list produces both a heat-generating sympathomimetic state and an ADH-mediated free-water retention problem simultaneously. Spontaneous or inducible clonus in this setting confirms serotonin-toxicity physiology (Hunter Criteria) over simple sympathomimetic excess.[1,3]

At the Bedside

  • Benzodiazepines for agitation and as first-line serotonin-syndrome-adjacent sedation.
  • Active cooling — ice-water immersion for severe hyperthermia (>40°C); dantrolene has case-series support specifically in MDMA-related hyperpyrexia, particularly above 40–42°C, despite mechanistic uncertainty.[4]
  • Check serum sodium in any patient with altered mental status or seizure after MDMA — do not assume the presentation is purely sympathomimetic.

Critical Care Escalation

  • Seizure from acute symptomatic hyponatremia → 100 mL bolus of 3% hypertonic saline; correct no faster than 6–8 mEq/L per 24 hours to avoid osmotic demyelination.
  • Seizure not attributable to hyponatremia → benzodiazepines as usual.
  • Cyproheptadine (12 mg initial, then 2 mg q2h) as an adjunct for serotonin-syndrome features refractory to benzodiazepines and cooling.
  • ICU-level monitoring for severe hyperthermia given risk of DIC, rhabdomyolysis, and acute renal failure.[1,3]
Practice Shift

Two failure modes kill MDMA patients and they pull in opposite directions: treating everyone as "just dehydrated" and under-hydrating a hyperthermic patient, or treating everyone as "at risk of hyponatremia" and over-hydrating a normonatremic one. Check the sodium before you decide which patient you have.

Stimulant · Synthetic Cathinone (β-keto-amphetamine)

Synthetic Cathinones

Mephedrone, MDPV, methylone, alpha-PVP ("flakka"), and related designer analogs

Bath saltsPlant foodFlakkaCloud nineIvory waveNot for human consumption
DEA-scheduled by analog; new derivatives outpace scheduling

Mechanism of Action

Synthetic cathinones are ring-substituted derivatives of cathinone, the natural psychoactive compound in khat (Catha edulis). Structurally they sit within the phenethylamine/amphetamine family and act on the same DAT/NET/SERT machinery, but individual compounds vary widely in selectivity: MDPV is a potent, highly selective DAT/NET reuptake inhibitor (cocaine-like, not a releaser) with essentially no direct serotonergic activity, while mephedrone behaves more like a non-selective releaser closer to MDMA. This heterogeneity — new analogs appear faster than they can be characterized or scheduled — is why there is no single "cathinone toxidrome," only a family of related sympathomimetic-plus-serotonergic pictures.[1,2]

Clinical Toxidrome

Tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, mydriasis, diaphoresis, agitation, and — disproportionately compared to cocaine/methamphetamine — severe agitated psychosis, which is frequently the presenting complaint and the main driver of ED resource use. Deaths have been reported, generally from hyperthermia, seizure, or cardiovascular collapse in the setting of prolonged agitation.[3]

Routine immunoassay urine drug screens frequently do not detect synthetic cathinones; confirmation requires send-out testing to a forensic/reference lab, which is too slow to inform acute management. Clinical suspicion (young patient, new-onset psychosis/agitated delirium, negative standard tox screen) should drive treatment, not a lab result.

Symptomology

The presenting picture is the standard sympathomimetic toxidrome, but skewed heavily toward the psychiatric end of the spectrum — agitation and psychosis are frequently the chief complaint, out of proportion to the cardiovascular findings, and tend to be more prolonged than a comparable cocaine presentation.

No Single Pathognomonic Sign

No individual exam finding is unique to synthetic cathinones — the class is defined by structural heterogeneity (MDPV behaves like cocaine, mephedrone behaves more like MDMA), so the toxidrome varies by which analog was actually consumed. The strongest clinical clue is a mismatch between severe, prolonged agitated psychosis and a negative standard urine immunoassay — the assay simply doesn't detect this class, so a "clean" screen in a floridly psychotic young patient should raise suspicion rather than lower it.[4]

At the Bedside

  • Supportive care is the core of management — there is no antidote and no established standard protocol; treatment targets the sympathomimetic/psychotic syndrome, not a specific compound.
  • Benzodiazepines and antipsychotics (e.g., olanzapine) together, titrated aggressively, are the most consistently reported successful approach across case series.
  • Aggressive cooling for hyperthermia.
  • Support renal perfusion with IV fluids given high rates of rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI in this group.[1,4]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Refractory agitation with escalating sedation requirements is common — plan early for airway control if benzodiazepine/antipsychotic doses climb without effect.
  • A high proportion of synthetic cathinone presentations in early case series required inpatient admission — have a low threshold to admit for monitoring, especially with any organ dysfunction.
Practice Shift

"Not for human consumption" on the packaging is the tell, not a disclaimer. If a young patient presents with new agitated psychosis and a negative standard urine tox screen, synthetic cathinones belong high on your differential — treat the syndrome you see, not the lab you don't have yet.

Stimulant · Piperidine Derivative

Methylphenidate

Ritalin (IR), Concerta/Metadate/Quillivant (ER), dexmethylphenidate (Focalin)

RitalinVitamin RKiddie cokeSkittlesR-ball
DEA Schedule II (Rx) — abuse via oral, insufflated, and IV routes

Mechanism of Action

Unlike amphetamine, methylphenidate is a pure reuptake inhibitor — it blocks DAT (and to a lesser extent NET) without reversing transporter direction, so it does not directly trigger vesicular dopamine release the way amphetamine does. Functionally, in overdose the net result is the same excessive-extracellular-dopamine/norepinephrine picture, just reached by a different transporter mechanism — which is why its overdose toxidrome is described as clinically indistinguishable from amphetamine intoxication.[1,2]

Crushed tablets used intranasally or intravenously introduce insoluble tablet fillers (talc, cellulose) that cause pulmonary/retinal microembolization and, with repeated IV misuse, has been reported to precipitate multi-organ failure.[3]

Pharmacokinetics & Formulations

Immediate-release: onset ~20–30 min, duration 3–4 hours, dosed 2–3×/day. Extended-release (osmotic-pump Concerta, biphasic Metadate CD, liquid Quillivant XR, transdermal patch): duration 8–12 hours, once-daily dosing. Formulation matters clinically — a crushed ER tablet can dump a full day's dose at once, and osmotic-pump "ghost tablets" may appear intact in stool despite full drug release.

Clinical Toxidrome

Agitation, hallucinations, psychosis (can mimic cocaine- or amphetamine-induced psychosis, particularly with chronic insufflation "runs"), lethargy paradoxically at very high doses, seizures, tachycardia, dysrhythmias, hypertension, and hyperthermia. Effects are dose-dependent; death has been reported but is rare.[2]

Symptomology

The overdose picture is indistinguishable on exam from amphetamine toxicity — the same sympathomimetic and neuropsychiatric findings apply, and there is no clinical sign specific to methylphenidate over other stimulants of this class.

Clinically Distinctive, Not Pathognomonic

One formulation-specific clue is worth knowing rather than a true toxicity sign: the osmotic-release (OROS) shell of Concerta-type extended-release tablets can pass intact in the stool after the drug has been fully released, alarming caregivers who find a "whole pill" — this reflects normal pharmacokinetics of that delivery system, not treatment failure or non-absorption, and should not be mistaken for an unabsorbed, retrievable overdose.

At the Bedside

  • Management is largely supportive, centered on benzodiazepines to interrupt the sympathomimetic syndrome — mirrors amphetamine overdose management directly.
  • Consider activated charcoal for large, recent oral ingestions with a protected airway.
  • For pediatric unintentional ingestions (the majority of ADHD-medication poison-center calls), risk stratify by formulation and time since ingestion — ER products carry a longer observation window.[4]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Seizures and dysrhythmias managed per standard stimulant-overdose pathways.
  • IV/insufflation misuse presenting with embolic phenomena (pulmonary infiltrates, retinal findings) or multi-organ dysfunction warrants ICU-level workup beyond simple overdose management.
Practice Shift

A "prescribed" stimulant is not a "safe" stimulant once it's crushed and insufflated. Ask about route, not just substance — the same pill bottle can represent a therapeutic dose or an IV drug of abuse depending on how it was taken.

Stimulant · Methylxanthine

Caffeine

1,3,7-Trimethylxanthine

Energy drinksPre-workoutNo-DozCaffeine powder/pills
Unscheduled — legal, OTC, ubiquitous

Mechanism of Action

Caffeine is a nonselective antagonist of adenosine A1 and A2A receptors, blocking adenosine's normal inhibitory/sedating tone on the CNS and cardiovascular system — the opposite mechanistic family from the monoamine-transporter stimulants above. At toxic doses it additionally inhibits phosphodiesterase (raising intracellular cAMP) and promotes catecholamine release from the adrenal medulla and sympathetic terminals, which is what produces a sympathomimetic-like toxidrome despite the fundamentally different primary target.[1]

Caffeine clearance varies up to 40-fold between individuals based on CYP1A2 genotype; pregnancy (third trimester) triples the half-life, and oral contraceptives roughly double it — the same nominal dose is not equally risky across patients.[1,2]

Clinical Toxidrome

Tachycardia, hypertension progressing to hypotension in severe cases, tremor, anxiety, GI upset, and — at toxic serum levels — seizures and cardiac dysrhythmias, which are the dominant causes of fatality. Rhabdomyolysis, significant electrolyte disturbance (notably hypokalemia from catecholamine-driven intracellular potassium shift), and aspiration from airway compromise contribute to morbidity in severe overdose.[1,3]

Symptomology

Tremor, anxiety, GI upset, and tachycardia dominate mild-moderate toxicity; there is no skin, mucosal, or dental finding characteristic of caffeine the way there is for cocaine or methamphetamine — caffeine toxicity is a biochemical diagnosis more than an exam diagnosis.

Closest Thing to a Signature Finding

Marked hypokalemia out of proportion to any GI losses, in a patient with tremor and tachycardia and no history of another sympathomimetic, is the closest caffeine comes to a distinguishing lab pattern — it reflects catecholamine-driven intracellular potassium shift rather than true total-body depletion, and over-correction with IV potassium risks rebound hyperkalemia as catecholamine excess resolves.

At the Bedside

  • Supportive care and oral hydration suffice for minor ingestions.
  • Activated charcoal if presenting early after a large ingestion, with airway protected.
  • Esmolol is first-line for tachydysrhythmia — beta-blockade is not contraindicated here the way it is a concern in cocaine toxicity, since caffeine's mechanism doesn't leave an unopposed-alpha problem.
  • IV fluids and correct hypokalemia for more significant toxicity.[1]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Benzodiazepines for seizures.
  • Refractory, supralethal ingestions have been successfully managed with combined lipid emulsion therapy and hemodialysis in case reports — consider early nephrology/toxicology consultation for massive intentional overdoses.[4]
  • Any intentional caffeine overdose warrants psychiatric evaluation prior to discharge.
Practice Shift

Caffeine is the one stimulant on this list where beta-blockade isn't a trap — esmolol is the actual first-line agent for its tachydysrhythmias. Don't extrapolate the "avoid unopposed beta-blockade" rule from cocaine onto every stimulant reflexively.

Drug Class

Opioids

Agents acting at the mu-opioid receptor to produce analgesia, euphoria, and dose-dependent respiratory depression. The shared toxidrome — miosis, respiratory depression, CNS depression — is remarkably consistent across the class; what differs is potency, receptor kinetics, half-life, and non-opioid mechanisms layered on top (serotonergic, sodium-channel, cardiac).

Opioid · Semisynthetic (Diacetylmorphine)

Heroin

Diacetylmorphine — prodrug rapidly deacetylated to 6-monoacetylmorphine then morphine

HSmackDopeHorseBlack tarChina white
DEA Schedule I

Mechanism of Action

Diacetylmorphine itself is largely inactive; its high lipophilicity allows rapid blood-brain-barrier penetration, after which it is deacetylated to 6-monoacetylmorphine (6-MAM, a potent, itself lipophilic metabolite) and then morphine. Both act as full agonists at the mu-opioid receptor, a Gi-coupled GPCR that inhibits adenylate cyclase, opens potassium channels, and closes voltage-gated calcium channels — the net effect is neuronal hyperpolarization and reduced neurotransmitter release, producing analgesia in the periaqueductal gray/spinal cord and euphoria via disinhibition of ventral tegmental dopamine neurons. Respiratory depression follows from mu-receptor-mediated blunting of the brainstem's response to hypercapnia.[1]

Pharmacokinetics & Route

IV/IM onset seconds-to-minutes; smoked/insufflated onset within minutes. 6-MAM is the only metabolite specific to heroin (vs. other morphine-derived opioids) and is the forensic/urine marker distinguishing heroin use from poppy-seed exposure or prescribed morphine, but it is detectable for only a few hours.

Symptomology

Classic opioid toxidrome: miosis, respiratory depression (rate and depth both reduced), CNS depression, and hyporeflexia.

Pathognomonic Clue

The triad of pinpoint pupils, depressed respiration, and depressed consciousness reversing promptly with naloxone is as close to a bedside-diagnostic sign as toxicology offers — response to the antidote confirms the diagnosis in real time. Track marks, skin-popping scars, and necrotic ulcers at injection sites support a chronic-use history; noncardiogenic pulmonary edema (heroin lung) shortly after use is a recognized, distinctive complication.[2]

At the Bedside

  • Bag-valve-mask ventilation first while preparing naloxone — respiratory support should not wait on the antidote.
  • Naloxone 0.04–0.4 mg IV/IM/IN titrated to adequate respiration (not full arousal) — the goal is breathing, not precipitating withdrawal.
  • Observe 6–12 hours post-reversal given naloxone's short half-life (30–80 min) relative to heroin/morphine's duration.[3]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Recurrent re-sedation despite bolus dosing → naloxone infusion (typically two-thirds of the effective bolus dose per hour).
  • Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema managed with supportive oxygenation/ventilation, not diuretics.
  • All patients with OUD, regardless of stated interest, should be offered take-home naloxone and a buprenorphine/OUD-treatment pathway before discharge.[3]
Practice Shift

Titrate naloxone to respiration, not wakefulness — over-reversal in an opioid-dependent patient buys you vomiting, aspiration, and a combative patient, not a better outcome.

Opioid · Synthetic Phenylpiperidine

Fentanyl & Analogs

Fentanyl, carfentanil, nitazenes (structurally distinct but functionally grouped here)

FentChina whiteApacheTango & CashBlues

Mechanism of Action

Full mu-opioid agonist, 50–100× more potent than morphine by weight, with extreme lipophilicity giving near-instant CNS penetration. Fentanyl and its analogs (carfentanil ~10,000× morphine potency, nitazenes with variable but often very high potency) share the same mu-receptor mechanism as heroin/morphine but bind with slower dissociation kinetics at high concentrations, which is the pharmacologic basis for concern about naloxone-resistant toxicity in mass-overdose events.[1]

Clinical Considerations

Onset is faster and duration often shorter than heroin per dose, but illicit fentanyl's extreme potency-per-milligram and inconsistent adulteration of the drug supply make dosing unpredictable; rapid-onset apnea without the gradual sedation seen with lower-potency opioids is characteristic. High-dose fentanyl (including rapid IV push in anesthesia) can cause fentanyl-induced chest wall rigidity ("wooden chest syndrome") severe enough to prevent bag-mask ventilation.

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

Sudden apnea/cyanosis without the typical progressive sedation curve, especially in a cluster of overdoses tied to one supply source, points to fentanyl or an analog rather than heroin alone. Chest wall rigidity refractory to bag-mask ventilation after rapid high-dose fentanyl exposure is distinctive enough that it changes the immediate resuscitation plan (see below).[2]

At the Bedside

  • Standard naloxone dosing is effective for fentanyl analogs per current animal and clinical data, contrary to earlier anecdotal concern — but a higher proportion of patients need a naloxone infusion rather than a single bolus.
  • Fentanyl test strips and harm-reduction supply checks are appropriate discharge counseling in any community with a contaminated drug supply.[3]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Wooden chest rigidity unresponsive to naloxone → neuromuscular blockade and intubation, since naloxone reversal may be too slow for an acutely obstructed airway.
  • Naloxone infusion titrated to respiratory rate/depth for recurrent re-sedation; observe well beyond naloxone's half-life given fentanyl's own variable duration.[3,4]
Practice Shift

Don't over-anchor on "naloxone won't work on fentanyl" — it does, at standard doses, in the overwhelming majority of cases. The real practice shift is infrastructure: plan for repeat dosing and infusions, not higher single-bolus doses.

Opioid · OUD Treatment Agents (Long-Acting)

Methadone & Buprenorphine

Methadone (full mu agonist); buprenorphine (partial mu agonist / kappa antagonist)

DollsDoneSubsBupeStrips
Methadone Schedule II; buprenorphine Schedule III

Mechanism of Action

Methadone is a full mu-opioid agonist with an additional NMDA-antagonist property (useful for opioid-tolerant pain and reducing withdrawal-related hyperalgesia) but also blocks the cardiac hERG potassium channel, prolonging the QT interval independent of opioid effect. Buprenorphine is a high-affinity partial mu agonist with a ceiling effect on respiratory depression and analgesia at higher doses, plus kappa-receptor antagonism and slow receptor dissociation — properties that make it safer in overdose but capable of precipitating withdrawal by displacing full agonists (heroin, fentanyl, methadone) from the receptor without providing equivalent activation.[1,2]

Pharmacokinetics

Methadone half-life 8–59 hours (highly variable, accumulates with repeat dosing) — a major reason methadone overdose is more dangerous and prolonged than its single-dose potency suggests. Buprenorphine sublingual bioavailability is high; its ceiling effect and slow dissociation give it a long duration of action (24+ hours) with a wide safety margin as monotherapy, though combination with other CNS depressants removes much of that margin.

Symptomology

Shared Toxidrome, Distinct Cardiac Signature

Both present with the standard opioid toxidrome. Methadone's distinguishing feature is QT prolongation and torsades risk disproportionate to its opioid effect — an ECG showing a long QT in an opioid-toxic patient should prompt methadone suspicion even without a confirmed history.

At the Bedside

  • Methadone overdose: naloxone titrated to respiration, with much higher cumulative doses and a naloxone infusion typically required given methadone's long half-life; obtain an ECG for QTc.
  • Buprenorphine-precipitated withdrawal in a patient on full agonists: supportive care, and — counterintuitively — continued/escalated buprenorphine dosing has case-report support for resolving precipitated withdrawal faster than stopping it.[3]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Torsades from methadone-associated QT prolongation: magnesium, overdrive pacing/isoproterenol as needed, correct electrolytes; avoid other QT-prolonging agents.
  • ICU-level buprenorphine (IV) has shown efficacy comparable to or exceeding titrated naloxone for methadone-induced respiratory depression in small studies, with less precipitated withdrawal and fewer intubations — an emerging alternative worth knowing even though naloxone remains first-line.[4]
Practice Shift

A patient in buprenorphine-precipitated withdrawal needs more buprenorphine, not less — the instinct to stop the "causative" drug is backwards here.

Opioid · Atypical / OTC Misuse

Tramadol & Loperamide

Tramadol (weak mu agonist + SNRI); loperamide (peripheral mu agonist, antidiarrheal)

UltramTrammiesImodium (loperamide OTC)
Tramadol Schedule IV; loperamide OTC/unscheduled

Mechanism of Action

Tramadol is a weak mu-opioid agonist whose analgesic effect depends heavily on its serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibition — a mechanism shared with SNRI antidepressants rather than classic opioids, which is why it can cause both seizures and serotonin syndrome independent of any co-ingested serotonergic agent.[1] Loperamide is a peripherally restricted mu agonist at therapeutic dose (excluded from the CNS by P-glycoprotein efflux), but at grossly supratherapeutic doses (misused for opioid-like euphoria or self-treatment of withdrawal) it saturates P-glycoprotein, crosses into the CNS, and also blocks cardiac hERG potassium and sodium channels — a dual-channel cardiotoxicity distinct from any other opioid on this list.[2]

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

Seizures in a patient with an otherwise mild opioid toxidrome is characteristic of tramadol — an opioid causing convulsions at or near therapeutic doses is atypical enough to point directly at this agent. For loperamide, QRS widening and QT prolongation with torsades or ventricular tachycardia in a patient using antidiarrheal medication at absurd doses (case reports document >100 mg/day, sometimes hundreds of tablets) is the signature finding — a mechanism no other opioid on this list produces.[3,4]

At the Bedside

  • Tramadol seizures: benzodiazepines; naloxone helps the opioid component but will not address the SNRI-mediated seizure/serotonin-syndrome risk.
  • Loperamide cardiotoxicity: continuous cardiac monitoring, correct electrolytes (especially potassium/magnesium), and treat as you would any sodium-channel-blocker/QT-prolonging overdose.

Critical Care Escalation

  • Sodium bicarbonate for loperamide-associated QRS widening; magnesium and overdrive pacing for torsades.
  • Serotonin syndrome from tramadol: benzodiazepines first-line, cyproheptadine for refractory cases, discontinue all serotonergic co-medications.[1]
Practice Shift

"It's just an over-the-counter antidiarrheal" is exactly the assumption that delays recognizing loperamide cardiotoxicity — ask about dose, not just drug name.

Drug Class

Sedative-Hypnotics

GABAergic and GABA-adjacent agents used for anxiety, insomnia, and sedation, sharing a common CNS-depressant toxidrome but differing sharply in safety margin, antidote availability, and withdrawal severity.

Sedative-Hypnotic · GABA-A Positive Allosteric Modulator

Benzodiazepines

Alprazolam, diazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam, and related agents

BenzosXanniesBarsBricksDowners
DEA Schedule IV

Mechanism of Action

Positive allosteric modulators at the GABA-A receptor — they don't open the chloride channel directly but increase the frequency of channel opening in the presence of GABA, enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission throughout the CNS. This is mechanistically distinct from barbiturates (which increase channel duration and can activate the channel independent of GABA at high dose) — the reason benzodiazepines have a ceiling effect on respiratory depression as monotherapy while barbiturates do not.[1]

Clinical Toxidrome

Sedation, ataxia, slurred speech, and — in isolated overdose — rarely progresses to significant respiratory depression or cardiovascular instability; the ceiling effect makes isolated benzodiazepine overdose one of the safer intentional overdoses in toxicology. Co-ingestion with alcohol, opioids, or other CNS depressants removes that safety margin and is the actual driver of most fatal benzodiazepine-involved overdoses.[2]

Symptomology

No Distinguishing Sign — the Absence of Findings Is the Clue

Deep coma, hyperreflexia/clonus, cardiovascular instability, or respiratory failure requiring ventilation in a suspected isolated benzodiazepine overdose should prompt a search for a co-ingestant (ethanol, opioids, TCAs) rather than confirming benzodiazepine severity — that clinical picture is not what benzodiazepines alone produce.[2]

At the Bedside

  • Supportive care — airway positioning, monitoring — is sufficient for the overwhelming majority of cases.
  • GI decontamination is not routinely indicated; the risk of aspiration in a sedated patient outweighs any benefit.

Critical Care Escalation

  • Flumazenil is a specific antidote but is relatively contraindicated in unknown/mixed overdose, chronic benzodiazepine use, seizure history, or QRS prolongation — it can precipitate seizures and life-threatening withdrawal, and a meta-analysis found significantly more serious adverse events with flumazenil than placebo (NNH = 50). Reserve for isolated, benzodiazepine-naive, iatrogenic reversal scenarios, and consult poison control first.[3,4]
  • Intubate for airway protection if truly compromised — this is far more common with co-ingestants than with benzodiazepines alone.
Practice Shift

Reach for flumazenil far less often than intuition suggests. In real-world mixed overdoses — which is most of them — the antidote causes more harm than the drug it's reversing.

Sedative-Hypnotic · GABA-A Agonist

Barbiturates & Z-Drugs

Phenobarbital, secobarbital (barbiturates); zolpidem, zaleplon, eszopiclone (Z-drugs)

Mechanism of Action

Barbiturates increase the duration of GABA-A chloride channel opening and, at high concentration, can activate the channel directly without GABA present — unlike benzodiazepines' GABA-dependent modulation, this gives barbiturates no ceiling effect and a narrow therapeutic index, making overdose far more dangerous. Z-drugs were designed to act more selectively at the alpha-1 GABA-A subunit (favoring sedation over the anxiolytic/muscle-relaxant effects of benzodiazepines) and behave clinically like low-toxicity benzodiazepines as monotherapy.[1]

Clinical Toxidrome

Barbiturate overdose: dose-dependent progression from sedation to respiratory failure, hypotension, and cardiogenic shock — potentially fatal even in isolated ingestion, unlike benzodiazepines. Z-drug overdose: sedation is the dominant finding; coma, respiratory depression, and cardiovascular instability are rare as monotherapy, mirroring benzodiazepines' relative safety.[1]

At the Bedside

  • Supportive care is the mainstay for both; GI decontamination is not routinely indicated for either.
  • Flumazenil may be cautiously considered for isolated Z-drug overdose (it does cross-react at the GABA-A benzodiazepine site) but carries the same seizure/withdrawal caveats as in benzodiazepine overdose — it has no effect on barbiturates at all, since they act at a separate site on the receptor.[2]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Severe barbiturate toxicity: airway/ventilatory support, vasopressors for cardiogenic shock, and — for phenobarbital specifically — urinary alkalinization plus multi-dose activated charcoal to enhance elimination (phenobarbital undergoes enterohepatic recirculation and has a pH-dependent renal clearance, unlike most other sedative-hypnotics).
  • Extracorporeal removal (hemodialysis/hemoperfusion) can be considered in massive, refractory barbiturate overdose.
Practice Shift

Don't lump barbiturates in with the "benzos are basically safe" mental model — the no-ceiling-effect mechanism makes isolated barbiturate overdose genuinely lethal in a way isolated benzodiazepine overdose rarely is.

GHB / GBL / 1,4-Butanediol

GLiquid ecstasyGeorgia home boyFantasyGoop

Mechanism of Action

GHB is an endogenous CNS metabolite of GABA that acts at two distinct sites: a specific, excitatory GHB receptor, and — at the higher concentrations reached with exogenous use — as a weak GABA-B receptor agonist, which is the inhibitory action responsible for its sedative-hypnotic and euphoric-disinhibiting effects. GBL and 1,4-BD are not active themselves but are rapidly converted to GHB by lactonase and alcohol/aldehyde dehydrogenase respectively, functioning as prodrugs with the same downstream toxicity.[1,2]

Pharmacokinetics

Extremely short half-life (~27–30 minutes), which produces a narrow, unpredictable dose-response curve — the difference between an intoxicating dose and a coma-inducing dose is small, and effects can end almost as abruptly as they began.

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

Abrupt-onset coma with unpredictable, rapid spontaneous recovery within a few hours — a patient who is deeply unresponsive one hour and abruptly awake, agitated, and trying to self-extubate the next — is the classic GHB presentation and distinguishes it from virtually every other sedative-hypnotic on this list, which have more gradual onset/offset. This kinetic signature is often more diagnostic than any lab test, since GHB is not detected on routine screens.[3]

At the Bedside

  • Supportive care — there is no specific antidote. Airway support and mechanical ventilation may be required for severe intoxication.
  • Bradycardia responds to atropine; hypotension usually responds to IV fluids.
  • Consider naloxone if opioid co-ingestion is suspected, but recognize it will not affect GHB itself.[4]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Because of the abrupt-awakening pattern, sedation is often unnecessary during mechanical ventilation — plan for possible sudden extubation attempts.
  • GHB withdrawal is a distinct, life-threatening emergency (unlike acute intoxication) — it can progress rapidly to severe agitation, autonomic instability, and delirium resembling severe alcohol withdrawal, sometimes within hours of last use. High-dose benzodiazepines are first-line; refractory cases have used barbiturates, baclofen, propofol, or dexmedetomidine, and case reports describe thiopental-induced burst-suppression coma for treatment-resistant withdrawal.[5,6]
Practice Shift

Don't mistake GHB withdrawal for a milder cousin of alcohol withdrawal — its onset is faster, it can be just as lethal, and standard benzodiazepine dosing often needs to go higher and faster than for alcohol withdrawal protocols.

Dissociatives

NMDA-receptor antagonists producing a characteristic detachment from surroundings and self — analgesia, altered perception, and, at high dose, catatonia or coma — distinct from the serotonergic hallucinogens despite superficial overlap in street use.

Dissociative · NMDA Receptor Antagonist

Ketamine & Phencyclidine (PCP)

Arylcyclohexylamines

Special KKVitamin KAngel dust (PCP)WetEmbalming fluid
Ketamine Schedule III; PCP Schedule II

Mechanism of Action

Both are non-competitive antagonists at the NMDA glutamate receptor, blocking the channel pore in an open-channel, use-dependent manner. This produces functional disconnection between the cortex and limbic/thalamic structures — the "dissociative" state — along with analgesia (ketamine's clinical anesthetic use) and, at high concentration, catalepsy. PCP additionally has dopaminergic and sigma-receptor activity contributing to its more prominent and prolonged psychotomimetic effects compared to ketamine.[1]

Clinical Toxidrome

Nystagmus (classically vertical or rotatory, a distinguishing feature from most other intoxications), dissociation, catatonia, hypertension, tachycardia, and — especially with PCP — violent, unpredictable agitation. Overdose can produce coma; death is rare from either agent alone but co-ingestion risk (CNS depressants) compounds respiratory risk.

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

Vertical or rotary nystagmus combined with a dissociative/catatonic state is distinctive for this class among recreational drugs — most other intoxications produce horizontal nystagmus if any. The emergence reaction — bizarre behavior, vivid dreams, derealization, or frank psychosis appearing specifically during the recovery phase rather than at peak intoxication — is a named, recognized phenomenon unique to ketamine/PCP recovery.[1] Chronic ketamine use also produces a distinctive ulcerative cystitis with dysuria, frequency, and hematuria not seen with other dissociatives or stimulants.

At the Bedside

  • Quiet, low-stimulation environment reduces emergence-reaction severity; benzodiazepines for agitation or significant emergence phenomena.
  • Supportive care for hypertension/tachycardia; these are usually self-limited.

Critical Care Escalation

  • Severe agitation refractory to benzodiazepines may require antipsychotics or, rarely, sedation/airway control for safety.
  • Evaluate any chronic ketamine user with dysuria/hematuria for ketamine-associated cystitis rather than assuming a routine UTI.[1]
Practice Shift

Vertical nystagmus in an agitated patient is your fastest bedside clue to this class — check for it before reaching for a broad differential.

Dissociative · OTC Antitussive / Inhaled Anesthetic Gas

Dextromethorphan & Nitrous Oxide

Mechanism of Action

DXM is an NMDA-receptor antagonist structurally related to opioids (a dextrorotatory morphinan) but with negligible mu-opioid activity at typical misuse doses — its dissociative effects come from the same NMDA-blockade mechanism as ketamine/PCP. It also inhibits serotonin reuptake, creating meaningful serotonin-syndrome risk, especially in combination with MAOIs or other serotonergic agents — a risk not shared by ketamine or PCP.[1] Nitrous oxide's mechanism is distinct entirely: it is a weak NMDA antagonist and GABA-A modulator producing rapid-onset, rapid-offset dissociation and euphoria, but its most clinically important effect is irreversible oxidation of the cobalt ion in vitamin B12, inactivating it as a coenzyme and producing a functional B12 deficiency with chronic use.[2,3]

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

For nitrous oxide: subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord — ascending paresthesias, gait ataxia, and absent reflexes progressing over days-to-weeks in an otherwise healthy young person — in the setting of a normal-to-borderline serum B12 (because it's the functional cobalamin activity, not the serum level, that's impaired) should trigger suspicion for chronic nitrous use specifically; standard toxicology screens do not detect it.[3,4] DXM has no comparably distinctive sign — its dissociative "plateaus" are a dose-dependent continuum shared conceptually with ketamine.

At the Bedside

  • DXM: supportive care, benzodiazepines for agitation; screen for serotonin syndrome, especially with co-ingested SSRIs/MAOIs.
  • Acute nitrous intoxication: typically resolves with removal from exposure and supportive observation; assess for hypoxia if used in a closed space (asphyxiation risk from displacing oxygen).

Critical Care Escalation

  • Suspected nitrous-induced subacute combined degeneration: high-dose IV vitamin B12 replacement started empirically without waiting for confirmatory testing, given the risk of irreversible neurologic injury with delay; MRI spine may show cord signal change but a normal MRI does not exclude the diagnosis.[4]
  • DXM serotonin syndrome: benzodiazepines, cyproheptadine for refractory cases, discontinue serotonergic co-medications.
Practice Shift

A normal serum B12 does not rule out nitrous-induced cord disease — treat the clinical syndrome, and don't let a "normal" lab talk you out of starting B12 while you wait on further workup.

Hallucinogens / Psychedelics

Serotonin 2A receptor agonists producing perceptual distortion and altered consciousness. Classic psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, mescaline) have a strong safety record for acute medical toxicity; designer analogs sold as substitutes (NBOMe) do not.

Hallucinogen · Serotonergic (Classic Psychedelics)

LSD, Psilocybin & Mescaline

Lysergic acid diethylamide; psilocybin/psilocin; mescaline

AcidTabsShroomsMagic mushroomsPeyoteSan Pedro
DEA Schedule I (psilocybin decriminalized/legal in some US jurisdictions)

Mechanism of Action

All three act primarily as agonists at the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor, with LSD showing additional affinity at 5-HT1A/2C and dopamine receptors. 5-HT2A activation in cortical layer V pyramidal neurons is thought to underlie the characteristic perceptual and cognitive distortions, via altered thalamocortical gating and reduced precision-weighting of prior beliefs (the basis of current "REBUS" models of psychedelic action).[1,2]

Clinical Considerations

The acute medical toxicity of classic psychedelics at typical recreational doses is low — no documented LSD fatalities from direct pharmacologic toxicity exist in the literature, and psilocybin's safety margin is similarly wide. The overwhelming majority of ED presentations relate to acute anxiety/panic ("bad trip"), trauma from impaired judgment, or (rarely) exacerbation of underlying psychiatric illness rather than organ toxicity. True serotonin toxicity is uncommon with these agents alone but has been reported, particularly with LSD combined with serotonergic antidepressants.[3]

At the Bedside

  • "Talk-down" in a calm, low-stimulation environment is first-line for acute anxiety/panic reactions and resolves most presentations without medication.
  • Benzodiazepines for significant agitation or anxiety that doesn't respond to reassurance and environment.
  • Screen for trauma and co-ingestants; the psychedelic itself is rarely the source of any objective abnormal vital signs.

Critical Care Escalation

  • True serotonin toxicity (rare with these agents alone): benzodiazepines, cyproheptadine for refractory cases, discontinue serotonergic co-medications.
  • Persistent psychosis or prolonged perceptual disturbance beyond the expected duration warrants psychiatric evaluation and consideration of an alternative diagnosis (underlying psychiatric illness unmasked, or a misrepresented/adulterated substance such as NBOMe).
Practice Shift

Most of what brings these patients to the ED is the environment and the fear, not the pharmacology — a quiet room and reassurance outperforms a medication cabinet for the majority of presentations.

NBOMe Compounds

25I-NBOMe, 25C-NBOMe, and related N-benzyl phenethylamines

N-BombSmiles25ISold falsely as "acid"

Mechanism of Action

NBOMe compounds are full agonists at 5-HT2A with far greater receptor affinity than LSD, developed originally as research radioligands rather than intended recreational drugs. Critically, they are frequently sold on blotter paper marketed and mistaken as LSD — but their mechanism produces a substantially more dangerous, stimulant-heavy toxidrome layered on top of the hallucinogenic effect, unlike true LSD.[1]

Clinical Toxidrome

Tachycardia (62%), hypertension (52%), hallucinations (81%), agitation, seizures, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, and — in a meaningful minority — death. A literature review of 42 published cases found 9 fatalities, in sharp contrast to LSD's essentially zero-fatality record from direct toxicity.[2]

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

A patient who believes they took "acid" (LSD) but presents with seizures, hyperthermia, severe hypertension, and rhabdomyolysis almost certainly did not — this severity profile is essentially diagnostic of NBOMe misrepresented as LSD, since true LSD toxicity this severe is exceptionally rare. Fatal cases have also shown toxic leukoencephalopathy on imaging in survivors with severe or prolonged exposure.[3,4]

At the Bedside

  • Benzodiazepines aggressively for agitation, seizures, and hypertension/tachycardia — this is functionally a stimulant-hallucinogen hybrid toxidrome, not a "just wait it out" bad trip.
  • Active cooling for hyperthermia; IV fluids for rhabdomyolysis prevention/treatment.

Critical Care Escalation

  • Status epilepticus, severe hyperthermia, and multi-organ failure have been fatal even with aggressive care in reported cases — treat any suspected NBOMe presentation as a serious toxicologic emergency from the outset, not a routine hallucinogen visit.[3]
Practice Shift

"Bad acid trip" with seizures and hyperthermia is a red flag, not a description — real LSD essentially never does this. Treat it as NBOMe until proven otherwise.

Cannabinoids

CB1/CB2 receptor agonists spanning a legal, low-acute-toxicity plant product and a family of synthetic analogs with a substantially more dangerous and unpredictable profile.

Cannabinoid · Phytocannabinoid

Cannabis / THC

WeedPotMarijuanaEdiblesDabs (concentrate)

Mechanism of Action

THC is a partial agonist at CB1 receptors (dense in the CNS, especially basal ganglia, cerebellum, and hippocampus) and CB2 receptors (predominantly peripheral/immune). CB1 activation inhibits presynaptic neurotransmitter release across multiple systems, producing the euphoric, anxiolytic-at-low-dose/anxiogenic-at-high-dose, and appetite-stimulating effects. Modern concentrate products (dabs, vape carts) can exceed 70-90% THC by weight, versus roughly 10-25% in traditional flower — a potency shift responsible for much of the rise in acute ED presentations.

Clinical Toxidrome

Tachycardia, conjunctival injection, dry mouth, impaired coordination, anxiety/panic at high dose, and — with edibles specifically — delayed, unpredictable onset (30-90+ minutes) that drives accidental re-dosing and disproportionately severe presentations in naive users, especially children.

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome — cyclical, severe nausea and vomiting in a chronic heavy user, classically relieved by compulsive hot showers or baths — is distinctive enough that the hot-water-seeking behavior itself is considered a diagnostic clue, despite marijuana's well-known antiemetic reputation making this paradoxical presentation easy to miss for years.[1,2]

At the Bedside

  • Acute intoxication/anxiety: reassurance, low-stimulation environment, benzodiazepines for severe agitation.
  • Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome: capsaicin cream applied topically to the abdomen is a reasonable first-line adjunct despite limited high-quality trial evidence; supportive antiemetics and IV fluids; counsel on cessation as the only definitive treatment.[3]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Pediatric edible ingestion with significant CNS depression: supportive airway management and observation; symptoms can be prolonged relative to inhaled exposure given delayed, variable absorption.
  • CHS refractory to standard antiemetics/capsaicin may need haloperidol or benzodiazepines as adjuncts per emerging case series.
Practice Shift

Cyclical vomiting plus compulsive hot showers in a heavy cannabis user is CHS until proven otherwise — it can take years and an extensive, expensive workup to reach this diagnosis if nobody asks about cannabis use directly.

Mechanism of Action

Unlike THC's partial agonism, most synthetic cannabinoids are full agonists at CB1 with far higher receptor affinity — a mechanistic difference that translates directly into greater toxicity, longer duration, and a much higher seizure/psychosis rate than natural cannabis, independent of dose comparisons.[1]

Clinical Toxidrome

Tachycardia and seizures are the most common findings across systematic review; third-generation compounds carry a higher rate of neuropsychiatric symptoms including psychosis. Standard cannabinoid immunoassays do not detect most synthetic cannabinoids, which is a major reason for delayed recognition.[1]

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

Unexplained coagulopathy with bleeding (hematuria, epistaxis, GI bleeding, bruising disproportionate to injury) in a synthetic cannabinoid user is diagnostic of brodifacoum (superwarfarin rodenticide) contamination — a 2018 multistate outbreak traced hundreds of cases to tainted product supply. At the Bedside

  • Benzodiazepines for agitation/seizures; supportive care is otherwise the mainstay.
  • Check coagulation studies (INR/PT) in any synthetic cannabinoid user with bleeding symptoms, even minor ones.

  • Confirmed or suspected brodifacoum coagulopathy: high-dose oral and/or IV vitamin K1, with 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate and/or fresh-frozen plasma for active severe bleeding. "Fake weed" with unexplained bleeding is a public-health-outbreak pattern, not a coincidence — think brodifacoum, not just synthetic cannabinoid toxicity itself, and expect a long vitamin K taper, not a single dose.

Inhalants

A heterogeneous group of volatile substances abused by inhalation for rapid-onset intoxication — organic solvents, aerosols/gases, and alkyl nitrites — unified by route rather than a shared receptor mechanism.

Inhalant · Volatile Solvents & Alkyl Nitrites

Volatile Solvents & Nitrites

Toluene, butane, propane (solvents/aerosols); isobutyl/amyl nitrite (poppers)

HuffingSniffingBaggingPoppersRushWhippets
Legal, unscheduled household/industrial products

Mechanism of Action

Volatile solvents (toluene and related hydrocarbons) are CNS depressants by a nonspecific mechanism resembling inhaled anesthetics — membrane fluidization and enhancement of GABA-A/glycine receptor function — but their most dangerous property is direct cardiac toxicity: sensitization of the myocardium to circulating catecholamines, producing a substrate for sudden fatal ventricular dysrhythmia. Alkyl nitrites work by an entirely different mechanism — they are potent oxidizing agents that convert hemoglobin's ferrous (Fe2+) iron to the ferric (Fe3+) state, producing methemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen.[1,2]

Clinical Toxidrome

Solvents: euphoria, hallucinations, headache, dizziness progressing to ataxia, seizures, and coma with heavy exposure; chronic use causes metabolic acidosis, renal tubular acidosis, and hepatic/renal impairment. Nitrites: headache, syncope, and — the hallmark — cyanosis and hypoxemia that does not correct with supplemental oxygen, reflecting methemoglobinemia rather than true hypoxia.[3]

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

"Sudden sniffing death" — cardiac arrest from ventricular fibrillation occurring during or shortly after a single inhalation session in an otherwise healthy young person, often precipitated by a sudden catecholamine surge (e.g., being startled and running) — is a recognized, distinctive fatal syndrome specific to volatile solvent abuse. For nitrites: cyanosis with a normal pulse oximetry reading that doesn't match the clinical picture, or a chocolate-brown arterial blood sample, is classic for methemoglobinemia — pulse oximeters cannot distinguish methemoglobin from normal hemoglobin and will overestimate true oxygen saturation.[4,5]

At the Bedside

  • Remove from exposure, provide supportive oxygen, and monitor closely for dysrhythmia with continuous cardiac monitoring — avoid catecholamine-driven stimuli (exertion, fright) given the myocardial sensitization risk.
  • Suspected methemoglobinemia: send a co-oximetry panel (standard ABG/pulse ox will not reliably detect it) and treat symptomatic patients.

Critical Care Escalation

  • Ventricular dysrhythmia from solvent-sensitized myocardium: avoid epinephrine/catecholamines if possible; beta-blockade has been used to blunt the sensitized response in refractory cases.
  • Symptomatic methemoglobinemia: methylene blue 1-2 mg/kg IV over several minutes, repeatable if needed — check for G6PD deficiency first when feasible, since methylene blue can precipitate hemolysis in G6PD-deficient patients.[5]
Practice Shift

A normal-looking pulse oximeter reading does not clear methemoglobinemia — if the clinical cyanosis doesn't match the number on the monitor, trust the patient and send a co-oximetry gas.

Anticholinergics

Competitive antagonists at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, spanning an OTC sleep aid/antihistamine and a naturally occurring, highly toxic plant.

Anticholinergic · Muscarinic Antagonist

Diphenhydramine & Jimsonweed (Datura)

Diphenhydramine (first-generation antihistamine); atropine/scopolamine (Datura stramonium alkaloids)

BenadrylSominexJimsonweedLoco weedDevil's trumpetMoon flower
Diphenhydramine OTC/unscheduled; jimsonweed legal (wild plant)

Mechanism of Action

Competitive antagonism of acetylcholine at central and peripheral muscarinic receptors, blocking parasympathetic tone throughout the body. Diphenhydramine has the added complication of being a sodium-channel blocker at high dose — a mechanism entirely separate from its anticholinergic activity and the reason its overdose can produce QRS widening and TCA-like cardiotoxicity, unlike pure anticholinergics such as atropine or scopolamine.[1,2]

Clinical Toxidrome

The classic anticholinergic (more precisely antimuscarinic) toxidrome is captured by the mnemonic: hot as a hare (hyperthermia), blind as a bat (mydriasis, blurred vision), mad as a hatter (delirium, often with mumbling speech and repetitive "picking" behaviors), red as a beet (flushed skin), dry as a bone (dry mucous membranes/axillae), and full as a flask (urinary retention) — plus tachycardia and decreased bowel sounds.[3,4]

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

The full six-feature mnemonic constellation together — dry, flushed, hot skin; mydriasis; urinary retention; absent bowel sounds; and a mumbling, "picking" delirium — is genuinely distinctive for this toxidrome as a whole, even though no single feature is unique in isolation. Hallucinations described as Lilliputian (seeing tiny people) or Alice-in-Wonderland-type distortions of size are classically associated with anticholinergic delirium specifically.[5]

At the Bedside

  • Benzodiazepines for agitation and seizures; evaporative cooling for hyperthermia.
  • Activated charcoal within one hour of ingestion is reasonable given anticholinergic-delayed gastric emptying, which extends the window somewhat beyond the usual one-hour rule.
  • Obtain an ECG and monitor QRS duration in any diphenhydramine ingestion — the anticholinergic and sodium-channel-blocking effects require separate assessment.[1]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Wide QRS from diphenhydramine's sodium-channel blockade: sodium bicarbonate, as in TCA overdose.
  • Physostigmine (acetylcholinesterase inhibitor) can rapidly reverse the central delirium in a pure antimuscarinic poisoning and may avoid unnecessary intubation/imaging — but it is contraindicated or should be used only with toxicology guidance when a sodium-channel blocker (diphenhydramine) or another mixed-mechanism agent is involved, since it can precipitate bradycardia, seizures, or asystole in that setting. Benzodiazepines remain the default first-line for undifferentiated or mixed presentations.[6,7]
Practice Shift

Diphenhydramine is not a "pure" anticholinergic — its sodium-channel blockade means physostigmine, the classic antidote taught for this toxidrome, is often the wrong move for this specific, most common cause of it.

Alcohol & Toxic Alcohols

Ethanol is the most widely used and normalized intoxicant on this entire reference; methanol and ethylene glycol share ethanol's chemistry (metabolized by the same enzyme) but their metabolites, not the parent compounds, are what kill.

Alcohol · Simple Alcohol

Ethanol

Ethyl alcohol

BoozeLiquorBeerWine
Legal for adults 21+ in the US; unscheduled

Mechanism of Action

Ethanol potentiates GABA-A receptor function and inhibits NMDA glutamate receptors — a dual mechanism (enhancing inhibition, blocking excitation) that explains both its acute sedative/disinhibiting effects and the severity of withdrawal, which represents unmasked NMDA-receptor hyperexcitability and reduced GABA tone once the drug is removed after chronic adaptation.[1]

Clinical Toxidrome

Dose-dependent CNS depression from disinhibition through ataxia, slurred speech, and — at high blood alcohol concentrations — respiratory depression, aspiration risk, and coma. Chronic use produces tolerance that shifts the dose-response curve, meaning a "normal" blood alcohol level in a naive user can be lethal while a dependent user may be conversant at levels that would incapacitate others.

Symptomology

Withdrawal Is the Distinctive Emergency, Not Intoxication

Acute intoxication has no distinguishing exam finding beyond the odor and standard CNS-depressant picture. Withdrawal, however, has a distinctive, staged natural history worth knowing cold: tremulousness at 6-12 hours, withdrawal seizures at 12-48 hours, alcoholic hallucinosis (clear sensorium with isolated hallucinations) at 12-24 hours, and delirium tremens — autonomic storm plus true delirium — at 2-7 days after the last drink, occurring in only 3-5% of hospitalized withdrawal patients but carrying meaningful mortality if unrecognized.[2,3]

At the Bedside

  • Acute intoxication: airway protection, IV fluids, glucose-containing fluids for hypoglycemia (common in binge drinkers), and thiamine — give thiamine before or with glucose, not after, given the theoretical risk of precipitating Wernicke's encephalopathy in a thiamine-deficient patient receiving glucose alone.
  • Withdrawal: use a validated scale (CIWA-Ar) to guide symptom-triggered benzodiazepine dosing, titrating to comfortable sedation rather than a fixed schedule.[4]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Suspected Wernicke's encephalopathy (nystagmus, ataxia, confusion): high-dose IV thiamine 500 mg q8h for 72 hours, not the standard 100 mg prophylactic dose.
  • Severe/refractory alcohol withdrawal or delirium tremens: escalating benzodiazepine doses; phenobarbital is a validated alternative/adjunct for benzodiazepine-refractory cases.[5]
Practice Shift

The emergency isn't usually the drunk patient in front of you — it's the withdrawal that hasn't started yet. Ask about last drink and typical daily intake on every alcohol-involved visit, not just the ones who look intoxicated.

Toxic Alcohol · Alcohol Dehydrogenase Substrates

Methanol & Ethylene Glycol

Methanol (windshield washer fluid, bootleg liquor); ethylene glycol (antifreeze)

AntifreezeWood alcoholMoonshine (methanol contamination)
Legal industrial products; toxicity is from ingestion, accidental or intentional

Mechanism of Action

Both are relatively nontoxic in their parent form — the danger comes entirely from their metabolism by alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), the same enzyme that metabolizes ethanol. Methanol is converted to formaldehyde and then formic acid, which is directly toxic to the optic nerve and retina. Ethylene glycol is converted through a cascade to glycolic acid (the main driver of the severe anion-gap metabolic acidosis) and then oxalic acid, which precipitates as calcium oxalate crystals in the renal tubules, causing acute kidney injury.[1,2]

Clinical Toxidrome

Both present initially with an ethanol-like inebriation (sometimes minimal), followed after a latent period — as the parent compound is metabolized into its toxic acid — by severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. Methanol's distinguishing late feature is visual disturbance progressing to blindness; ethylene glycol's is acute kidney injury.

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

For methanol: "snowstorm" or blurred vision progressing to complete blindness, with fundoscopic exam showing optic disc hyperemia/edema, is the hallmark finding pointing directly at formic-acid-mediated optic neurotoxicity. For ethylene glycol: calcium oxalate crystals in the urine (envelope-shaped or needle-shaped) combined with hypocalcemia and an elevated anion gap in a patient with a nontoxic or absent blood alcohol level is close to diagnostic — and a fluorescent antifreeze dye under a Wood's lamp on skin/clothing/vomitus is an old but real supporting clue.[3,4]

At the Bedside

  • Any unexplained high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis with an elevated osmolar gap should trigger empiric treatment before confirmatory levels return — these labs are frequently sent-out and slow.
  • Sodium bicarbonate for severe acidosis, recognizing that alkalinizing the urine in ethylene glycol poisoning can paradoxically promote calcium oxalate crystal precipitation and worsen renal injury — treat the systemic acidosis, not the urine pH, as the goal.[5]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Fomepizole (competitive ADH inhibitor) is first-line antidotal therapy for both, blocking further toxic metabolite formation — give empirically for a documented recent ingestion with an osmolar gap >10, or for strong suspicion plus at least two of: pH <7.3, bicarbonate <20 mEq/L, osmolar gap >10, or urinary oxalate crystals (AACT criteria), rather than waiting for a confirmatory level that may take days.[6]
  • Hemodialysis is indicated for severe acidosis, visual symptoms (methanol), renal impairment (ethylene glycol), or very high measured levels — dialysis clears both the parent compound and toxic metabolites and is the EXTRIP-guideline standard for severe cases.[6,7]
  • If fomepizole is unavailable, IV ethanol can competitively saturate ADH as a bridge therapy, though it requires careful serum-level-guided titration and carries its own CNS-depressant and hypoglycemic risk.
Practice Shift

Don't wait for the send-out toxic alcohol level to start treatment — an elevated osmolar gap plus anion gap acidosis in the right clinical context is enough to start fomepizole today, not in two days when the confirmatory result arrives.

Nicotine

The most widely used addictive substance globally, now reformulated into high-concentration liquid form that has meaningfully changed the acute-toxicity picture for children and naive users.

Stimulant/Cholinergic · Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptor Agonist

Nicotine & E-Liquid

Nicotine; e-liquid also contains propylene glycol/vegetable glycerin as carrier

Vape juiceE-liquidJuul podsNic salts
Legal, age-restricted; e-liquid largely unregulated for concentration/purity

Mechanism of Action

Nicotine is an agonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors throughout the peripheral and central nervous system — both at ganglionic synapses (sympathetic and parasympathetic) and at the neuromuscular junction. This dual autonomic activation is why nicotine toxicity produces a classically biphasic picture: an initial stimulatory phase (sympathetic ganglion activation) followed by a later depressor phase as ganglionic transmission is overwhelmed and blocked (depolarizing blockade), flipping the clinical picture from hyperactivity to depression within the same overdose.[1]

Intoxication Considerations

Liquid nicotine concentration varies enormously across products (often 20-60 mg/mL, with some "nic salt" formulations higher), and ingested dose correlates poorly with resulting symptom severity across case reports, making risk stratification by history alone unreliable — any pediatric e-liquid ingestion should be treated as potentially serious regardless of the stated concentration.[2]

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

The biphasic syndrome — early nausea, hypertension, tachycardia, tremor, and possibly seizures, followed hours later by hypotension, bradycardia, weakness, and CNS/respiratory depression in the same patient — is the signature, well-described pattern of significant nicotine toxicity and should prompt extended observation even after apparent early improvement, since the second (depressor) phase can be the more dangerous one.[3]

At the Bedside

  • Supportive care is the mainstay; monitor through both phases rather than discharging after early symptom resolution.
  • Atropine for symptomatic bradycardia/excessive parasympathetic (muscarinic) symptoms during the depressor phase; vasopressor support for hypotension as needed.[4]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Respiratory failure from neuromuscular blockade (depolarizing block at high nicotine levels, analogous mechanistically to a depolarizing paralytic) may require ventilatory support until the receptor blockade resolves.
  • Seizures managed with benzodiazepines per usual protocols.
Practice Shift

Don't discharge a significant e-liquid ingestion just because the early stimulatory phase has settled down — the depressor phase can arrive later and be the more dangerous half of the same overdose.

Kratom

An herbal product occupying an ambiguous legal and pharmacologic middle ground — botanically unrelated to opium poppy but acting substantially through opioid receptors.

Opioid-Adjacent Herbal · Partial Mu Agonist

Kratom

Mechanism of Action

Mitragynine is a partial mu-opioid receptor agonist (with kappa/delta antagonism) using G-protein-biased signaling with limited beta-arrestin recruitment — theoretically less respiratory depression than classic full agonist opioids, though clinically significant respiratory depression is still reported, especially in combination with other CNS depressants. Low doses: stimulant-like energy and alertness. Higher doses: opioid-like sedation, analgesia, and — in overdose or with concentrated/adulterated products — respiratory depression, seizures, and rarely cardiac arrest (primarily reported with polydrug use).[3]

Pathognomonic Clue

Cholestatic hepatotoxicity — jaundice with markedly elevated bilirubin (case reports exceeding 20 mg/dL) developing over 1-8 weeks of regular use, generally reversible with cessation — is a distinctive pattern for kratom among the substances in this reference, most of which don't cause liver injury as a primary toxicity. Kratom does not appear on standard opioid immunoassays and can cause a false-negative standard opioid screen even in a patient with a genuinely opioid-like clinical picture.[3]

At the Bedside

  • Naloxone can reverse kratom-related respiratory depression given its mu-receptor mechanism, though response may be less predictable than with classic opioids.
  • Supportive care and benzodiazepines for seizures.
  • Ask specifically about kratom/herbal supplement use — a negative standard opioid screen does not rule it out and patients may not volunteer it as a "drug."[3]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Withdrawal resembles mild-to-moderate opioid withdrawal (myalgias, rhinorrhea, diaphoresis, insomnia, irritability) and can be scored/managed using the Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS); generally less severe than classic opioid withdrawal but real and can drive dependency.[3]
  • Hepatotoxicity workup and supportive management; usually reversible with abstinence, though monitor closely if bilirubin is markedly elevated.

A negative opioid screen does not clear kratom off your differential for an opioid-like presentation — the standard immunoassay simply doesn't see it.

Anabolic-Androgenic Steroids

Synthetic testosterone derivatives abused for muscle mass and athletic performance rather than acute intoxication — the harm profile here is chronic organ toxicity and sudden cardiac events rather than an overdose toxidrome.

Performance-Enhancing Hormone · Androgen Receptor Agonist

Anabolic-Androgenic Steroids

Testosterone esters, nandrolone, stanozolol, trenbolone, and related compounds

GearJuiceRoidsStackersCycle
DEA Schedule III

Mechanism of Action

Full agonists at the androgen receptor, driving increased protein synthesis and muscle hypertrophy (the anabolic effect) alongside masculinizing effects (the androgenic effect) that cannot be pharmacologically separated. 17-alpha-alkylated oral formulations (methandrostenolone, stanozolol, oxandrolone) resist first-pass hepatic metabolism to achieve oral bioavailability, but this same structural modification is directly responsible for their disproportionate hepatotoxicity compared to injectable, non-alkylated esters.[1,2]

Chronic Toxicity Profile

Cardiovascular: left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, dyslipidemia (reduced HDL), hypertension, prothrombotic state, and sudden cardiac death in young, otherwise healthy athletes/bodybuilders — a pattern increasingly documented on forensic autopsy series. Hepatic: cholestatic jaundice with 17-alpha-alkylated agents, and rare but serious peliosis hepatis (blood-filled hepatic cysts) or hepatocellular adenoma/carcinoma with prolonged use. Renal: focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and chronic kidney disease from direct toxicity, hypertension, and rhabdomyolysis related to intense training.[3,4,5]

Symptomology

Pathognomonic Clue

Peliosis hepatis — blood-filled cystic cavities within the liver, which can present with spontaneous hepatic rupture or subcapsular hematoma — is a distinctive, if rare, complication strongly associated with 17-alpha-alkylated AAS use and rarely seen from other causes in an otherwise healthy young adult. In a young bodybuilder-type patient, the combination of marked polycythemia (elevated hematocrit), significant left ventricular hypertrophy on echo without a hypertensive or valvular explanation, and gynecomastia together points strongly toward chronic AAS use even without a volunteered history.[4,6]

At the Bedside

  • This is not an acute-overdose toxidrome — presentations are typically related to a chronic complication (chest pain/arrhythmia, abdominal pain from hepatic pathology, or an incidental lab/imaging finding).
  • Ask directly and non-judgmentally about supplement and injectable use in any young patient with unexplained cardiomyopathy, hepatic lesions, or marked polycythemia — AAS are also frequently present, undeclared, in over-the-counter "supplements."[2]

Critical Care Escalation

  • Suspected peliosis hepatis with hemorrhage: this is a surgical/interventional radiology emergency — hemodynamic support and urgent imaging/consultation for possible embolization or surgical intervention.
  • Ventricular arrhythmia/sudden cardiac event in a young AAS user: standard ACLS, with cardiology workup for structural cardiomyopathy once stabilized.[5]
Practice Shift

Unexplained LVH, polycythemia, or a liver lesion in a young, muscular patient deserves a direct, nonjudgmental question about AAS and supplement use — this population underreports use to physicians far more than the prevalence data would suggest.
