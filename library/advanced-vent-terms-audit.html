Pendelluft, P-SILI, Viscoelasticity: The Advanced Vent Glossary, Checked

A seven-slide primer on ventilator physiology that cites its sources. The physics is right, the P-SILI slides argue both sides fairly, and the two viscoelasticity slides lean on a paper about something else.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · October 2026. Original post: @pulmtoilet (PulmToilet.com, The Peak Inspiration podcast), "Essential Ventilator Terms: Advanced Edition." This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the original creator.

Verdict: the pendelluft and P-SILI slides are accurate and well sourced, and presenting an "argument against" P-SILI is unusually honest for the format. The correction: both viscoelasticity slides cite PMID 17575096, which is Bates et al. 2007 on percolation in fibrosis and emphysema. It supports slide 5's network-stiffening points and its figure, but not the definition of viscoelasticity or anything on slide 6 about ARDS and inspiratory holds.

01

The terms, in one table

TermPlain definitionWhy it matters at the bedside
Time constantResistance × compliance: how fast a lung unit fills and empties.Units with different time constants fill out of step with each other.
PendelluftGas moving between lung regions within a breath, even with no flow at the airway opening.With strong patient effort, gas shifts from non-dependent to dependent lung, stretching dependent regions more than the tidal volume suggests. Shown in injured pig lungs with EIT and dynamic CT (Yoshida 2013): matching that dependent inflation under paralysis took nearly three times the driving pressure. Invisible on the ventilator; visible on electrical impedance tomography.
P-SILILung injury attributed to the patient's own vigorous inspiratory effort. Term proposed by Brochard, Slutsky and Pesenti in 2017, originally about spontaneously breathing, non-intubated patients with high respiratory drive.The same concern extends to ventilated patients: a strong drive on pressure support can generate large transpulmonary pressures while the airway pressure looks reassuring.
ViscoelasticityLung tissue behaves partly like a spring (stores energy) and partly like a damper (dissipates it), so its stiffness depends on time and speed of inflation.During an inspiratory hold, pressure falls from the first zero-flow value toward the plateau as tissue stress relaxes and gas redistributes, so hold length changes the plateau you read. Covered in Bates's Lung Mechanics, chapter 4 (tissue resistance and elastance).
PercolationA threshold effect: once damaged regions connect across a network, the whole network's behavior changes abruptly.Explains why lung function can stay near normal through early disease, then worsen quickly. The basis of slide 5's figure.

03

P-SILI: what the trials say

Slide 4 mentions neuromuscular blockade. Here are the two trials behind that.

TrialPopulationResult
ACURASYS (2010)340 patients, severe ARDS (P/F <150) within 48 h, cisatracurium vs placebo for 48 hAdjusted 90-day mortality HR 0.68 (0.48–0.98). Crude 90-day mortality 31.6% vs 40.7%, P = 0.08, not significant unadjusted
ROSE (2019)1,006 patients, moderate-to-severe ARDS, high-PEEP strategy in both arms, lighter sedation targets in control90-day mortality 42.5% vs 42.8%; stopped for futility. Paralyzed patients were less physically active and had more adverse cardiovascular events

The usual reading: when the control arm is lightly sedated on higher PEEP, routine early paralysis adds nothing, which fits Yoshida's argument that PEEP can make spontaneous effort safer. It also shows why slide 4's caution matters. Neither trial measured effort or pendelluft, so neither tests P-SILI directly. Measuring drive and effort at the bedside (airway occlusion pressure, P0.1, esophageal pressure) is the practical next step the slides point toward.

04

Next up: mechanical power

Slide 7's teaser, previewed.

Mechanical power is the energy delivered to the respiratory system per minute, combining tidal volume, driving pressure, PEEP, flow and rate in one number (Gattinoni et al., 2016). The slide's figure splits each breath into PEEP, elastic and resistive components. In Gattinoni's derivation, power rose with the square of tidal volume, driving pressure and flow, with rate to the power 1.4, and linearly with PEEP. The idea: no single setting injures the lung on its own, and rate is a multiplier people forget. The equation was validated against measured pressure-volume loops in 80 patients; no trial has yet established a safe threshold.
