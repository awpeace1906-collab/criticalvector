High-Dose Rocuronium & First-Pass Success

v1. What the new DEVICE/PREOXI secondary analysis actually shows, where it agrees and disagrees with prior registry data, and why "just push more" isn't a free decision. Verified against Am J Respir Crit Care Med, CJEM, Ann Emerg Med, and primary pharmacology literature. July 2026.

This is an association from observational/secondary analyses, not a randomized trial answer. The authors of the lead study say so explicitly and call for an RCT. Treat this as "reason to think harder," not "new standard of care."

1. The Question

The labeled intubating dose of rocuronium spans 0.6 to 1.2 mg/kg; with an ED95 of about 0.3 mg/kg, the usual RSI dose of 1.0 mg/kg is more than three times the ED95. Emergency and critical care literature has spent the better part of a decade circling the same question: does pushing more than that — 1.2 mg/kg and above — meaningfully raise the odds of a successful first attempt, or does it just prolong paralysis for no benefit?

Why this isn't trivial: first-attempt success matters. Failed first attempts are linked to more hypoxemia, more hemodynamic instability, and more esophageal intubations — the stakes of the dosing decision are real, not academic.

2. The New Data: DEVICE/PREOXI Secondary Analysis

April et al. pooled data from two large multicenter randomized trials — DEVICE (video vs. direct laryngoscopy) and PREOXI (preoxygenation strategy) — which together enrolled critically ill adults undergoing emergency tracheal intubation across 9 EDs and 24 ICUs. This wasn't a new trial; it was a propensity-matched secondary analysis of rocuronium dosing already captured in that pooled dataset.

The numbers: of 2,440 total trial participants, 1,822 received rocuronium — 720 (39.5%) at high dose (>1.2 mg/kg) and 1,102 (60.5%) at standard dose (≤1.2 mg/kg).

PopulationAdjusted relative risk of first-attempt success
Overall1.06 (95% CI 1.01–1.11) — high-dose favored, modestly
ED intubations only0.97 (95% CI 0.90–1.04) — no association
ICU intubations only1.12 (95% CI 1.05–1.19) — clear association

The finding that matters most isn't the headline number — it's the interaction. The benefit of high-dose rocuronium essentially disappeared in the ED subgroup and persisted in the ICU subgroup, with a statistically significant interaction (p=0.002). The overall "high-dose wins" headline obscures a split result underneath it.

The authors' own framing: this is a secondary analysis with real potential for unmeasured confounding — sicker or harder-airway patients may have been dosed differently for reasons the data can't capture — and they explicitly call for a randomized trial rather than presenting this as definitive.

3. What Came Before: Registry Evidence

The DEVICE/PREOXI analysis didn't emerge in a vacuum — it lands on top of a decade of ED-registry data pointing the same general direction, with one recurring wrinkle: the benefit tends to concentrate in direct laryngoscopy, not video laryngoscopy.

StudyFinding
NEAR registry (Levin et al., 2021)8,034 ED rocuronium intubations across 25 centers. First-attempt success climbed with dose: 88.4% (<1.0 mg/kg), 88.1% (1.0–1.1), 89.7% (1.2–1.3), 92.2% (≥1.4 mg/kg).
NEAR, device-stratifiedWith direct laryngoscopy, ≥1.4 mg/kg carried adjusted OR 1.9 (95% CI 1.3–2.7) for first-attempt success vs. the 1.0–1.1 mg/kg reference. With video laryngoscopy, first-attempt success was statistically similar across every dosing range — the dose-response relationship was a direct-laryngoscopy phenomenon.
Single-center retrospective, ED/ICU (2026)First-attempt success 100% with >1.2 mg/kg vs. 89.5% with ≤1.2 mg/kg (p<0.01), with fewer mean attempts (1 vs. 1.1).

Put the device-stratification finding next to the DEVICE/PREOXI ED-vs-ICU split and a pattern starts to emerge: the benefit of higher rocuronium dosing looks concentrated in situations without a camera doing some of the work for you — direct laryngoscopy in the NEAR data, ICU intubations (often without immediate video backup or by a wider range of operator experience) in the newer analysis. That's a hypothesis worth testing prospectively, not yet a proven mechanism.

4. The Tradeoff: Why More Isn't Free

"Go big" has a real cost, and it isn't hemodynamic — rocuronium is comparatively cardiostable. The cost is time: a bigger dose buys a modestly faster onset in exchange for a much longer paralysis.

Onset: 1.2 mg/kg shortens onset to roughly 45–60 seconds, only marginally faster than 0.9–1.0 mg/kg — one dose-finding study found no further improvement in intubating conditions at 60 seconds going from 0.9 to 1.2 mg/kg.

Recovery: the same study found time to spontaneous recovery was meaningfully prolonged after 1.2 mg/kg compared with 0.6 and 0.9 mg/kg — the relationship between post-tetanic count and return of the first twitch stretched out at the higher dose.

ConsiderationWhy it matters
Masked neuro examIn the ICU population where this analysis found its signal, a longer paralysis window is a longer window without a reliable neurologic exam in a critically ill patient — a real cost in exactly the population where the dosing benefit showed up.
Reversal cost and kineticsSugammadex reverses high-dose rocuronium effectively, but the required dose scales with the block depth — reversing a profound block from 1.2 mg/kg needs a larger sugammadex dose than reversing a lighter block, with a real cost difference, not just a kinetic one.
Interpatient variabilityElderly and hepatically impaired patients already run a prolonged duration of action at standard doses — stacking a high dose on top of reduced clearance compounds unpredictably.

None of this argues against high-dose rocuronium where it's indicated — it argues against treating "more is simply better" as a costless decision. The DEVICE/PREOXI analysis measured first-attempt success; it did not measure how long each patient sat paralyzed afterward, and that's not a trivial omission for an ICU population.

5. Practical Synthesis

In the ED, especially with video laryngoscopy available: the evidence for going above 1.2 mg/kg is weak-to-absent in this dataset. Standard dosing (1.0–1.2 mg/kg) is reasonable as a default.

In the ICU, or anywhere direct laryngoscopy is the primary tool: the signal for high-dose rocuronium improving first-attempt success is real across multiple analyses now — worth considering, especially in a predicted difficult airway, while accounting for the prolonged paralysis it buys you.

The honest summary a trainee should walk away with: this is genuinely unsettled, the ED/ICU split is the most clinically actionable detail in the whole story, and an RCT is the reasonable next step the authors themselves are calling for — not a reason to change practice unilaterally today.
