Neutropenic Fever Notes: Good Bones, Wrong PMIDs

Handwritten sticky-note notes on neutropenic fever for the ICU. The structure and most of the content hold up. Two of the three cited PMIDs are unrelated 1982 neuroscience papers, the third excludes neutropenic patients, and three clinical numbers need fixing.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · November 2026. Original post: @gallodemoraesmd, “Neutropenic Fever for the Intensivist” (9-slide carousel, #hemecritcare). This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the original creator.

Verdict: definitions, mortality, microbiology and the empiric gram-negative strategy are right. Corrections: fever affects 10–50% (not >50%) of solid tumor patients; antifungal therapy follows persistent fever at 4–7 days, not bacteremia; and the 7-day circulating neutrophil lifespan is wrong as stated. PMIDs 7121977 and 7120136 point to unrelated 1982 animal studies. The primary sources are IDSA 2010 and ASCO/IDSA 2018. Missing: antibiotics within 1 hour of triage.

Slide-by-slide check

Post saysVerdictNote
Fever: single oral temp ≥38.3 °C, or ≥38.0 °C for more than 1 hVerifiedMatches IDSA 2010. ESMO 2016 uses >38.3 °C or two readings >38.0 °C over 2 h. The caption says “more than 38.5”, which contradicts the slide; 38.3 is correct.
Neutropenia ANC <1500; severe <500; profound <100Needs contextThresholds right, but IDSA defines the treatable group as ANC <500 or expected to fall below 500 within 48 hours. Someone at 900 and falling on day 8 of a cycle qualifies.
Fever during neutropenia: >50% of solid tumor patients and >80% of heme malignancyCorrectionIDSA gives 10–50% for solid tumors (with at least one chemotherapy cycle) and >80% for hematologic malignancies. The solid tumor figure is overstated.
Most common etiology: gut and oral mucosal translocation; remember HSV and devices; drug and tumor fever are diagnoses of exclusionVerifiedConsistent with IDSA. Add C. difficile, typhlitis (neutropenic enterocolitis) and perirectal infection to the mucosal list.
10% in-hospital mortalityVerifiedKuderer 2006, 41,779 US admissions: 9.5% overall; 2.6% with no comorbidity, 10.3% with one, ≥21.4% with more than one.
Up to 25% have documented bacteremia; gram-positive most common, gram-negative higher mortalityVerifiedIDSA: bacteremia in 10–25%, mostly in prolonged or profound neutropenia. Gram-negative sepsis was an independent mortality predictor in Kuderer.
50% have no identified organismNeeds contextUnderstated. IDSA puts clinically documented infection at 20–30% of episodes, so most febrile neutropenic patients never get an organism.
Prolonged bacteremia ≥7 days = think fungalCorrectionThe trigger is persistent fever, not bacteremia. IDSA: empiric or pre-emptive antifungal therapy for high-risk patients still febrile after 4–7 days of broad-spectrum antibiotics with no source. Persistent bacteremia means a retained focus (line, abscess, endocarditis) or resistance.
Neutrophils last about 7 days in the circulationCorrectionClassic estimates of circulating half-life are hours. Pillay 2010 (in vivo deuterium labeling) reported 5.4 days and was contested by later modeling. What makes counts fall a week or two after chemo is the marrow production pipeline emptying, not circulating lifespan (see the figure).
Without neutrophils, local and systemic host response is compromised; no leukocyte-mediated tissue repairVerifiedTrue, with the bedside corollary: little pus, faint infiltrates, minimal pyuria. Fever may be the only sign.
Low risk: asymptomatic, good support, ANC >500, can return within 60 minNeeds contextIDSA and ASCO/IDSA define low risk by expected neutropenia ≤7 days and few comorbidities, or a validated score: MASCC ≥21, or CISNE for stable solid tumor patients. ANC >500 at presentation is not a criterion. Return within 1 hour is part of ASCO/IDSA outpatient eligibility.
High risk: sepsis, ANC ≤100 or neutropenia >7 days, suboptimal supportVerifiedMatches IDSA: anticipated prolonged (>7 days) profound (≤100) neutropenia, or significant comorbidity or instability. Every ICU patient is high risk by definition.
Blood cultures; sputum culture and PCR; imaging by symptomsNeeds contextCorrect, plus specifics: at least 2 blood culture sets, one from each lumen of any central line and one peripheral, before antibiotics if that causes no delay. Urine culture, CBC, CMP, LFTs. Chest imaging if respiratory signs.
Empiric antibiotics to cover gram-negatives and PseudomonasVerifiedAntipseudomonal β-lactam monotherapy (cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, meropenem). ASCO/IDSA: first dose within 1 hour of triage. Not on the slide, and the most time-critical item.
MRSA coverage if shock or severe sepsisNeeds contextShock is one indication. IDSA also lists suspected catheter infection, skin or soft tissue infection, pneumonia, known MRSA colonization and gram-positive cocci in blood cultures. Stop it at 48–72 h if no MRSA is found.
Fungal coverage if prolonged, profound neutropenia and septic shockNeeds contextReasonable for shock in a high-risk patient. The routine trigger is fever persisting 4–7 days; mold-active agents if pulmonary nodules or a positive galactomannan.
G-CSF: consult heme; no clinical impact, just shorter neutropeniaNeeds contextClose. Cochrane (14 RCTs, 1,553 patients): no overall mortality benefit (HR 0.74, 0.47–1.16), but fewer hospital stays over 10 days, faster neutrophil and fever recovery, and shorter antibiotic courses. Shorter stay is a clinical impact, even if survival isn’t.
03

Why the count falls when it does

Schematic of a typical myelosuppressive cycle, not drawn from patient data. Timing varies by regimen; induction for acute leukemia and stem cell transplant conditioning produce deeper, longer aplasia. The lag is set by marrow production time, not by how long a neutrophil lasts in the blood.
04

First hour in the ICU

1

Treat the fever as sepsis until proven otherwise

Exam findings will be muted. Look at the line sites, mouth, perianal area (inspect, no rectal exam), skin and lungs.

2

Cultures, then antibiotics within the hour

Two or more blood culture sets including each central line lumen. Then an antipseudomonal β-lactam. Don’t let cultures delay the first dose.

3

Add on for specific reasons

Vancomycin for shock, line or skin infection, pneumonia or MRSA history. Second gram-negative agent for shock or known resistant colonization. Antifungal for shock in prolonged aplasia, or persistent fever at 4–7 days.

4

Look for a source the usual tests miss

CT abdomen for typhlitis, CT chest for nodules, C. difficile testing, HSV of oral ulcers, line removal for tunnel infection or persistent bacteremia.

5

Bring hematology in early

Expected duration of neutropenia, G-CSF, transfusion thresholds and any planned chemotherapy all change the plan.
