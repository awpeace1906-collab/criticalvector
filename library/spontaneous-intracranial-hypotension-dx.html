Spontaneous Intracranial Hypotension: How the Diagnosis Is Made

ICHD-3 criteria, the SEEPS signs, the Bern score, what opening pressure can and can’t tell you, and how leaks are localized, from the primary literature.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · November 2026. Prompted by a @keyradiologist case carousel on SIH. This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the original creator.

Bottom line: SIH is a CSF volume problem from a spinal leak. ICHD-3 needs a headache temporally linked to low pressure (<60 mm CSF) or imaging evidence of a leak, with no recent dural puncture or trauma; the headache does not have to be orthostatic. Brain and whole-spine MRI come first. Brain MRI is normal in about 19% and opening pressure normal in about 32%, so neither excludes the diagnosis.

01

What SIH is

Spontaneous intracranial hypotension is a syndrome of CSF volume loss from a spinal leak, without a preceding procedure or trauma. “Hypotension” is a misnomer that has stuck: about a third of patients have a normal opening pressure. The problem is volume. The brain loses buoyancy and sags, and by the Monro–Kellie doctrine the venous system, dura and pituitary engorge to fill the space CSF has vacated.

The orthostatic pattern can fade with time. Long-standing cases often describe a headache that builds through the day, or one that is barely positional. Other symptoms that point toward a leak: muffled hearing or tinnitus, interscapular pain, nausea, and relief within minutes of lying flat.

Three details that matter

Orthostatic is not a criterion. ICHD-3 describes the headache as usually but not invariably orthostatic and explicitly says the postural feature cannot be relied on for diagnosis.

A dural puncture in the prior month excludes 7.2.3. That case is 7.2.1, post-dural puncture headache.

<60 mm CSF equals 6 cm H2O. Brain MRI evidence of intracranial hypotension counts as imaging evidence of leakage, which is why most patients reach the diagnosis without a lumbar puncture.

The criteria miss people. In one series of 85 patients with suspected leaks, 69 did not meet ICHD-3, yet many improved durably after blood patching; relief when lying flat before treatment predicted response. Treat the criteria as a floor for research classification, not a gate for treatment.

Brain MRI: SEEPS and the Bern score

SEEPS, the mnemonic popularized by Schievink, lists the five classic findings. Order of appearance in practice is roughly the reverse: pachymeningeal enhancement is the most common sign (73% pooled), subdural collections the least.

The score predicts a positive spinal study, not the diagnosis itself. It was derived in a single Bern cohort (56 leak-positive patients, 16 orthostatic headache without leak, 60 controls) and validated internally in 20 patients. A low score in a convincing history does not end the workup.

04

Lumbar puncture: rarely needed, often misleading

Opening pressure is normal in about a third of patients with SIH. In Kranz’s series of 106, only 34% had pressure ≤6 cm H2O; higher abdominal girth, longer symptom duration and a normal brain MRI all pushed the measurement up. An LP also adds a new dural hole. The UK consensus puts MRI brain and whole spine first and does not require an LP for diagnosis. Reserve it for excluding infection or malignancy when the enhancement pattern is atypical.

CSF-venous fistulas were rare in that 2016 series because the imaging techniques to find them were new. Decubitus CT myelography and digital subtraction myelography now find them far more often in centers that look, and they explain many cases once labeled “indeterminate.” Pooled data show extradural CSF on spinal imaging in 48–76% of patients.

06

Treatment, briefly

1

Conservative care

Bed rest, fluids, caffeine, analgesia. Effective in 28% pooled; reasonable for mild, recent cases but not a reason to delay a patch.

2

Non-targeted epidural blood patch

UK consensus first-line, as early as possible. A single patch succeeded in 64% pooled; larger volumes (>20 mL) did better than smaller (77% vs 66%).

3

Targeted therapy

After localization: CT-guided targeted patch, fibrin glue, surgical repair of a ventral tear or diverticulum, or transvenous embolization of a CSF-venous fistula.

4

Watch for rebound high pressure

Some patients develop a new headache that is worse lying down after a successful seal: rebound intracranial hypertension. It is a sign the leak closed, and it is usually treatable.

07

Five ways to miss it

  1. Normal brain MRI. One in five patients. Image the spine anyway if the story fits.
  2. Normal opening pressure. One in three. The disease is volume loss, not a number on a manometer.
  3. “Chiari I” with a sagging brainstem. Look at the mamillopontine distance and cisterns before anyone plans a decompression.
  4. “Pituitary adenoma” or “pachymeningitis.” Both are SEEPS findings. Smooth, diffuse dural enhancement with the rest of the pattern is a leak until shown otherwise.
  5. Bilateral subdurals in a young patient without trauma. Drained without sealing the leak, they recur and the brain sags further.
