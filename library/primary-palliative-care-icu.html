Primary Palliative Care in the ICU: The Conversations We Should All Be Able to Have

If you have Palliative Care built into your ICU team, you're lucky. But if you don't, or they're stretched thin, these are the skills that shouldn't wait for a consult.

Original post: definshock. This page is an independent third-party verification, not affiliated with or endorsed by the account.

Verdict: the distinction the post is drawing on — primary vs. specialty palliative care — is a real, established framework in the palliative medicine literature, and the argument that every intensivist needs baseline competency in it (not just consult-team access) is well-supported.
01

Primary vs. Specialty Palliative Care

LevelWho provides itScope
Primary palliative careAny treating clinician (intensivist, resident, bedside nurse)Basic symptom management, straightforward goals-of-care conversations, family communication skills — the baseline competency every ICU clinician should have
Specialty palliative carePalliative medicine-trained consultantsComplex or refractory symptom management, conflict resolution in high-stakes family disagreements, complicated grief, and cases where the primary team's own bandwidth or skill set is exceeded

This framework exists specifically because specialty palliative care is a limited resource relative to ICU demand — if every goals-of-care conversation and every symptom-management question required a consult, most patients would wait far longer than appropriate for basic, time-sensitive conversations that the primary team is fully capable of having.

02

The Core Primary Palliative Care Skills

  • Basic goals-of-care conversations. Eliciting what matters most to the patient, what they understand about their prognosis, and what tradeoffs they'd accept — frameworks like "Ask-Tell-Ask" and "NURSE" statements (Naming, Understanding, Respecting, Supporting, Exploring emotion) are teachable, structured communication tools, not innate talent.
  • Basic symptom management. Opioid titration for dyspnea and pain, first-line anxiolytics, and standard approaches to terminal secretions are within the scope of any ICU clinician, not exclusively the palliative team's domain.
  • Delivering serious news. Structured approaches (e.g., SPIKES protocol) for communicating a poor prognosis or a significant change in condition are learnable communication skills with a real evidence base behind them, distinct from "having a good bedside manner."
  • Recognizing when to escalate to specialty palliative care. Complex family conflict, refractory symptoms despite standard management, or a primary team that's reached the limit of its own capacity or comfort are the actual triggers for a specialty consult — not every death or every difficult conversation.
03

Bottom Line

The source post's framing reflects a well-established and important distinction in the palliative care literature: primary palliative care — basic communication and symptom-management skills — belongs to every ICU clinician, while specialty palliative care exists for the complex, refractory, or high-conflict cases that exceed the primary team's bandwidth or training. Building these skills in-house isn't a workaround for not having enough palliative staff, it's the appropriate baseline of care regardless of staffing.
