Waveform Anatomy & Physiology

The arterial pressure waveform is a beat-by-beat integration of cardiac function, vascular resistance, and volume status. Each labeled inflection point reflects a discrete physiologic event. Hernandez et al. (2022) emphasize that clinicians who reduce the waveform to mean arterial pressure (MAP) alone are discarding a continuous, information-dense hemodynamic signal.

Annotated Radial Arterial Waveform — Single Beat

Anacrotic Limb systole

Sharp upstroke from diastolic nadir to systolic peak. Slope reflects LV contractility (dP/dt) and aortic compliance. Steep = vigorous LV. Gradual = poor contractility or aortic stenosis (slow-rising "pulsus parvus et tardus").

Systolic Peak (SBP) systole

Maximum transmitted force from LV ejection into the aorta. Increases with higher stroke volume, faster heart rate, and reduced aortic compliance (stiff vessels → higher SBP). Site-dependent: radial SBP often exceeds aortic SBP by 10–20 mmHg.

Dicrotic Notch (Incisura) transition

Transient pressure deflection marking aortic valve closure. Position reveals SVR: proximal (high on downstroke) = high SVR/vasoconstiction. Low/absent = low SVR states (sepsis, vasodilation). Migrates distally with peripheral measurement (less distinct at radial vs. aortic root).

Dicrotic Wave

Small positive pressure wave immediately after the notch, generated by elastic recoil of the aortic wall (Windkessel effect). Accentuated with increased aortic compliance (young patients); blunted in atherosclerosis or hypovolemia.

Diastolic Run-off

Exponential decay from notch to the next systolic upstroke. Rate of decay reflects peripheral vascular resistance. Rapid run-off → low SVR (AR, AVM, sepsis, nitroprusside). Prolonged slow decay → high SVR or aortic stenosis.

Diastolic Nadir (DBP) end-diastole

Minimum pressure in the cycle. Maintained by SVR and heart rate (more cycles = less run-off time = higher DBP). Aortic regurgitation: DBP drops precipitously. Low DBP + low SVR: pharmacologic vasodilation or distributive shock.

Heart–Lung Interaction: PPV & SPV

Scheeren and Saugel (2018) identify PPV and SPV as the mechanistic foundation for predicting intraoperative hypotension and guiding goal-directed fluid therapy. The physiology is rooted in the Frank-Starling relationship under cyclic pleural pressure swings.

PPV — Respiratory Cycle Variation (Mechanically Ventilated)
Mechanism — Inspiration

Positive-pressure inspiration increases intrathoracic pressure → compresses pulmonary vasculature → transiently augments LV preload (brief Starling augmentation) → increases SV and PP.

In a preload-dependent patient on the steep portion of the Starling curve, this swing is exaggerated — hence high PPV = fluid responsive.

Mechanism — Expiration

Expiration withdraws this augmentation. In a volume-depleted patient, the PP drops substantially. Preload-independent patients (already on the flat of the Starling curve) show minimal PPV even at low volumes.

Scheeren & Saugel (2018): PPV-guided GDT reduced intraoperative hypotension and vasopressor requirements in elective surgery.

PPV Validity Conditions — Non-Negotiable

Required: (1) Controlled mechanical ventilation. (2) Regular sinus rhythm. (3) Tidal volume ≥8 mL/kg (ideally; PPV unreliable at Vt <6 mL/kg — lung-protective ventilation era caveat). (4) Closed chest. (5) Absence of right heart failure.

Invalidity contexts: spontaneous breathing, atrial fibrillation, ARDS (low Vt), open chest, abdominal compartment syndrome, severe RV dysfunction, very high PEEP. Use alternatives (PLR, echocardiography, end-expiratory occlusion test) when PPV is invalid.

Predicting Fluid Responsiveness

The conceptual backbone from Scheeren and Saugel (2018): only ~50% of hemodynamically unstable patients respond to a fluid bolus. Static parameters (CVP, PAOP) are poor predictors. Dynamic indices derived from the arterial waveform are the current standard — but each has conditions of validity.

Frank-Starling Conceptual Anchor

A patient on the steep portion of the Starling curve (preload-dependent): small ↑ in preload → large ↑ in SV. PPV/SPV swing is exaggerated. Fluid will increase CO.

A patient on the flat portion (preload-independent): ↑ preload → minimal ↑ in SV. PPV/SPV swing is small. Fluid will not help and may cause harm (VILI, fluid overload, abdominal compartment syndrome).

1
Check PPV/SVV validity CMV + sinus rhythm + Vt ≥8 mL/kg + closed chest + no severe RV dysfunction. If invalid → proceed to alternative tests.
2
Read the PPV threshold — mind the gray zone PPV ≥13% predicts fluid responsiveness. But Cannesson et al. (Anesthesiology 2011, n=413) demonstrated a gray zone of 9–13% where prediction is inconclusive — roughly one-quarter of OR patients fall here. The French multicenter ICU pooled analysis (Biais et al. 2014, n=556) found an even wider gray zone (4–17%, 62% of patients) with PPV AUC only 0.73. Inside the gray zone, do not trust PPV alone — go functional.
2b
Tidal volume challenge — rescue for the gray zone Transiently increase Vt from 8 to 12 mL/kg for 2 minutes and re-read PPV. An augmented PPV12 carries excellent discrimination (AUC 0.935; sensitivity 95%, specificity 72%). Useful precisely when baseline PPV at protective Vt is indeterminate.
3
Passive Leg Raise (PLR) test — when PPV invalid Autotransfusion of ~300 mL from lower extremities. The strongest functional test available: Monnet, Marik & Teboul (Intensive Care Med 2016, 21 studies, 991 patients) report pooled sensitivity 86%, specificity 92%, AUC 0.95 — independent of ventilation mode and cardiac rhythm. Best threshold: PLR-induced ↑CO ≥10±2%. Reversible, no fluid given, works in AF and spontaneous breathing.
3b
PLR — five rules, not a drop of fluid (Monnet & Teboul) (1) Start from semi-recumbent, not supine. (2) Measure CO/SV directly — not pulse pressure (PP-based PLR sensitivity falls to ~56%). (3) Use a real-time method that catches transient change — the effect vanishes within ~1 min. (4) Avoid pain/cough/discomfort that triggers adrenergic response. (5) Beware intra-abdominal hypertension: IAP >15–20 mmHg causes false negatives — lower the threshold to ~5%.
4
End-Expiratory Occlusion Test (EEOT) 15-second end-expiratory pause → increases preload. ↑PP or ↑CO ≥5% = fluid responsive. Requires tolerance of apnea — not practical in all patients.
5
Mini-fluid challenge 100 mL crystalloid bolus over 1 minute with real-time SV monitoring. ↑SV ≥6–10% = fluid responsive. Limits exposure in patients where harm is likely (oliguria, pulmonary edema).

Intraoperative Hemodynamic Management

Scheeren and Saugel (2018) propose a prediction → prevention → personalization framework for intraoperative hypotension (IOH), defined as MAP <65 mmHg for ≥1 minute. IOH is associated with myocardial injury, AKI, and 30-day mortality even in brief episodes.

IOH — The 3P Framework (Scheeren & Saugel 2018)

Predict: Pre- and intraoperative risk stratification. PPV >13% before induction predicts post-induction hypotension. Continuous non-invasive arterial pressure (ClearSight, CNAP) enables pre-induction waveform data.

Prevent: Goal-directed fluid therapy (GDT) using PPV/SVV to optimize SV on the Starling curve before hypotension occurs. Vasopressors (norepinephrine, phenylephrine) titrated to MAP — initiated before hypotension, not in response to it.

Personalize: Target MAP to the individual. Autoregulation assessment (transcranial Doppler, cerebral oximetry) defines the optimal MAP for each patient — particularly relevant in neurosurgery, cardiac surgery, CKD, and chronic hypertension.

Hypotension Prediction Index (HPI) — The "Predict" Pillar, Operationalized

The natural extension of the Scheeren/Saugel prediction concept. HPI is a machine-learning algorithm that analyzes ~50 features of the arterial pressure waveform (slope, area, complexity, dynamic indices) and outputs a 0–100 score for impending hypotension. It alerts the clinician before MAP falls below 65, converting reactive treatment into pre-emptive intervention.

Performance: predicts hypotension 5 min in advance with sensitivity and specificity ~0.86; an HPI of 80–89 gives a median ~6 minutes of warning before MAP <65. Validated on both invasive arterial lines and non-invasive finger-cuff waveforms (ClearSight), extending applicability to nearly any surgical patient. In major abdominal surgery, HPI-guided care reduced the time-weighted average of hypotension versus standard pulse-contour monitoring.

Caveat: HPI is not magic — it inherits every waveform-quality dependency in Section 07 (damping, leveling, signal artifact). A poorly damped trace produces a poor HPI. It also draws controversy over how its training data defined events; treat the score as a prompt to look at the patient and the waveform, not a substitute for either.

Technical Setup, Troubleshooting & Artifacts

Garbage in, garbage out. Hernandez et al. (2022) note that the waveform is only as trustworthy as the system generating it. Transducer positioning, damping, and zeroing errors produce false alarms and misguided interventions.

Zeroing & Leveling

Transducer must be at the phlebostatic axis (4th ICS, mid-axillary line) for MAP accuracy. Each 10 cm below → +7.4 mmHg false elevation. Re-zero after every position change and every 8–12 hours. "Fast-flush" test to confirm optimal damping before relying on waveform dynamics.

Over-Damping

Air bubbles, clots, long compliant tubing, kinking. Result: falsely low SBP, falsely high DBP, MAP relatively preserved. Waveform appears "slurred" — rounded upstroke, absent notch. Fast-flush test: slow return to baseline, no oscillations.

Under-Damping (Resonance)

Stiff short tubing, high cardiac output, tachycardia. Result: falsely high SBP, falsely low DBP, oscillatory artifact. Fast-flush test: >2 oscillations before return to baseline. Correct with the Gardiner chamber or resonance-reducing devices.

Optimal Damping

Natural frequency >25 Hz with damping coefficient 0.6–0.7. Fast-flush test: 1–2 oscillations, crisp return to baseline. Stiff (non-compliant) short tubing, minimal stopcocks, no air. Standard tubing length ≤120 cm.

Site-Specific Amplification

Radial SBP typically 5–20 mmHg higher than aortic root SBP due to wave reflection. DBP and MAP closer. Femoral = closer to aortic. Post-cardiopulmonary bypass: radial-aortic gradient can exceed 30 mmHg ("radial artery vasospasm") — use femoral or resume aortic monitoring.

Post-CPB Radial Artifact

After cardiac surgery and rewarming, radial arterial pressure can significantly underestimate aortic pressure for 20–60 minutes. Mechanism: peripheral vasospasm and thermoregulatory vasoconstriction. Use femoral or aortic root for these patients until resolution confirmed.

Pulse Contour Analysis & CO Estimation

Beyond qualitative waveform reading, pulse contour algorithms (PiCCO, LiDCO, EV1000/VolumeView, Edwards ClearSight) derive continuous beat-by-beat SV and CO from the arterial pressure waveform morphology — primarily the area under the systolic portion and the waveform's shape characteristics.

Pulse Contour Principle (Wesseling/Modelflow) SV_beat ≈ ∫[systolic area] / Zao(t) // Zao = aortic impedance (estimated from MAP, HR, demographics) CO = SV × HR SVV (%) = (SV_max − SV_min) / SV_mean × 100 // over 30-sec window or respiratory cycle
Clinical Limitation Note

Pulse contour algorithms require periodic calibration (thermodilution via central venous catheter for PiCCO/LiDCO) or uncalibrated population-based estimation (FloTrac/Vigileo, ClearSight). Accuracy deteriorates with irregular rhythms, severe vasomotor instability, and aortic regurgitation. Hernandez et al. (2022) caution that SV estimates should always be interpreted alongside waveform morphology — not replaced by them.

Bedside Waveform Interpretation — Rapid Algorithm

→
Step 1: Is the system reliable? Transducer level correct? Fast-flush test: optimal damping? No kinks, bubbles, or long compliant tubing? If not → fix first.
→
Step 2: What is the MAP? <65 = action threshold. But MAP alone is insufficient — note the DBP and SBP that created it. High SBP + low DBP (wide PP) ≠ same as narrow PP hitting same MAP.
→
Step 3: What is the pulse pressure? Narrow PP (<25–30): low SV, tamponade, AS, hypovolemia. Wide PP (>60): aortic regurgitation, sepsis, high CO state, arterial stiffness.
→
Step 4: What does the upstroke tell you? Steep = good LV contractility. Slow/gradual = cardiogenic or AS. Bisferiens (double peak) = HOCM or combined AS+AR.
→
Step 5: Where is the dicrotic notch? High = high SVR (vasoconstriction). Low = low SVR (sepsis, nitroprusside). Absent radially ≠ absent centrally (normal peripheral dampening).
→
Step 6: Is there respiratory variation? Watch the monitor screen over 5–10 breaths. Visible swings ≥13% PPV in a CMV patient = preload-dependent. If patient is breathing spontaneously, use PLR test instead.
→
Step 7: Is there beat-to-beat variation? Alternating amplitude = pulsus alternans → severe LV failure or bigeminy. SBP drop >10 mmHg with inspiration = pulsus paradoxus → tamponade, severe obstructive disease.
→
Step 8: Integrate with clinical context Waveform → hemodynamic phenotype → targeted intervention (fluid, vasopressor, inotrope, drainage). Re-assess after each intervention. The waveform is a real-time loop — use it continuously, not as a one-time snapshot.

PA Catheter Anatomy, Lumens & West Zones

The balloon-tipped flow-directed pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz) is a 7.5 Fr, 110 cm catheter with four functional ports. Each lumen provides both pressure data and infusion access at specific anatomic sites. Understanding the catheter anatomy is prerequisite to reading its waveforms reliably.

Distal Lumen tip — PA

At the catheter tip, in the pulmonary artery. Measures PA pressure, PCWP (when balloon inflated), and pulmonary arterial blood for SvO2 sampling. This is the waveform-rich port.

Proximal Lumen 30 cm from tip — RA

Opens in the right atrium (30 cm from tip in most adults). Measures CVP/RAP and provides the injectate port for thermodilution cardiac output. RA waveform is read here.

Balloon Port tip inflation

Inflates the 1.5 mL latex balloon at the tip for flotation during insertion and for PCWP measurement. Balloon must fully deflate after wedge — sustained wedge = PA infarction risk.

Thermistor 4 cm from tip

Temperature sensor 4 cm proximal to tip. Detects cold injectate bolus passing by for thermodilution CO calculation. CCO-capable catheters use a thermal filament for continuous output.

VIP / Infusion Lumen ~14–26 cm from tip

Mid-catheter port sitting in the RV or right-sided central venous circulation. Used for drug infusion, does not measure waveforms in most standard catheters.

Continuous SvO2 fiberoptic catheter

Specialized catheters include a fiberoptic oximetry channel for continuous mixed venous O₂ saturation. Critical in cardiogenic shock monitoring where SvO2 trends precede clinical deterioration.

Flotation Sequence: RA → RV → PA → PCWP

As the catheter advances with the balloon inflated, the pressure waveform transitions through four characteristic patterns. Recognizing these transitions in real-time determines catheter position without fluoroscopy — and prevents life-threatening misplacements.

Pressure Waveform Progression During Catheter Flotation
Critical Transition Recognition

RA → RV: Sudden dramatic increase in systolic pressure (from ~5 to ~25 mmHg) with low diastolic remaining near baseline. Waveform becomes square-wave-like. No dicrotic notch.

RV → PA: Systolic pressure stays the same (~25 mmHg) but diastolic pressure jumps (from ~4 to ~10–12 mmHg). Dicrotic notch appears. This is the most important transition — confirms tip has crossed the pulmonic valve.

PA → PCWP: Overall pressure drops and waveform loses its PA pattern — returns to low-amplitude undulating pattern with visible a and v waves. If balloon inflation does not produce wedge waveform at appropriate catheter depth (45–55 cm from right IJ), do not advance further — PA rupture risk.

PCWP Pathology & Giant V Waves

The PCWP is a left-heart window delivered through the pulmonary vasculature. Its morphology reveals left atrial compliance, mitral valve function, and LV end-diastolic filling pressures. Giant V waves are one of the most recognizable and action-forcing waveform patterns in invasive hemodynamics.

PCWP Patterns — Normal, Giant V Wave (MR), Elevated (LHF), Mitral Stenosis
Giant V Wave — What It Actually Means

Classic definition: V peak − mean PCWP ≥10 mmHg → specificity 94% for severe MR, sensitivity only 27% (Karger, 2018, n=126 RHCs). The traditional high-bar definition is highly specific but misses most severe MR.

More sensitive: V − mean PCWP ≥7 mmHg (sens ~73%, spec ~64%). V waves also occur with any non-compliant left atrium (HFpEF, MS, post-AF cardioversion), VSD (shunt increases pulmonary venous return), and with MCS devices.

Practical rule: A giant V wave is proportionally dramatic. MR + dilated LA + elevated LVEDP → V waves >20 mmHg in virtually all patients. A V wave that exceeds the mean PCWP by <7 mmHg is rarely severe MR.

PCWP vs LVEDP — When They Diverge

PCWP overestimates LVEDP: Mitral regurgitation (giant V wave artificially elevates mean PCWP), non-compliant LA, Zone 1/2 position with PEEP (PCWP reflects Palv).

PCWP underestimates LVEDP: PEEP overcorrection, Zone 3 tip migration with patient movement, pulmonary venous obstruction (PVOD, pulmonary vein stenosis).

PCWP ≠ LAP: Mitral stenosis (LAP-LVEDP gradient, measure LAP directly via transseptal or LA pressure transducer). PEEP correction: true PCWP ≈ measured PCWP − (0.5 × PEEP [cmH₂O × 0.74 to convert]).

Intracardiac Shunt Detection — Oximetry Step-Up

Blood samples drawn during catheter withdrawal from PA → RV → RA → SVC/IVC detect left-to-right shunts via a "step-up" in oxygen saturation.

Central Venous Pressure (CVP) — Dedicated Reference

CVP measured via a central venous catheter (CVC) produces the identical a/c/v/x/y waveform as the RA tracing in Section 12. What is absent from that section is the clinical context of CVC-based monitoring: its profound limitations as a fluid predictor, site-specific nuances, the JVP correlation, and the differential diagnosis of elevated CVP.

CVP as Fluid Responsiveness Predictor — The Evidence

Marik PE et al. (Crit Care Med 2008, 24 studies, 803 patients): CVP AUC 0.56 for predicting fluid responsiveness — statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip. Neither the absolute value nor its change after a bolus reliably predicts CO response.

Practical rule: CVP <2 mmHg = likely hypovolemic. CVP >15 = likely volume-overloaded or RV failure. Between those extremes, use dynamic indices (PPV, PLR, EEOT). Never use a single CVP value as a fluid trigger.

Normal Values & Site Differences

IJ / subclavian: 0–8 mmHg (mean ~4–5). Approximates RAP when no SVC obstruction exists.

Femoral CVP: 2–4 mmHg higher than IJ due to hydrostatic column and intra-abdominal pressure. Not interchangeable with RAP without correction — particularly important in obese patients and those with elevated IAP.

Kussmaul's sign: CVP rises with spontaneous inspiration (normal = falls 1–3 mmHg). Positive Kussmaul = constrictive pericarditis, RV failure, or rarely cardiac tamponade with loculated effusion.

LV Pressure Waveform & Aortic Pullback

Left heart catheterization provides direct LV pressure measurement — the highest-fidelity assessment of LV systolic and diastolic function available. Catheter pullback from LV to aorta quantifies outflow obstruction and is the gold standard for aortic stenosis severity and dynamic LVOT obstruction diagnosis.

LVEDP — Clinical Thresholds

Normal: 4–12 mmHg. Elevation reflects reduced LV compliance (hypertrophy, ischemia, diastolic dysfunction) or volume overload. Measured at the Z point (onset of QRS) on the LV pressure trace — not at the a-wave peak.

LVEDP >25 mmHg: pulmonary edema threshold acutely. Chronic LHF: ≥18 mmHg = elevated filling. Acute severe AR: LVEDP may equilibrate with aortic diastolic — pathognomonic of extreme severity (premature mitral valve closure on echo confirms).

Hemodynamic paradox: ↓CO + ↑↑SVR (opposite of normal pregnancy physiology). Waveform: ↑MAP, narrow PP, high notch, ↑DBP. Peripartum Cardiomyopathy

Last month of pregnancy to 5 months postpartum. De novo LV dysfunction (EF <45%). Hemodynamic profile: ↓CO, ↑PCWP, ↑SVR — cardiogenic shock pattern. Waveform: narrow PP, slow upstroke, pulsus alternans in severe cases. Recovery in ~50% by 6 months. Bromocriptine (prolactin inhibitor) may improve outcomes (European evidence).

Post-Cardiac Surgery Hemodynamic Monitoring

The post-CPB patient inhabits a unique hemodynamic state driven by systemic inflammatory response to bypass, myocardial stunning, temperature dysregulation, protamine reactions, and acute correction of the surgical lesion. Standard waveform assumptions require significant adjustment in this population.

Radial-Aortic Gradient Post-CPB

Radial SBP underestimates aortic SBP by 10–30 mmHg for 20–60 min post-bypass. Mechanism: peripheral vasospasm + cold cardioplegia rewarming. Clinical consequence: treating a "low" radial MAP that is actually adequate at the aortic root. Resolution: spontaneous within ~60 min or with vasodilator. Use femoral or aortic root pressure (via aortic cannula) if radial is grossly discordant.

Myocardial Stunning Post-Bypass

Transient LV dysfunction from ischemia-reperfusion injury after aortic cross-clamp. Duration: minutes to 6 hours. Waveform: slow upstroke, reduced PP, pulsus alternans in severe cases, low dP/dt. Treatment: temporary inotropic support (dobutamine, milrinone), AV sequential pacing (HR 80–90 optimizes CO), IABP if refractory.

Protamine Reaction

(1) Systemic hypotension only: MAP drops, SVR falls, wide PP — most common. (2) Pulmonary vasoconstriction: ↑↑PAP, ↑PVR, ↑RAP, RV strain — true protamine pulmonary vasoconstriction. (3) Anaphylaxis: distributive shock + bronchospasm + urticaria. Risk factors: fish allergy, prior protamine exposure, NPH insulin users. Treatment: epinephrine, calcium, slow/stop protamine infusion.

Post-Sternotomy Tamponade

Often loculated — may not equalize all chamber pressures. Presents as unexplained ↓CO + ↑CVP + ↓UO rather than classic equalization. CVP waveform: X>Y pattern, pulsus paradoxus may be absent with loculated clot. Do not attempt pericardiocentesis — surgical re-exploration required. Urgent bedside echo identifies culprit chamber compression.

Vasoplegia Post-CPB

Refractory vasodilation from complement activation, cytokines, contact pathway stimulation. Wide PP, low SVR, low DBP, ↑CO — distributive pattern despite adequate filling. Vasopressin 0.03–0.1 units/min is first-line (unaffected by CPB-inflammatory mediators). Methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg for refractory vasoplegia (NO synthase inhibition). Hydroxocobalamin as alternative.

NIRS Cerebral Oximetry

Regional cerebral O₂ saturation (rSO₂) via bifrontal NIRS. Normal 55–75%. Drop >20% from baseline or absolute <50% for >2–3 min correlates with neurocognitive injury. Integrates with arterial MAP: MAP drops + rSO₂ drops = hemodynamic cause (raise MAP). MAP stable + rSO₂ drops = O₂ delivery problem (Hgb, SaO₂, EtCO₂). CPB flow adjustments guided by rSO₂ reduce stroke and ICU LOS.

Intraparenchymal Bolt

Codman, Camino, Raumedic — fiber-optic or strain-gauge placed directly in brain parenchyma. Accurate long-term, no drift issues of older fluid-coupled systems. Cannot drain CSF. Non-dominant frontal placement. Cannot re-zero in situ — calibrate at insertion only. Preferred in coagulopathic patients or when ventricles are small/slit-like.

Monroe-Kellie Doctrine

Total intracranial volume = constant: brain (~80%) + blood (~10%) + CSF (~10%). Compensatory reserve: CSF displacement to spinal canal (largest buffer) then venous blood displacement. Once reserve exhausted: exponential ICP rise from small volume additions. Why a 100 mL slow-growing meningioma may be asymptomatic while a 30 mL acute subdural causes herniation.

Cerebral Autoregulation & PRx

Normal: CBF constant at CPP 50–150 mmHg via vasomotor response. Disrupted TBI: CBF becomes pressure-passive (MAP↑ → ICP↑ → no protection). PRx (correlation MAP to ICP, 5-min rolling window): <0.2 = intact; >0.2 = impaired. PRx–CPP curve: find the CPP at minimum PRx → optimal CPP for that patient. Typically 60–80 mmHg in TBI but highly individualized.

Herniation Recognition

Cushing's triad (hypertension + bradycardia + irregular breathing) = late herniation — autoregulation lost. Arterial trace: widened PP + bradycardia (Cushing reflex). Uncal herniation: ipsilateral CN III palsy + contralateral hemiplegia. Treat immediately: hyperventilate (PaCO₂ 35, temporary), mannitol 1 g/kg IV, hypertonic saline 3% or 23.4% 30 mL bolus, emergent neurosurgical decompression.

CO₂ Reactivity & Hyperventilation

Each 1 mmHg ↓PaCO₂ = ~4% ↓CBF = ↓CBV = ↓ICP within minutes. Hyperventilation to PaCO₂ 30–35 mmHg lowers ICP rapidly. Temporary measure only — tolerance develops in 4–6h (renal bicarbonate compensation). Do not hyperventilate prophylactically (cerebral ischemia risk). Target PaCO₂ 35–40 mmHg at baseline; use 30–35 only for acute herniation as bridge to definitive intervention.

Pulse Wave Transformation, Reflection & Arterial Stiffness

The pressure wave generated by LV ejection does not arrive at the capillary bed unchanged. It is progressively reshaped by arterial wall mechanics and wave reflection as it travels outward, and the entire purpose of this reshaping is to convert a pulsatile, high-energy ejection into the steady, low-pressure flow that capillary exchange actually requires. The waveform you read at any given site is a record of how far the wave has traveled and how stiff the vessel bed has become.

Why a Radial Arterial Line Is Not a Perfect Aortic Surrogate

This is the physiologic basis behind the radial-aortic discrepancy referenced throughout this guide (Sections 01, 07, 26). A young patient's radial SBP can legitimately exceed central aortic SBP by 10–20 mmHg due to favorable pulse pressure amplification — this is normal physiology, not measurement error. An elderly or post-CPB patient with stiff vessels shows little to no amplification, and post-bypass vasospasm can even reverse the gradient. The clinical takeaway: when radial and central pressures disagree, consider where the patient sits on the stiffness spectrum before assuming line malfunction [Ref. 33, 34].

The Arteriole — Where the Pressure Actually Drops

The SVR formula in Section 02 treats resistance as a single lumped value, but resistance is not distributed evenly across the vascular tree. Anatomically, one segment does almost all of the work.

Pressure Decrement Across the Systemic Vascular Tree
The 70% Rule

In the systemic circulation, there is minimal pressure change from the aorta through the large and small arteries — but small arteries and arterioles account for roughly 70% of the total pressure drop, and they are the principal site regulating SVR [Ref. 30]. This is anatomically distinct from the pulmonary circulation, where the drop is distributed far more evenly across arteries, capillaries, and even veins [Ref. 14] — one reason PVR and SVR behave so differently as physiologic levers.

Direct micropuncture data (hamster cheek pouch, cat mesentery) localize the steepest drop to arterioles in the 200–100 μm range, with a second, smaller drop at precapillary sphincters (15–25 μm) [Ref. 36].

Why the Smallest Vessels Aren't the Highest-Resistance Vessels

Resistance follows the Hagen-Poiseuille relationship: R ∝ 1/r⁴. A capillary has a smaller radius than an arteriole, but capillaries run in such dense parallel networks that their combined resistance is low — parallel resistors always reduce total resistance below any single resistor's value. Arterioles, with fewer parallel channels and active smooth muscle tone, are the true resistance vessels [Ref. 37]. This is also why a tiny change in arteriolar radius — from sympathetic tone, vasopressors, or local metabolic signals — produces an outsized change in SVR and regional flow.

Hemodynamic Coherence — When the Macrocirculation Lies

Every monitor discussed in this guide — arterial line, PAC, CVP, ICP — measures the macrocirculation: the pressures and flows in vessels large enough to cannulate. None of them directly see the capillary bed, where oxygen delivery actually happens. Hemodynamic coherence is the principle, formalized by Can Ince, that normalizing macrocirculatory targets (MAP, CO, ScvO₂) only restores tissue perfusion if the microcirculation remains coupled to those targets — and in shock states, that coupling is frequently lost [Ref. 38].

The Core Problem

A patient can have a normalized MAP, an adequate CO, and a reassuring ScvO₂ — every number on this guide's monitors looking acceptable — while the sublingual or splanchnic microcirculation remains shut down, capillaries unperfused, tissue hypoxic. ["Acceptable" pressure may coexist with poor microcirculation — the same observation made independently in bedside critical care teaching, and the entire rationale for handheld vital microscopy and lactate-trend monitoring in resuscitated shock [Ref. 38, 39].]

When Coherence Holds vs Breaks

In early, uncomplicated shock, macro- and microcirculatory changes track together — fluid that raises CO also raises microvascular flow. Once inflammation and endothelial/glycocalyx injury set in, this coupling degrades: a fluid challenge can increase CI, MAP, and CVP in lockstep while sublingual and gut microcirculatory flow indices fail to follow [Ref. 40]. Time matters — Boerma et al. demonstrated that the relationship between intestinal and sublingual microcirculation itself shifts over the first three days of septic shock [Ref. 41].

Practical Implication for This Guide's Monitors

None of this argues against the PAC, arterial line, or CVP — it argues for humility about what they prove. A normalized hemodynamic profile (Sections 15–16, 23) is necessary but not sufficient evidence of adequate resuscitation. Lactate clearance, capillary refill, and skin mottling remain useful precisely because they are downstream of the microcirculation in a way that MAP and CO are not. Where available, point-of-care sublingual videomicroscopy directly visualizes the compartment none of the catheters in this guide can reach.

The Counterargument — Coherence Is Debated, Not Settled

Hernandez and Teboul published a direct challenge to the framework as commonly applied, arguing that apparent "dissociation" in some studies reflects assessment timing rather than a true loss of coupling, and that early normalization of macrocirculatory parameters and lactate still correlates with improved survival in large cohorts [Ref. 42]. Ince's response defended the concept while acknowledging the methodological critique [Ref. 42]. Treat hemodynamic coherence as an important conceptual corrective to monitor-worship, not as a fully validated bedside test with its own actionable thresholds.
