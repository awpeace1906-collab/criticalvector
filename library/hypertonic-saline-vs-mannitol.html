Hypertonic Saline vs. Mannitol

v1. Not which one is better — which one is safer for the patient in front of you. 1. Why Hyperosmolar Therapy Works

Both agents lower intracranial pressure by the same fundamental mechanism: creating an osmotic gradient across an intact blood-brain barrier that pulls free water out of brain tissue and into the vasculature, reducing cerebral edema and brain volume. Both are genuinely effective — the more clinically important question is not whether either works, but which one is safer for a specific patient's physiology.

The premise most reference cards skip: most clinicians pick between these two based on habit — whichever their training program or attending defaulted to — rather than the patient's volume status, renal function, or sodium trajectory in front of them.

2. Mechanism Comparison

PropertyMannitolHypertonic saline
ClassOsmotic diuretic (sugar alcohol)Hyperosmolar sodium solution
Reflection coefficient (BBB)~0.9 — some crosses an injured BBB over time, blunting the osmotic gradient~1.0 — stays almost entirely intravascular even with some BBB disruption, a more durable gradient
Secondary effectsRheological effect: reduces blood viscosity, transiently improves cerebral blood flow and oxygen delivery independent of the osmotic effect; obligate diuresisVolume expansion: increases intravascular volume and cerebral perfusion pressure, generally without the diuretic volume loss
Effect on volume statusDepletes intravascular volume via diuresis — a problem in an already hypovolemic or hypotensive patientExpands intravascular volume — an advantage in hypotension, a liability in volume-overloaded states (heart failure)

The reflection-coefficient difference is the mechanistic reason hypertonic saline is generally considered to hold its osmotic gradient more reliably across a disrupted blood-brain barrier than mannitol — the theoretical basis for HS's proposed edge, distinct from the clinical trial question addressed in section 3.

3. The Evidence: Does Either Actually Win?

This is a heavily meta-analyzed question, and the honest answer depends on which time window you look at — a nuance the clean "HTS has the edge" framing common in social media summaries tends to flatten.

StudyFinding
Kamel et al., Crit Care Med 2011Meta-analysis of randomized trials comparing equiosmolar doses. Findings suggested hypertonic saline may be superior to mannitol as the standard of care, but the authors explicitly called for a large multicenter RCT to definitively establish first-line therapy — a call that, as of this writing, still hasn't been fully answered.
Gu et al., Neurosurg Rev 201912 RCTs, 438 patients. HS and mannitol were close on both functional outcome (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.89–1.54) and ICP reduction (MD −0.16, 95% CI −0.59 to 0.26) — no significant difference found.
Mangat et al., Neurosurgery 2020HS showed a superior combined effect on ICP and CPP burden. Notably, HS patients had a significant CPP increase from baseline (63±15 to 76±17 mmHg at 120 min) that was not seen with mannitol — consistent with HS's volume-expansion mechanism in section 2.
Cai & He, Brain Inj 202415 RCTs, 624 patients. HS raised CPP more than mannitol at 30–60 minutes (MD 5.54 mmHg, 95% CI 3.04–8.03). No significant difference in mortality, functional outcome, successful ICP treatment, ICP at 30–60 or 90–120 minutes, CPP at 90–120 minutes, or treatment failure.

The nuance worth remembering: hypertonic saline's advantage is most consistently demonstrated in the acute, early post-infusion window — not as a durable, sustained neuroprotective superiority over mannitol. Whether HS confers lasting outcome benefit over mannitol remains unresolved (Cochrane 2020; Cai 2024). This is a genuinely live evidence question, not a settled one in HS's favor.

4. Dosing

ParameterMannitolHypertonic saline
Typical dose0.25–1 g/kg IV bolus3%: 150–250 mL bolus, or continuous infusion; 23.4%: 30 mL bolus (central line preferred)
AccessPeripheral line acceptable3% peripheral IV generally acceptable; higher concentrations (23.4%) preferably via central venous catheter
Onset of action15–30 minutes5–15 minutes
Duration of action4–6 hours2–4 hours

The faster onset and shorter duration of HS versus mannitol maps directly onto the acute-vs-sustained evidence pattern in section 3 — a faster-acting agent showing its clearest advantage in the acute window, then converging with the slower, longer-acting agent by the time both have reached steady effect.

5. Safety Profiles & Contraindications

RiskMannitolHypertonic saline
Hard stopSerum osmolality >320 mOsm/LSerum sodium >160–165 mEq/L
Volume/hemodynamic riskHypotension and hypovolemia from obligate diuresisVolume overload in patients with underlying heart failure
Renal riskAcute kidney injury risk, particularly with repeated dosing or in dehydrated/renal-compromised patientsLower direct renal toxicity signal
Rebound phenomenonRebound ICP elevation described, particularly with repeated dosing as the agent redistributesLess prominently described, though not entirely absent from the literature
Electrolyte riskDilutional hyponatremiaHypernatremia and volume overload if not monitored; central pontine myelinolysis is a theoretical concern with rapid correction, though the acute-bolus context of ICP treatment differs from the chronic hyponatremia correction scenario that risk is best established in

Avoid mannitol in: dehydrated, hypotensive, or renal-compromised patients — the obligate diuresis compounds all three problems simultaneously.

Hypertonic saline's edge in specific populations: more stable blood pressure with fewer hemodynamic swings; a better option in patients who are already hemodynamically unstable; preferred in dehydration and renal dysfunction where mannitol's diuretic effect would compound the problem; may reduce ICU length of stay in some analyses; effective ICP reduction with a comparatively predictable response, per the dosing/onset data in section 4.

Reading sections 3 and 5 together: mannitol's safety concerns and hypertonic saline's proposed advantages both trace back to the same mechanistic difference — mannitol's obligate diuresis versus HS's volume-expanding effect. The choice is less "which drug is better" and more "which direction do you need this patient's volume status to move."

HemodynamicsStable, normotensiveUnstable, hypotensive, or at risk of hypotension
Access availablePeripheral line onlyPeripheral OK for 3%; central access available if higher concentration considered
Lab trigger to stopOsmolality >320 mOsm/LSodium >160–165 mEq/L

The one-line summary worth remembering: it's not about which one is better — the meta-analytic evidence in section 3 doesn't clearly support either as definitively superior once you look past the acute 30–60 minute window. It's about which one is safer for the specific patient's volume status, renal function, and hemodynamics in front of you.
