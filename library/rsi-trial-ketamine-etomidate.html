The RSI Trial: Ketamine Isn't as Hemodynamically Neutral as We Thought

Casey JD, Seitz KP, Driver BE, et al; RSI Investigators and the Pragmatic Critical Care Research Group. Ketamine or Etomidate for Tracheal Intubation of Critically Ill Adults.

N Engl J Med. 2025. doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2511420. PMID: 41369227 · 14 US hospitals (ED + ICU) · Original social summary: definshock. This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the account.

Verdict on the source post: the cover slide's framing ("if you said ketamine because it's hemodynamically neutral, we need to talk") is exactly right and matches the trial's actual headline finding. This is the largest, most pragmatic RCT yet on this question, and it directly overturns a widely-taught assumption.
01

The Setup

Etomidate's adrenal suppression concern pushed a generation of clinicians toward ketamine as the "safer" induction agent for hemodynamically fragile patients. This trial tested whether that switch actually helps.

Peri-intubation cardiovascular instability is common and dangerous: the INTUBE study found it in 43.4% of critically ill intubations, with an adjusted OR of 2.47 for ICU mortality when it occurred. Etomidate causes transient adrenal suppression via 11-beta-hydroxylase inhibition, which pushed many clinicians toward ketamine as the reflexive "hemodynamically neutral" alternative for sick patients — a belief based largely on smaller trials and physiologic reasoning rather than a large, pragmatic head-to-head test in the sickest population.

02

Design

DesignMulticenter, pragmatic, randomized, open-label trial, intention-to-treat analysis
Population2,365 critically ill adults undergoing tracheal intubation in an ED or ICU at 14 US hospitals; excluded trauma, pregnancy, prisoners
Groups1,176 ketamine (1–2 mg/kg IV, actual body weight) vs. 1,189 etomidate (0.2–0.3 mg/kg IV, actual body weight)
Case mix~Half with sepsis/septic shock; ~20% already on vasopressors at time of intubation; average age ~60; >99% received their randomized drug
Primary outcomeIn-hospital death by day 28
Cardiovascular collapse defined asSBP <65 mmHg, new or increased vasopressor dose, or cardiac arrest during intubation
03

Results

OutcomeKetamineEtomidateResult
In-hospital death by day 28330/1,173 (28.1%)345/1,186 (29.1%)Adjusted risk diff −0.8 pts (95% CI −4.5 to 2.9), P=.65
Cardiovascular collapse during intubation260/1,176 (22.1%)202/1,189 (17.0%)Risk diff +5.1 pts (95% CI 1.9 to 8.3) — more common with ketamine

No mortality benefit for ketamine over etomidate — the drugs performed essentially identically on the outcome that mattered most. But ketamine produced more cardiovascular collapse, not less, driven mainly by new or increased vasopressor requirements rather than cardiac arrest itself. This directly inverts the assumption that drove many departments to switch away from etomidate in unstable patients.

04

What This Doesn't Mean

  • Not an argument to abandon ketamine. Independent reviews (EMCrit, St Emlyn's, NAEMSP) are consistent: ketamine remains a reasonable, often preferred choice — particularly in severe asthma/COPD where preserving respiratory drive matters, and in most routine intubations with mild-to-moderate shock. The trial corrects overconfidence in ketamine's hemodynamic "safety margin," it doesn't demonstrate etomidate superiority.
  • Open-label design caveat. Clinicians knew the assigned drug, which could theoretically influence how quickly they escalated vasopressors. But supplemental analyses found no significant between-group differences in pre-intubation, within-1-hour, or prophylactic vasopressor use, and <1% of patients received the non-assigned drug — both arguing against meaningful behavioral bias explaining the result.
  • The 1-point mortality gap isn't a signal either direction. A numerically-lower ketamine mortality (28.1% vs 29.1%) is not statistically significant and, given the trial's size, shouldn't be read as a near-miss trend in either direction.
05

The Practical Takeaway

Not "which drug is safer" — "prepare for instability regardless of which drug you pick."

The consistent message across independent commentary on this trial: the actual lesson isn't a verdict on ketamine vs. etomidate, it's that neither agent is a hemodynamic free pass in critically ill patients. Whichever induction agent is chosen, have vasopressors drawn up and ready, anticipate peri-intubation hypotension as the default expectation rather than the exception, and treat the induction-agent choice as one input among several (respiratory status, adrenal concerns, familiarity) rather than the primary lever for preventing collapse.

06

Bottom Line

The source post's framing holds up precisely: "ketamine because it's hemodynamically neutral" is a belief this trial directly tests and doesn't support. No mortality difference between ketamine and etomidate, but significantly more cardiovascular collapse with ketamine (22.1% vs 17.0%), driven by vasopressor escalation. This is the largest, most pragmatic head-to-head trial on this question to date, and its message is preparation over drug selection: assume peri-intubation instability is coming, regardless of which agent you reach for.
