Methylene Blue in Adult Shock: Underpowered, or Buried by an Optimistic TSA Assumption?

Rodríguez-Lima DR, Rodríguez-Villegas A, Toro Egas JD, Campo Bautista EM. The Effectiveness of Methylene Blue in Adult Shock: A Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, and Trial Sequential Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.

J Clin Med. 2026;15(12):4481. doi:10.3390/jcm15124481 · PROSPERO CRD420261326534 · Original social summary: CriticalCareNow (#CritBits)

Verdict on the source post: every number CriticalCareNow cites is accurate — OR 0.73 (0.40–1.36), the 1,773-patient required information size, and the ~30% enrollment fraction (535/1,773 ≈ 30.2%) all match the primary paper exactly. What the caption doesn't have room for is that this newest, largest meta-analysis is actually the most conservative read of this literature — several smaller, earlier meta-analyses on overlapping trials found statistically significant mortality benefit, and one of those findings triggered a published methodological dispute over how TSA's own "inconclusive" verdict gets calculated.
01

The Setup

Methylene blue blocks the nitric oxide–guanylate cyclase pathway that drives vasoplegia. Whether that hemodynamic effect converts to a survival benefit has been asked, and answered differently, several times now.

This is at least the fourth systematic review/meta-analysis on methylene blue (MB) in adult shock published since 2023, each with a different scope: some restricted to septic shock specifically, others pooling septic shock with post-cardiac-surgery vasoplegia. Rodríguez-Lima et al. is the newest and, at 9 RCTs (535 patients), the largest septic-shock-focused pool to date, searched through February 2026 and registered on PROSPERO.

Why these disagree

The differences trace to scope, not to contradictory data. Ng et al. and Rodríguez-Lima et al. both restrict to septic shock, but Ng's pool is smaller (5 vs. 9–8 trials) and happened to catch a more favorable point estimate before later, larger trials diluted it. Pisano/Wieruszewski's larger, significant result comes from pooling septic shock together with post-cardiac-surgery vasoplegia — a different, generally lower-risk population responding to the same drug for a related but distinct physiologic problem. Rodríguez-Lima's team explicitly excluded the cardiac-surgery outlier trial from their own TSA for exactly this reason. None of these papers are wrong; they're answering slightly different questions with overlapping but non-identical trial sets.

04

The TSA Assumption Dispute

"Trial sequential analysis says inconclusive" sounds like an objective fact. It's actually downstream of a chosen number that reasonable people have publicly disagreed about.

TSA requires the analyst to pre-specify an assumed relative risk reduction (RRR) to calculate how many patients would be needed to detect it (the required information size). Rodríguez-Lima et al. used an assumed 25% RRR, yielding the 1,773-patient RIS the source post cites. But a published letter responding to the Pisano/Wieruszewski meta-analysis raised exactly this point about a similar TSA: that meta-analysis's original TSA used an assumed 20% RRR and found the Z-curve inconclusive — but the actual, observed mortality reduction in that pooled data was 40.7% (15% MB vs. 25.3% control). When the letter's authors recalculated TSA using the observed 40.7% RRR instead of the conservative 20% assumption, the Z-curve did cross the significance boundary.

What this means for reading "TSA says inconclusive" claims generally

This isn't a criticism specific to Rodríguez-Lima et al. — their 25% assumption is a defensible, moderately conservative choice, and their own point estimate (OR 0.73, a smaller effect than the 40.7% RRR debated above) makes their inconclusive verdict the more internally consistent one. The broader lesson, useful well beyond this drug: a TSA's "we need more data" conclusion is only as trustworthy as the effect-size assumption baked into its required-information-size calculation. Two honest analysts can look at similar data and land on different verdicts depending on how optimistic that upfront assumption is.

05

What's Actually Consistent Across All Four Analyses

  • Every meta-analysis on MB in shock, regardless of population or mortality conclusion, finds a consistent hemodynamic signal: increased MAP, reduced vasopressor requirement, and shorter time to vasopressor discontinuation.
  • No analysis has found a mortality harm signal — every point estimate favors the treatment direction, even the ones that don't reach significance.
  • Every review, including the most favorable ones, ends with a call for larger, adequately powered, population-specific trials. None claim the question is settled.

A dedicated, adequately powered trial answering the septic-shock-specific mortality question is already underway: EMPRESS (Early Methylene Blue for Mortality Reduction in High-Dose VasoPREssor-Dependent Septic Shock), a multicenter RCT targeting exactly the vasoplegic, high-vasopressor-dose subpopulation where MB's mechanism should matter most.

06

Bottom Line

The source post is accurate and its framing is right: this is evidence of not-enough-data, not evidence of no effect. What's worth adding is that this newest, largest, most conservative analysis sits at one end of a spread of overlapping meta-analyses on the same drug — two smaller ones found statistically significant mortality benefit, and the TSA method itself is sensitive to an upfront assumption that a published methodological letter has directly disputed in a closely related analysis. The hemodynamic benefit (MAP, vasopressor-sparing) is the most reproducible finding across all four papers; the mortality question remains genuinely open, and EMPRESS is the trial to watch for a real answer.
