The counterweight. When Ontario mandated the same checklist province-wide, operative mortality and complications did not fall significantly (Urbach 2014). The successes share a pattern: teams that trained on the list, owned it, and used it at a real pause point. A checklist works when the team uses it, not when it exists. Design and buy-in are the intervention.

Building one that gets used (adapted from Gawande):

1
Pick the pause point: the moment a mistake becomes expensive or impossible to undo (before induction, before incision, before the tube comes out).
2
Choose the type. DO-CONFIRM: work from memory, then stop and verify. READ-DO: read each step and do it, for rare or high-stakes tasks.
3
Killer items only: the steps most often skipped and most harmful when they are. Five to nine items.
4
One line each, answerable yes or no, readable in under a minute. Replace "check" and "ensure" with a concrete action.
5
Force one conversation: what gets said aloud, by whom, before what.
6
Run it, then revise. A checklist that survives its first real use unchanged wasn't tested.

The extubation rescue plan in extubation-decision-protocol is a four-item READ-DO checklist at a pause point.

Shift performance

The "75-day non-negotiables," graded against the evidence.

RuleVerdictWhat the evidence supports
10,000 steps a dayADJUSTMortality benefit rises with steps and levels off around 6,000–8,000 a day in people over 60, and 8,000–10,000 in younger adults. More is fine; 10,000 isn't a threshold.
Lift 3 times a weekHOLDSResistance training twice or more a week is the standard recommendation. Scheduling it next to a shift is a reasonable adherence tactic.
Protein 0.6 g/lb (about 1.3 g/kg)HOLDSAbove the 0.8 g/kg RDA and within the range used to support resistance training.
Bring real foodHOLDSPlanned meals beat vending-machine decisions made while fatigued.
Thirst is mistaken for hungerWEAKLittle direct evidence. Drink to thirst and urine color; don't use water to suppress hunger.
No caffeine after noonADJUST400 mg of caffeine taken even 6 hours before bed significantly disrupted sleep (Drake 2013). The rule is "none within about 6 hours of planned sleep," which on nights means none in the last third of the shift, not "after noon."
2-minute resetHOLDSBrief breaks and breathing exercises are low-cost and reasonable; the clinical version is a deliberate pause before a high-stakes procedure.
Phone out of the bedroomHOLDSPre-sleep screen use is linked to later sleep onset and poorer sleep.

Missing from the list: fatigue itself, the habit with the strongest evidence.

Interns working traditional 24-hour shifts made 35.9% more serious medical errors and 5.6 times as many serious diagnostic errors than on a schedule without extended shifts (Landrigan 2004).

After an extended shift, the odds of a motor vehicle crash were 2.3 times higher and of a near miss 5.9 times higher; each extended shift in a month raised the risk of a crash on the commute home by 16% (Barger 2005).

Protect dark, quiet sleep after nights, nap where allowed, and treat the drive home as the riskiest part of the shift.

04

Carrying the weight

Moral injury is the distress of being part of, or unable to prevent, outcomes that violate one's sense of what's right: rationed care, families saying goodbye by video, patients dying alone. The pandemic produced it at scale. The second victim effect is the related distress after an adverse event or error.

  • Peer support works best when it's structured and available within hours, not weeks. RISE (Resilience In Stressful Events), the Johns Hopkins peer responder program, is a widely copied model.
  • A short team debrief after a death or a bad resuscitation normalizes the reaction and catches system problems.
  • Know your institution's peer support line before you need it.

Acknowledging the people who worked through the pandemic is not political. Public health decisions will keep being debated; the clinicians who carried those years still need support.
