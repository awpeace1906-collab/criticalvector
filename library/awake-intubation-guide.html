Awake Intubation in the ICU

What if the safest move before intubating your sickest patient is not putting them to sleep? Verified against the primary literature. July 2026.

01

The Problem

ICU intubation is not OR intubation. Your average ICU patient is not a healthy 30-year-old getting an ACL fixed.

Up to 45% of ICU intubations have a major adverse event.

Cardiovascular instability: 42.6%

Severe hypoxemia (SpO2 <80%): 9.3%

Cardiac arrest: 3.1%

This is the INTUBE study: 2,964 critically ill patients across 197 sites in 29 countries (Russotto et al, JAMA 2021). 45.2% had at least one major peri-intubation event.

02

The Hammer: Why RSI Can Wreck ICU Patients

When you rapid-sequence-intubate a critically ill patient, you're doing three things at once:

1. Removing sympathetic tone (induction agents)
2. Eliminating spontaneous ventilation (paralytics)
3. Switching to positive pressure, which drops preload

It's like pulling three Jenga blocks at once from a tower that's already leaning. A healthy patient tolerates all three. A patient in shock, or severely hypoxemic, or acidotic, may not have the physiologic reserve to absorb even one.

03

Enter: the Awake Intubation

What if you just didn't do those three things? Awake intubation keeps spontaneous ventilation, sympathetic tone, and airway reflexes intact, and buys time instead of racing a desaturation clock.

Propensity-matched study of ICU patients (135 intubations, 45 awake using a hyperangulated-blade videolaryngoscope, matched 1:2 against standard VL and direct laryngoscopy under general anesthesia):

Awake intubation adverse events: 2.2%

Standard VL under GA: 22%

Direct laryngoscopy under GA: 47%

That's not a typo. Adverse events were defined as MAP under 55 mmHg, SpO2 under 80% after adequate preoxygenation, or peri-procedural cardiac arrest.

04

Who Stays Awake

Ask: who can't tolerate apnea or hemodynamic chaos?

Anatomic difficulty: predicted difficult airway plus difficult rescue, the classic indication.

Physiologic difficulty, the ICU special:

Severe hypoxemia, can't tolerate apnea

Shock or hemodynamic instability, can't tolerate induction

Severe metabolic acidosis, compensatory tachypnea is keeping the patient alive

Right heart failure, positive pressure ventilation is a disaster in this setting

NEJM, ASA, and CAFG guidance converge on the same principle: when physiology is very fragile, keep the patient breathing through the procedure.

05

When Not to Intubate Awake

  • Completely uncooperative or combative patient. Can't follow commands, won't tolerate topicalization. Consider DSI instead (Section 10).
  • True crash airway or cardiac arrest. No time. Secure the airway now.
  • Active vomiting or massive hematemesis. Aspiration risk is immediate; you need paralysis and suction speed, not a slow topicalization.
  • Allergy to local anesthetics. Rare, but real. No topicalization means no awake intubation.
  • Operator inexperience without backup. A poorly done awake intubation is worse than a well-executed RSI. Know your limits.

Patient behavior and high urgency may preclude an awake approach even when the anatomy favors it.

06

The Setup: Topicalization

This is where the procedure succeeds or fails. Nail topicalization and the patient tolerates the rest.

Atomizer: 4% lidocaine to the oropharynx and hypopharynx

Nebulizer: 4% lidocaine, 4-5 mL over 10-15 minutes if time allows

Spray-as-you-go: lidocaine through the scope's working channel onto the cords

Maximum dose: 9 mg/kg lean body weight for topical airway use, per the Difficult Airway Society guideline. Plasma levels stay well below the toxic range at this dose because mucosal absorption is incomplete.

Glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg IV dries secretions, which helps lidocaine actually stick to the mucosa instead of washing off.

07

Sedation: the Art of "Just Enough"

Goal: calm, cooperative, spontaneously breathing. Not asleep.

AgentNotes
DexmedetomidineAnxiolysis, antisialagogue effect, minimal respiratory depression. Reduces hypoxemia vs remifentanil, RR 2.47 (95% CI 1.32-4.64) in a meta-analysis of 8 RCTs. Load 1 mcg/kg over 10 min, then infuse 0.2-0.7 mcg/kg/hr; watch for bradycardia.
RemifentanilGreat cough suppression, but watch for apnea and chest wall rigidity.
KetamineDissociation with preserved airway reflexes; useful in the combative patient.

Avoid oversedation. If the patient stops breathing, you've just turned your awake intubation into an RSI, except now with a mouth full of lidocaine and no paralytic on board yet.

08

Choose Your Weapon: Scope vs VL

DeviceStrengths
Flexible bronchoscopeGold standard for awake intubation, nasal or oral route, best for severe anatomic distortion, requires practice and maintenance.
Video laryngoscope (hyperangulated blade)Faster intubation time in meta-analysis, easier learning curve, comparable first-pass success, may reduce desaturation events, a solid option when a bronchoscope isn't available.

Bottom line: VL is a legitimate alternative, not a downgrade. Use what you're best at. The best device is the one in skilled hands.

09

The Recipe

1. Decide: is this patient too sick for RSI?
2. Prepare: glycopyrrolate IV, suction, backup plan
3. Topicalize: atomize 4% lidocaine to the oropharynx, spray-as-you-go to the cords
4. Sedate: dex infusion titrated to calm, not asleep, plus analgesic of choice
5. Oxygenate: HFNC at 40-60 L/min throughout
6. Intubate: scope or VL, confirm with ETCO2
7. Then induce: give sedation only after the tube is confirmed

The tube goes in before the patient goes to sleep. That's the whole point.

10

Honorable Mention: Delayed Sequence Intubation

For the patient who's too agitated for awake intubation but too hypoxic for RSI: delayed sequence intubation (DSI), procedural sedation where the procedure is preoxygenation.

The concept: give ketamine 1 to 1.5 mg/kg IV, the patient dissociates, now tolerates NIPPV or high-flow nasal cannula for preoxygenation, then give the paralytic and intubate.

The catch: apnea can happen even at low doses. Driver and Reardon reported a case of apnea after just 0.31 mg/kg IV ketamine (25 mg) given to facilitate preoxygenation in a critically ill patient. Always be ready to bag or intubate immediately.

DSI and awake intubation are cousins, not twins. DSI still ends in paralysis and RSI, just delayed until after preoxygenation. Awake intubation secures the airway before any induction agent or paralytic is given at all. Different tools for different problems.

11

Quick Reference Card & Primary Sources

"This takes too long." Reality check: a failed RSI leading to desaturation and cardiac arrest takes longer, and the damage might be permanent. Topicalization with an atomizer takes 3 to 5 minutes. VL-assisted awake intubation can be done in under 3 minutes. The only true contraindication is a patient who cannot cooperate at all, and even then, DSI is in your back pocket.
