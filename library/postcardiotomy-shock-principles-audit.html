Postcardiotomy Shock: Ten Principles and a Closing Window

Critical Care Reviews’ Paper of the Day graphic from a 2026 Annals of Thoracic Surgery review. The ten principles are reproduced faithfully. They are a framework from experienced surgeons, which the post should say.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · November 2026. Original post: @criticalcarereviews, Paper of the Day. This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the original creator.

Verdict: the figure matches Weiss et al. (2026). The review presents its principles as a framework built on experience and case vignettes, not an algorithm tested against outcomes. Postcardiotomy shock remains rare (about 0.5–1.5% of cases) and lethal (in-hospital mortality around 67%).

01

The ten principles

Titles verbatim from the figure; one-line glosses are ours, drawn from the abstract’s framing.

1

Systematically characterize preoperative risk

Know before incision who is likely to need support: ventricular function, pulmonary hypertension, redo surgery, emergency status.

2

Have a game plan and prepare for the worst

Cannulation strategy, device availability and the team’s threshold for escalation agreed before bypass.

3

Expect the unexpected

Separation from bypass is a stress test; treat a difficult wean as information, not bad luck.

4

Recognize and acknowledge problems early

Rising inotrope doses, lactate and filling pressures are a trajectory. Naming it early keeps options open.

5

Simplify the problem by addressing noise

Correct the confounders first: rhythm, pacing, acidosis, calcium, bleeding, tamponade, vasoplegia.

6

Address technical concerns the first time

Graft flow, valve function and anatomic problems are fixed on the table, not after a trip to the ICU. TEE is the arbiter.

7

Vent the heart

Peripheral VA-ECMO raises LV afterload, and a weak LV can distend. Decompress it before it injures the lung or the myocardium.

8

Recognize the goals of tMCS

Recovery, bridge to durable therapy or transplant, or palliation. Name the goal at deployment.

9

Match support to phenotype

Left, right or biventricular failure, with or without oxygenation failure, each point to different devices.

10

Plan an exit strategy

Weaning criteria, the next destination, or a decision to stop: settled early and revisited daily.

02

Reading the curve

The figure plots two bands over time: opportunity (organ preservation, hemodynamic stability, therapeutic options) narrowing from above, and irreversibility (organ injury, hemometabolic collapse, loss of options) rising from below. The patient’s course oscillates between them; each green arrow is an intervention that pushes back, each red arrow a lost chance. The three outcomes on the right are recovery, advanced therapies and palliation. That is the review’s argument in one picture: postcardiotomy shock is a time-dependent process, and the window closes regardless of which device you own.

04

Matching support to phenotype

CV schematic of principle 9, drawn from general device physiology, not reproduced from Weiss et al. Device choice depends on center experience, cannulation access and the goal named under principle 8. On peripheral VA-ECMO, watch for LV distension (principle 7).

The 2026 SCAI SHOCK update now names postcardiotomy as its own phenotype, so these principles and the SCAI staging can be used together: stage on perfusion, then use principle 9 to pick the device.
