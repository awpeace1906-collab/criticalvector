NEXUS, C-Spine, CT Head & New Orleans Rules

Four validated ways to skip imaging after trauma, spine and head. Verified against the original derivation and validation studies. July 2026.

01

Why These Rules Exist

Both cervical spine injury and clinically important brain injury after blunt trauma are uncommon relative to how often clinicians worry about them, roughly 2 to 5 percent of imaged patients for the spine, single digits for the head. Imaging everyone is safe but expensive, slow, and exposes a lot of people to radiation for no reason. Four validated rules exist to answer the same underlying question in two different regions of the body: which patients can skip the scan entirely, based on history and exam alone?

For the cervical spine: NEXUS and the Canadian C-Spine Rule (CCR).

For the head: the Canadian CT Head Rule (CCHR) and the New Orleans Criteria (NOC).

Within each pair, one rule is a flat checklist and the other adds a tiered or functional test, and that structural difference is what drives most of the performance gap. None of the four are interchangeable with each other, and the differences between them matter more than any one rule alone.

02

NEXUS Criteria

From Hoffman et al, NEJM 2000: 34,069 patients across 21 US hospitals. If all five criteria are absent, the cervical spine can be clinically cleared without imaging.

  • No posterior midline cervical tenderness
  • Normal alertness
  • No intoxication
  • No focal neurologic deficit
  • No painful distracting injury
Sensitivity99.6% (derivation/validation cohort)
Specificity12.9%
Age applicabilityNo formal age cutoff, theoretically all ages over 1 year

The low specificity is the tradeoff for simplicity: NEXUS is easy to apply but clears comparatively few patients, since any one positive criterion mandates imaging.

03

Canadian C-Spine Rule

From Stiell et al, JAMA 2001: 8,924 patients across Canadian emergency departments. Built specifically for alert (GCS 15), hemodynamically stable trauma patients. It works as a three-tier decision tree rather than a single checklist.

Step 1, high-risk factors (any one mandates imaging): age 65 or older, dangerous mechanism (fall from 3+ feet/5 stairs, axial load to head, high-speed MVC, rollover, ejection, motorized recreational vehicle, bicycle collision), or paresthesias in the extremities.
↓ none present
Step 2, low-risk factors (any one allows range-of-motion testing): simple rear-end MVC, sitting position in the ED, ambulatory at any point since injury, delayed onset of neck pain, absence of midline C-spine tenderness.
↓ at least one present
Step 3, range of motion: can the patient actively rotate their neck 45 degrees left and right? If yes, no imaging needed. If no, or if none of the low-risk factors are present, image.
Sensitivity100% (derivation cohort)
Specificity42.5%
Age applicabilityAlert, stable trauma patients only; age 65+ is itself a high-risk criterion requiring imaging
04

Spine Rules Head-to-Head

Stiell et al ran the only prospective direct comparison, NEJM 2003: 8,283 patients across nine Canadian EDs, both rules applied by the same treating physicians before imaging.

MetricCCRNEXUS
Sensitivity99.4%90.7%
Specificity45.1%36.8%
Would-be imaging rate55.9%66.6%

Both differences were statistically significant (P<0.001). In this head-to-head, the CCR caught more injuries and ordered fewer scans doing it.

05

Why CCR Outperforms NEXUS

The gap isn't about one rule being smarter. It's structural.

NEXUS is a flat checklist: any one positive finding sends the patient to imaging, with no further discrimination. It can't distinguish a truly dangerous mechanism from a minor one once tenderness or distraction is present.

CCR adds a functional test, active range of motion, for the intermediate group who have a low-risk factor but aren't automatically clear. That extra step lets it release more true negatives without missing true positives, which is exactly why its specificity and would-be imaging rate both beat NEXUS at a comparable sensitivity.

The cost is complexity. CCR takes longer to learn and apply correctly, and range-of-motion testing on a trauma patient makes some clinicians uneasy even when the rule says it's appropriate.

06

Limitations & Pitfalls

NEXUS in the elderly: despite no formal age cutoff, sensitivity drops in patients over 65, reported as low as 66 to 84% in some cohorts, against 99.6% overall. A large retrospective study of 231,018 patients still found sensitivity holding at 94.8% (95% CI 92.1-96.7%), so the picture is mixed, but caution in the elderly is warranted either way.

CCR structurally excludes what NEXUS includes: CCR only applies to alert (GCS 15), hemodynamically stable patients, and treats age 65+ as an automatic imaging indication rather than something to be reasoned through. It also excludes penetrating trauma, known vertebral disease, pregnancy, and acute paralysis.

Neither rule applies to: unconscious or intubated patients, children under 16 (NEXUS has pediatric data but weaker support; CCR was not derived in children at all), or patients presenting more than 48 hours after injury.

"Distracting injury" and "intoxication" in NEXUS are not rigidly defined. The original study left this to clinician judgment, which is part of why NEXUS specificity is so low: different clinicians apply the criteria differently.

07

Canadian CT Head Rule

From Stiell et al, The Lancet 2001: a prospective cohort of 3,121 patients across 10 Canadian centres. Built for minor head injury, defined as witnessed loss of consciousness, definite amnesia, or witnessed disorientation, with a GCS of 13 to 15. Same two-tier logic as the CCR: a high-risk tier that flags who needs neurosurgery, and a wider medium-risk tier layered on top that flags who needs a CT at all.

High-risk factors (any one predicts need for neurosurgical intervention, mandates CT):

  • GCS under 15 at 2 hours after injury
  • Suspected open or depressed skull fracture
  • Any sign of basal skull fracture (hemotympanum, raccoon eyes, CSF otorrhea or rhinorrhea, Battle's sign)
  • Vomiting, 2 or more episodes
  • Age 65 or older

Medium-risk factors (added on top of the high-risk list; any one predicts clinically important brain injury on CT):

  • Amnesia before impact greater than 30 minutes
  • Dangerous mechanism (pedestrian struck by vehicle, occupant ejected from vehicle, fall from height greater than 3 feet or 5 stairs)

Excludes patients under 16, those on anticoagulants in the original derivation, obvious open skull fracture on presentation, seizure before assessment, and anyone returning for reassessment of the same head injury.

08

New Orleans Criteria

From Haydel et al, NEJM 2000: 520 patients derivation, 909 patients prospective validation. Narrower population than CCHR: adults with a normal GCS of 15 and a normal neurologic exam, with witnessed LOC, definite amnesia, or witnessed disorientation. Structured as a single flat checklist, like NEXUS: any one criterion present means CT.

  • Headache
  • Vomiting, any episode
  • Age over 60
  • Drug or alcohol intoxication
  • Deficits in short-term memory (persistent anterograde amnesia)
  • Physical evidence of trauma above the clavicles
  • Seizure
Sensitivity100% in the original derivation and validation cohorts; pooled meta-analysis estimates 97.2 to 98.3%
SpecificityRoughly 25% in the derivation cohort; pooled meta-analysis estimates 8.5 to 12.3%
Age applicabilityDerived across ages 3 to 97, though anticoagulated and GCS <15 patients were underrepresented

"Headache" and "vomiting" alone will flag a large share of any minor head injury population. NOC is intentionally loose: it trades away specificity for a simple, hard-to-miss checklist.

09

Head Rules Head-to-Head

Stiell et al ran the direct comparison, JAMA 2005: 2,707 adults across nine Canadian EDs, narrowed to a 1,822-patient subgroup with GCS 15 to allow a fair comparison against NOC, which was derived only in that population. 8 patients (0.4%) needed neurosurgical intervention; 97 (5.3%) had a clinically important brain injury.

MetricCCHRNOC
Sensitivity, neurosurgical intervention100%100%
Specificity, neurosurgical intervention76.3%12.1%

Both rules found in this comparison caught every case that mattered. The entire practical difference is specificity: CCHR would have sent far fewer people to CT for the same safety margin. A broader pooled meta-analysis (14 studies, 21,140 patients) found a similar pattern for clinically important brain injury more generally: CCHR sensitivity 89.8 to 92.5% and specificity 38 to 40%, versus NOC sensitivity 97.2 to 98.3% and specificity 8.5 to 12.3%. NOC's extra sensitivity in that broader estimate comes at a steep specificity cost.

Where either rule is contraindicated, CCHR excludes anticoagulated and pediatric patients; NOC does not carry those exclusions, which is part of why it still gets reached for in practice.

Which spine rule is simpler to apply?NEXUS, a flat five-item checklist

Bottom lineAlert, stable, cooperative patient: CCR for the neck, CCHR for the head, both trade a little simplicity for meaningfully fewer scans.
