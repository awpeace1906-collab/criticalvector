01

The Normal Waveform

Capnography measures CO2 partial pressure continuously through the respiratory cycle, producing a waveform (capnogram) with four distinct phases per breath. EtCO2 — the value most people quote — is just the number read at the end of the plateau, phase III.

PhaseWhat's happening
0 (baseline)Inspiration — CO2 should read zero on a functioning circuit.
I (dead space)Start of exhalation — CO2-free gas from the anatomic dead space (conducting airways) is expelled first.
II (alveolar upstroke)CO2-rich alveolar gas begins mixing with the dead-space gas; the steep rise.
III (alveolar plateau)Relatively uniform alveolar gas is exhaled; EtCO2 is read at the very end of this phase, the point closest to alveolar (and by extension, arterial) CO2.

Normal values (adult): EtCO2 35–45 mmHg. PaCO2 typically exceeds EtCO2 by 2–5 mmHg (the a-ET gradient) due to dead-space ventilation — a gradient that widens meaningfully in conditions that increase dead space, like PE or severe COPD.

EtCO2 is a ventilation, perfusion, and metabolism monitor simultaneously — a sudden change can mean any of the three shifted, which is exactly why the waveform shape matters as much as the number.

Abnormal Patterns

PatternAppearanceCause
Obstructive ("shark fin")Upsloping phase II, no clean plateau, "scooped" phase IIIBronchospasm — asthma, COPD, partial ETT obstruction/kink
HypoventilationElevated EtCO2 (>45), normal shapeSedation, opioids, rebreathing, hyperthermia or malignant hyperthermia, deliberately permissive settings
HyperventilationDecreased EtCO2 (<30), normal shapePain, anxiety, sepsis, pregnancy, compensatory response to metabolic acidosis; hypothermia lowers EtCO2 by reducing CO2 production
Curare cleftSmall notch/dip mid-phase IISpontaneous respiratory effort breaking through incomplete paralysis — a real-time paralysis-depth signal
Cardiogenic oscillationsRegular small ripples on the plateau Mechanical transmission of cardiac contraction to the airway — usually a reassuring sign of adequate cardiac output
RebreathingBaseline doesn't return to zeroExhausted CO2 absorber, absorber not properly attached, low fresh gas flow, circuit leak
Sudden fall to near-zeroAbrupt loss of waveformEsophageal intubation, complete disconnection or extubation, or — the one to never forget — cardiac arrest

The dangerous confusion: a flat capnography line does not automatically mean esophageal intubation. If the tube was confirmed correctly placed moments earlier, an abrupt flatline is a circulatory arrest until proven otherwise — chest compressions restore some waveform even in a correctly placed tube, so a flat line during ongoing CPR (rather than at a pulse check) is a different problem than a flat line right after intubation.

03

Capnography in Cardiac Arrest

ETCO2 during CPR is primarily a marker of pulmonary blood flow, and therefore a real-time, breath-by-breath readout of compression quality and cardiac output — arguably its single highest-yield use in resuscitation.

FindingEvidence
Low threshold, poor prognosisEtCO2 <10 mmHg after 20 minutes of CPR has been associated with as low as a 0.5% likelihood of ROSC across pooled observational data.
ILCOR 2020 consensusSuggests against using any EtCO2 cutoff value alone to predict mortality or to decide to stop resuscitation (weak recommendation, low-certainty evidence). Values above about 20 mmHg are associated with better odds, but no single number is decisive.
Trend over thresholdMultiple reviews now emphasize that trending EtCO2 (rising, falling, abrupt jump) carries more prognostic weight than any single static value, given how many confounders (epinephrine dosing, airway type, compression quality itself) affect the absolute number.
Abrupt rise during CPRA sudden jump in EtCO2 mid-resuscitation is one of the most reliable non-invasive signals that ROSC has just occurred — often visible before a pulse check confirms it.

Confirmed CO2 detection after intubation remains the single most specific bedside method to confirm correct tracheal (versus esophageal) tube placement during CPR — that role is settled; the prognostic-threshold role is genuinely still being refined.

04

Clinical Applications by Setting

Traumatic brain injury: target EtCO2 35–40 mmHg. Avoid hyperventilation outside of brief, deliberate use for acute herniation with Cushing's reflex — cerebral vasoconstriction from over-ventilation can worsen ischemia in an already injured brain.

Post-ROSC: target EtCO2 35–45 mmHg — avoid both hyper- and hypoventilation in the immediate post-arrest period, when cerebral autoregulation is impaired.

Sepsis / DKA / other compensatory tachypnea: match the ventilator to what the patient's own respiratory drive is already doing rather than "normalizing" a compensatory hyperventilation — a metabolic acidosis being compensated by a low PaCO2 needs that low PaCO2 maintained, not corrected.

Procedural sedation: capnography detects hypoventilation before it becomes hypoxemia — often the earliest available warning sign, arriving before the pulse oximeter changes at all, especially with supplemental oxygen running.

If you're bagging a patient without capnography, you're ventilating blind — the waveform (or its absence) is the fastest confirmation of both airway position and adequacy of ventilation available at the bedside.
