AID-ICU: Does Haloperidol Actually Help ICU Delirium?

Andersen-Ranberg NC, Poulsen LM, Perner A, et al; AID-ICU Trial Group. Haloperidol for the Treatment of Delirium in ICU Patients.

N Engl J Med. 2022;387(26):2425–2435. doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2211868 · Original social summary: RebelEM, plus CAM-ICU algorithm reference card

Verdict on the source post: the RebelEM caption asks the right question and links the right primary trial, but leaves the actual answer as a cliffhanger ("find out here"). This audit gives the answer AID-ICU found — plus a follow-up analysis published a year later that meaningfully complicates it, and two prior trials that set up why AID-ICU was needed in the first place.
01

The Setup

Haloperidol is the most commonly used ICU delirium drug in the world. Until AID-ICU, the evidence for it was thin.

Delirium affects an estimated 30–50% of ICU patients and is associated with longer ICU stay, longer mechanical ventilation, and worse long-term cognitive outcomes. Haloperidol has been the default pharmacologic treatment for decades largely on the strength of clinical tradition rather than trial data. Two prior trials had already cast doubt: HOPE-ICU found early prophylactic haloperidol did not prevent delirium from developing, and MIND-USA (2018, n=566) found no difference in days alive without delirium or coma with haloperidol or ziprasidone versus placebo — and haloperidol nearly reached statistical significance for worse 90-day mortality (38% vs. 34%, OR 1.17, 95% CI 0.99–1.40). AID-ICU was designed as a larger, dedicated test of haloperidol specifically for treating delirium once it's already present, using CAM-ICU or ICDSC to confirm the diagnosis first — the same algorithm shown in the companion post.

03

The Frequentist Result: Negative

The primary, published NEJM finding

Treatment with haloperidol did not lead to a significantly greater number of days alive and out of hospital at 90 days than placebo. On the standard frequentist analysis that made the NEJM headline, this was a negative trial.

04

The Part Most Summaries Skip: the Bayesian Reanalysis

A pre-planned secondary analysis, published a year later, tells a meaningfully different story from the same dataset.

The AID-ICU investigators had pre-specified a Bayesian reanalysis of their own trial, published in Intensive Care Medicine in 2023. Using weakly informative priors, adjusted Bayesian regression on the same 987 patients found:

OutcomeResult
Days alive and out of hospital (primary outcome)Mean difference +2.9 days (95% CrI −1.1 to 6.9); 92% probability of any benefit, 82% probability of clinically important benefit
Mortality (risk difference)−6.8 percentage points (95% CrI −12.8 to −0.8); 99% probability of any benefit, 94% probability of clinically important benefit
Serious adverse reactions+0.3 percentage points (95% CrI −1.3 to 1.9); 98% probability of no clinically important difference
Two statistically legitimate readings of the same trial

The frequentist analysis says "no significant difference." The pre-planned Bayesian analysis of the identical dataset says "high probability of clinically important benefit, including on mortality." Both are valid statistical approaches to the same 987 patients — the discrepancy comes from how each framework treats uncertainty and prior information, not from a data error. This is exactly the kind of nuance a single-slide "how effective is haloperidol" caption can't hold, and it's the single most important thing missing from the original post.

Read across all four, the picture is genuinely unsettled rather than clearly negative: the largest, best-designed trial to date produced a null frequentist result but a favorable Bayesian one, while the prior trial (MIND-USA) leaned toward possible harm. That's not a clean story, and any single-post summary claiming certainty in either direction is oversimplifying.

06

Bottom Line

The RebelEM post correctly identifies AID-ICU as the trial to know here and correctly links the CAM-ICU algorithm as the diagnostic step that has to happen first. What it leaves as an exercise for the reader is the actual finding — and the honest answer is two-part: the primary, published frequentist result was negative, but the trial's own pre-planned Bayesian reanalysis of the same patients found a high probability of meaningful benefit, including on mortality. Combined with MIND-USA's near-significant harm signal beforehand, haloperidol for ICU delirium remains a genuinely open question rather than a settled negative or positive — treat any confident claim in either direction with the same skepticism this audit is applying to the source post.
