Whole Blood vs Components: The Meta-Analysis Says Yes, the Trials Say Not Yet

A close read of Ibrahim et al. in JAMA Surgery (2026), set against the two large randomized trials that arrived after its search ended, the major cohort studies, and the safety data.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · November 2026. Prompted by Ibrahim W, Meza Monge K, Menzel J, et al. JAMA Surg 2026;161(5):497–506. This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the original creator.

Verdict: the meta-analysis finds lower civilian 24-hour mortality with whole blood (OR 0.73), almost entirely from observational studies, with a prediction interval of 0.28–1.91. Its search ended in June 2025, before SWiFT (n = 942) and TOWAR (n = 1,020) were published in 2026. Neither trial found a benefit from prehospital whole blood. Whole blood is safe and logistically useful; a survival advantage over balanced component therapy is not established.

01

What is actually being compared

“Whole blood versus components” sounds like two products. In practice it is three different comparisons, and the literature mixes them.

ProductWhat it isWhere the evidence comes from
Low-titer group O whole blood (LTOWB)Cold-stored, uncrossmatched, anti-A/anti-B titers below a set threshold (TOWAR used <256), shelf life up to 21 days. Contains cold-stored platelets and less additive solution than the equivalent components.Almost all modern civilian data; SWiFT and TOWAR
Modified whole bloodLeukoreduced with a platelet-sparing filter, or platelets removed. Cotton’s 2013 pilot used this; the product is no longer in routine use.Cotton 2013 RCT
Fresh whole bloodCollected from a walking blood bank and transfused warm within hours.Military series; not comparable to cold-stored LTOWB
Balanced componentsRed cells, plasma and platelets given near 1:1:1. PROPPR set this as the standard: fewer exsanguination deaths (9.2% vs 14.6%) and more hemostasis than 1:1:2, without a significant mortality difference.PROPPR 2015
The hidden variable

In nearly every observational study the “whole blood” group is whole blood plus components, compared with components alone. In TOWAR, 31.6% of the whole-blood arm also got components, and 59.1% of the component arm got both red cells and plasma. The real comparison is often one balanced strategy against another.

Ibrahim and colleagues (University of Colorado) used restricted maximum likelihood with the Hartung–Knapp adjustment, which widens confidence intervals when studies disagree, and they reported prediction intervals, which most meta-analyses in this field have not. Those are good choices, and they deserve credit. Other pooled results: civilian 30-day mortality OR 0.76 (0.60–0.98) and 2.66 fewer units transfused (95% CI 1.35–3.96). The 4.6-point absolute risk reduction (NNT 22) assumes a control-group 24-hour mortality of 20%. One methods choice matters more than the rest: every pooled analysis used each study’s unadjusted counts.

The authors called the evidence moderate certainty and recommended “selective” adoption in civilian centers with better patient selection. Their own prediction interval is the strongest argument for that caution.

Where the meta-analysis is weaker than its headline

The I² of 87–89% says the studies are not measuring one shared effect, and three studies with ORs of 0.21–0.36 (Feinberg, Dilday, Hazelton 2022) carry much of the signal.

04

Why observational studies keep finding a benefit

Two of the most-cited single-center studies show the problem plainly. In both, crude survival was identical. The advantage appeared only after statistical adjustment.

73% vs 74%
Williams 2020, crude survival
LTOWB vs components, Houston. After adjustment: OR 2.19 (1.01–4.76) for survival. The WB group arrived sicker (lactate 5.1 vs 3.5).
75% vs 76%
Brill 2022, crude survival
1,377 patients. After logistic regression: OR 4.10 for survival. WB patients had higher ISS (27 vs 20) and lactate.

Adjusting for greater injury severity can legitimately unmask a benefit. It can also manufacture one. Three biases recur:

  • Survivor (immortal-time) bias. A patient must live long enough to receive whole blood, and units are limited. Patients who die in the first minutes tend to land in the component group. Torres et al. excluded deaths within 1 hour, which helps but does not remove it.
  • Confounding by era and system. Centers that adopt LTOWB also tend to adopt prehospital blood, faster MTP activation, REBOA and TXA protocols. Before-and-after comparisons credit all of it to the bag.
  • Exposure defined as “any whole blood.” One unit among twenty components counts as a whole-blood patient.

The largest prospective multicenter cohort favoring whole blood, Hazelton’s 14-center study of 1,623 patients, reported that whole-blood recipients were 48% less likely to die after adjustment for center, age, sex, mechanism and ISS. It enrolled anyone transfused during initial resuscitation, not only massive transfusion, and the abstract does not give the mortality time point. Shock index and prehospital physiology were not in that model, though the whole-blood group arrived with a higher shock index (0.98 vs 0.83).

Not every large cohort found the benefit. SWAT (7 centers, 1,051 patients, prospective) found no adjusted difference overall (4-hour RR 0.90, 0.59–1.39) and a benefit only in a post hoc high-risk subgroup. A 2025 TQIP propensity-matched analysis of 4,434 patients found fewer complications with whole blood but no difference in in-hospital mortality. The 2024 EAST meta-analysis of 21 studies found no mortality association, only less red cell and plasma use.

What the 2026 trials do and don’t settle

At the dose tested, up to two units before hospital arrival, cold-stored whole blood did no better than red cells and plasma. That is as far as the trials go. Neither was built as an equivalence trial, and both confidence intervals still allow a clinically meaningful difference in either direction. The NEJM editorial (Rowell and Rogers) makes three further points:

  • Lower mortality than planned for. Thirty-day mortality of 24–28% is well below the PAMPer era, so any true difference is probably smaller than either trial could detect. SWiFT assumed a 68% event rate and observed about 48%.
  • Few patients died of bleeding. In SWiFT, uncontrolled hemorrhage caused 11.5% of whole-blood deaths and 10.8% of standard-care deaths; traumatic brain injury caused 26.4% and 37.8%. In TOWAR, hemorrhage was a cause of death in 9.0% vs 7.8% of patients. A hemostatic product cannot show its worth in patients who die of something else.
  • Not the same product. SWiFT used a platelet-sparing filter and uniform, platelet-replete units. TOWAR accepted local practice, a mix of conventionally leukoreduced and non-leukoreduced units. Neither is the warm fresh whole blood behind the military data.

Neither trial tested whole blood as the backbone of an in-hospital massive transfusion, where the mechanistic case is strongest. TROOP is the trial built for that question.

06

The case for whole blood that survives the trials

1

Logistics

One bag instead of three, one cooler instead of two, cold-stored platelets available where platelets otherwise aren’t. This is the strongest argument, and it doesn’t need a mortality benefit to justify a prehospital or rural program.

2

Staying balanced

A pooled analysis of 4,858 patients from four cohorts found that ratio drift begins to cost lives after the first cooler: with whole blood available, separation appeared beyond 6 red cell units (11–20 units: 17% vs 31% mortality in SWAT). Whole blood makes 1:1:1 the default rather than something to track.

3

Less product

Fewer units transfused is the most consistent signal across the meta-analyses (EAST and Ibrahim) and the pilot RCTs. Fewer donor exposures is a real if modest good.

4

Hemostatic quality

Less dilution from additive solution, cold-stored platelets with preserved function. Supported by laboratory and TEG data, but SWiFT’s higher rate of prolonged PT in the whole-blood arm is a reminder that two units do not correct a consumptive coagulopathy by themselves.

Safety

ClaimVerdictNote
Hemolysis in non-group O recipientsVerifiedNot seen with modest volumes of LTOWB in adult or pediatric series (Dishong 2021 review; Williams 2020 serial hemolysis panels). Most programs cap the number of uncrossmatched units before switching to type-specific product.
RhD-positive whole blood in females of childbearing potentialNeeds contextThe real trade-off. Modeled risk of fetal or neonatal death from hemolytic disease after D-alloimmunization ranges 0–6.5% depending on age and other factors, set against roughly 24% mortality from hemorrhagic shock (Yazer 2023). A 2025 JAMA Network Open guideline now covers management of alloimmunized pregnancy partly because of this practice. EAST’s conditional recommendation explicitly says it may not apply to women of childbearing age.
Thrombosis and organ failureVerifiedNo excess in SWiFT, TOWAR or the TQIP analysis; TQIP found fewer complications with whole blood.
Storage age up to 21 daysVerifiedTOWAR substudy: no outcome difference between 1–14 and 15–21 day units.
08

Bottom line

An honest summary

Whole blood is safe, practical, and makes balanced resuscitation easier to deliver. It has not been shown to save lives compared with well-run balanced component therapy. The observational signal, which the JAMA Surgery meta-analysis condenses into an OR of 0.73, comes from studies whose crude survival rates were often identical, and from a pool so heterogeneous that the prediction interval includes harm. The two large randomized trials, both published after that meta-analysis’s search window, found no prehospital benefit.

Choose whole blood for what it reliably delivers: simpler logistics, ratio by default, cold-stored platelets, fewer exposures. Don’t choose it expecting the survival effect in the abstract. Keep a plan for women of childbearing potential and a unit cap for uncrossmatched product. The in-hospital question waits on TROOP.

SettingWhere the evidence points
Prehospital, short transportEither product. Two RCTs, no difference. Pick by logistics and supply.
Prehospital, long or austereWhole blood has the stronger practical case (platelets, weight, one bag); no trial tested this setting directly.
ED and OR massive transfusionBalanced components remain the evidence-based standard (PROPPR). Whole blood is a reasonable way to deliver the same ratio; mortality effect unproven pending TROOP.
ICU, ongoing bleedingGoal-directed component therapy. No whole-blood data.
