The HA-Study: Low-Dose Haloperidol for Acute Benign Headache

Verdict on the source post: every number on the infographic checks out against the primary trial. This audit adds two things the card format can't: where this trial sits relative to a decade of prior dopamine-antagonist-for-headache studies (this is not the first time this class has worked), and a real discrepancy between who the trial enrolled and who REBEL EM's own conclusion recommends treating.
01

The Setup

Headache is one of the most common ED complaints, and the standard cocktail (NSAIDs, metoclopramide, diphenhydramine) leaves a meaningful fraction of patients under-treated with long lengths of stay.

Dopamine antagonists for headache aren't new — droperidol and prochlorperazine both have prior positive RCT data in this space, and even haloperidol itself had already been shown roughly equivalent to metoclopramide (Gaffigan et al., 2015) and effective as migraine rescue therapy (Honkaniemi et al., 2006) before this trial. What THE-HA Study adds is the first RCT testing a reduced 2.5 mg IV dose specifically, in a broader "undifferentiated benign headache" population rather than migraine-only, without routine diphenhydramine pretreatment.

02

Design, Confirmed

DesignSingle-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
Population287 screened; 118 enrolled, ages 13–55, acute benign headache
Groups58 received 2.5 mg IV haloperidol; 60 received 5 mL 0.9% saline placebo
Primary outcomePain reduction (VAS) at 60 minutes
MonitoringVAS and vitals at 30, 60, 90 minutes; QTc at baseline and discharge
ExclusionsBP >200/100, sudden/thunderclap onset, fever or altered mental status, acute trauma, history of brain mass/stroke or abnormal intracranial anatomy, abnormal neuro exam, pregnancy, incarceration — a standard secondary-headache red-flag screen

Every figure on the REBEL EM card matches the published paper. Worth pulling out because it's easy to skim past on the infographic: the benefit wasn't just faster acute relief — the haloperidol group also had a lower rate of headache recurrence at 24 hours (32.7% vs. 50.9%), which is a durability signal, not just a speed signal.

04

The Discrepancy: Who Was Enrolled vs. Who's Recommended

This is worth catching before it becomes a protocol.

Trial enrollment: ages 13–55. REBEL EM's own conclusion: ages 18–55.

The infographic's clinical question and methods both correctly state the trial enrolled patients 13 to 55 years old. But REBEL EM's own conclusion box recommends the treatment "in patients b/w ages of 18–55" — narrower than what was actually studied. This isn't a REBEL EM error so much as a faithful carry-through of the original authors' own hedge: the published paper's conclusion explicitly states adolescent results need further confirmation, implying the 13–17 subgroup wasn't independently powered or fully reassuring on its own. If you're building a protocol off this infographic, don't extend it to adolescents based on the trial's inclusion criteria alone — the authors and REBEL EM both stop at 18 for a reason that isn't fully spelled out in the card.

05

Why the Low Dose Is the Actual Story

2.5 mg is half the typical prior study dose, and that's not incidental to the results.

Most prior ED haloperidol-for-headache literature used 5 mg with routine diphenhydramine pretreatment to blunt extrapyramidal side effects. THE-HA Study's authors specifically attribute their low observed rates of akathisia and sedation — and the fact that somnolence wasn't observed at all — to the halved 2.5 mg dose, concluding that routine diphenhydramine pretreatment may be unnecessary at this dose. That's a meaningful practice point the infographic's side-effect row (14.6% vs. 7%, "uncommon, benign, easily treated") doesn't fully unpack: the low side-effect rate isn't just a lucky result, it's a direct consequence of the dose-reduction design choice.

06

Bottom Line

The REBEL EM infographic is accurate on every number and appropriately restrained in its conclusion (18–55, not the full 13–55 enrolled range). This adds two things worth carrying into practice: first, low-dose (2.5 mg) IV haloperidol for undifferentiated benign headache isn't an isolated finding — it extends a decade-plus of positive dopamine-antagonist headache trials, with the dose reduction itself likely explaining the low side-effect burden. Second, don't extrapolate to adolescents on the strength of the trial's inclusion criteria; both the original authors and REBEL EM's own conclusion box are more conservative than the enrollment range would suggest, and that's worth respecting rather than smoothing over.
