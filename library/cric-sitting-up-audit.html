Can We Cric a Patient Sitting Up? The Numbers Behind "Train What You'll Do"

A combat medic educator asks whether to cric in the position of comfort or lay the patient flat. His success rates are hypothetical, as he says. The real ones make his argument for him.

R. E. Suscitate & A. Systole · October 2026. Original post: @nextgenerationcombatmedic (video and caption). This page is an independent third-party evaluation, not affiliated with or endorsed by the original creator.

Verdict: sound. The 80% and 60% figures in the caption are explicitly illustrative, not data. Published combat data back the core claim that real-world crics fail far more often than simulation suggests: 26% failed to reach the trachea in one review of Iraq and Afghanistan casualties, with medic failure rates around 33%. We found no outcome data comparing upright and supine cricothyrotomy, so the position question is a matter of judgment and training, not evidence.

What the success rates actually are

Civilian pooled estimates vs. the battlefield.

Mabry and Frankfurt reported that the military medic failure rate was three to five times higher than comparable civilian studies. Most patients in both combat series had major head, face or neck injuries, which is exactly the anatomy that makes positioning a live question. Survival was low in both, about one-third and one-half respectively, reflecting injury severity more than the airway alone.

02

The position question

No trial answers it; here's what does inform it.

The patient who needs a cric while sitting up is usually the one with massive maxillofacial trauma who can only keep an airway by leaning forward and letting blood drain. Current TCCC guidelines tell providers to allow a conscious casualty to assume any position that best protects the airway, including sitting up and/or leaning forward. The same guidelines direct lidocaine for surgical cricothyroidotomy in a conscious casualty, so a cric on an awake patient who has been holding their own airway upright is a scenario the doctrine anticipates. Laying that patient flat may make the anatomy easier to find while making the airway worse.

So the trade-off is real on both sides. Supine with suction gives the most familiar landmarks and hand position, but risks aspiration and loss of a self-maintained airway. Upright preserves drainage and airway, but changes neck angle, operator position and the feel of the landmarks, which is the post's point if the operator has never trained it. We found no published comparison of upright and supine cricothyrotomy success in live patients.

03

What to train

The post's recommendation, made concrete.

  1. Volume first, supine. The post's observation that many medics don't grip the larynx or handle equipment correctly matches the failure modes in the combat series: wrong anatomic placement, subcutaneous passage, main-stem tube.
  2. Higher-fidelity reps. Tissue-based or high-fidelity models over plastic necks, so landmarks and bleeding feel real.
  3. Then add position and stress. Seated, leaning forward, low light, blood in the field, once supine performance is reliable.
  4. Decision training, not just technique. Barnard et al. explicitly called for training in when to cric, not only how.

Laryngeal handshake, scalpel–bougie–tube with a vertical incision when landmarks aren't palpable (the Difficult Airway Society 2015 technique, which names scalpel cricothyroidotomy the preferred rescue and calls for regular practice), and confirming placement with end-tidal CO2 are the elements most transferable between positions.
