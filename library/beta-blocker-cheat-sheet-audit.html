Why Each Blockade Does What It Does

The mechanism column in the original post is correct; here's the physiologic reasoning underneath it.

BlockedWhy it produces that effect
B1B1 receptors are the dominant cardiac subtype; blocking them reduces cAMP-driven calcium influx in the SA node, AV node, and myocardium — slower rate, weaker contraction, slower conduction.
B2B2 receptors mediate smooth-muscle relaxation in bronchi and skeletal-muscle vasculature; blocking them removes that relaxation, which is why non-selective agents carry bronchospasm risk in reactive airway disease and can blunt the adrenergic warning signs of hypoglycemia.
A1Alpha-1 blockade removes peripheral arterial and venous vasoconstriction, dropping afterload directly — this is the mechanism that gives carvedilol and labetalol an added BP-lowering effect beyond what B1 blockade alone provides.
Na+Also called membrane-stabilizing activity (MSA): high-dose blockade of fast sodium channels slows phase-0 depolarization, widening the QRS. Clinically most relevant in beta-blocker overdose, where propranolol's combined B1/Na+ blockade makes it disproportionately dangerous compared with cardioselective agents.
K+Sotalol's potassium-channel blockade is its defining class III antiarrhythmic property (this is why it's dosed and monitored differently from other beta blockers) — it prolongs the QT interval, which is therapeutic for reentrant arrhythmias but is also the mechanism behind sotalol's dose-dependent torsades risk.
NO↑Nebivolol stimulates endothelial nitric oxide release, producing vasodilation independent of beta blockade — the mechanism behind its comparatively neutral hemodynamic and metabolic profile relative to older agents.

Where the One-Page Version Simplifies

Nothing here is wrong. These are the dose- and context-dependencies a single grid can't show.

"Cardioselective" is dose-dependent, not absolute

Metoprolol, atenolol, and bisoprolol are B1-selective at typical therapeutic doses, but that selectivity narrows and eventually disappears at higher doses — a "cardioselective" agent can still provoke bronchospasm in a severe asthmatic at high enough dosing. The chart's clean X-in-a-box format necessarily can't carry a dose-response curve.

Nebivolol's NO release is also dose- and selectivity-dependent

The vasodilatory, NO-mediated benefit is most prominent at the doses where B1 selectivity holds; at higher doses nebivolol loses selectivity and the NO-release advantage is correspondingly less clinically distinct from other agents.

Carvedilol and labetalol's alpha blockade is genuinely mild, but clinically relevant

The chart correctly labels their A1 effect "mild" rather than a full checkmark — worth noting for bedside use: this is exactly why both are reasonable choices when you want beta blockade plus some afterload reduction without the more pronounced orthostatic hypotension risk of a dedicated alpha blocker.

Bottom Line

This is an accurate, well-constructed reference chart. Every receptor assignment matches standard pharmacology teaching, and the two-column format (blocked receptor / mechanism when blocked) is a genuinely efficient way to hold this much information on one page. The additions above are context a static grid structurally can't carry — dose-dependence of selectivity, and the clinical stakes of the Na+ and K+ columns specifically in overdose and arrhythmia management, respectively.
