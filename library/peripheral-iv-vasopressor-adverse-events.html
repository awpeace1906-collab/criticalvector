Peripheral IV Vasopressors: How Risky, Really?

ZhangJian S-J, Niu K-Y, Chen C-B, Seak C-J, Yen C-C. Incidence of Adverse Events in Peripheral Intravenous Vasopressor Use: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.

JAMA Network Open. 2026;9(3):e260710. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.0710 · Chang Gung Memorial Hospital & New Taipei Municipal Tucheng Hospital, Taiwan

Why this one matters: "central line first, vasopressors second" is deeply ingrained teaching, largely built on case reports of skin necrosis from the 1950s onward. This is the largest meta-analysis to date testing whether that caution is proportionate to the actual, measured risk — 49 studies, 33,060 catheters, agent-by-agent.
01

The Setup

Delayed vasopressor initiation kills. CVC placement takes time, and isn't free of its own risk. The question is whether the peripheral route is actually as dangerous as tradition assumes.

Central venous catheters have been the traditional default for vasopressor infusion largely out of concern for extravasation and tissue necrosis — concerns that trace back partly to individual case reports (skin necrosis after IV norepinephrine, BMJ 1955; skin necrosis after vasopressin extravasation, Crit Care Med 2002). But CVC placement carries its own immediate risks (arrhythmia, vascular injury, pneumothorax) and delayed ones (DVT, bloodstream infection), and takes time to place — time during which a hypotensive patient isn't getting pressor support. The 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign formally recommended starting vasopressors peripherally rather than delaying for central access, which makes this exactly the right moment for a large, current evidence synthesis.

04

Major Adverse Events: the Whole Story Is Catheter Type

This is the paper's central, clinically actionable finding.

Catheter typeStudiesCathetersMajor AEsPooled incidence
Midline catheter41,12630 VTE1.4% (95% CI 0.4–5.4)
Short PIV catheter4329,5961 tissue necrosis0.0% (95% CI 0.0–0.0)
P<.001 between subgroups

Every single major adverse event captured across this entire 33,060-catheter dataset that wasn't a VTE in a midline catheter was one tissue necrosis case, out of nearly 30,000 short-PIV catheters. All 30 venous thromboembolism events occurred in the much smaller midline-catheter subgroup. The authors attribute this to dwell time, not the drug: midline catheters commonly stay in for over a week, while short PIV catheters are typically used for hours to days — and longer vascular device dwell time is an independently established VTE risk factor, consistent with a broader midline-catheter thrombosis literature estimating roughly 22 VTE events per 1,000 catheter-days.

05

CVC Avoidance: 60%, but Wildly Setting-Dependent

The practical payoff metric, and the one most directly relevant to your own department's protocol design.

Across 38 studies (15,371 catheters) reporting it, the pooled CVC avoidance proportion was 59.7% (95% CI 46.4–71.7), but individual studies ranged from 0% to 100%. The OR and ward settings showed significantly higher avoidance than ICU/ED/mixed settings — largely because OR peripheral pressor use is typically for transient anesthesia-related hypotension under intensive monitoring, while ED/ICU peripheral use often functions as a bridge to, rather than a replacement for, eventual central access. Midline catheters showed significantly higher CVC avoidance than short PIV (83.8% vs. 58.1%, P<.001) — the flip side of their higher VTE signal: they buy more days off a central line at the cost of measurably more clot risk.

06

Two Findings Worth Reading Carefully, Not Just Citing

  • Retrospective studies significantly underreport minor AEs compared with prospective studies and RCTs (1.5% vs. 4.5% vs. 11.1%, P<.001) — consistent with under-ascertainment of mild, self-limited events in chart-review-driven cohorts. Since 63% of the included studies were retrospective, the pooled 2.3% headline figure is likely a floor, not a ceiling, on true minor AE incidence.
  • Post-2021 studies report significantly higher minor AE rates than pre-2021 studies (3.4% vs. 1.4%, P=.048) — the authors attribute this to increased peripheral pressor adoption and reporting vigilance following the 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommendation, not to the practice becoming less safe over time.
07

Limitations, as Stated by the Authors

  • 61.2% of included studies were rated unclear or high risk of bias, mainly for lack of standardized AE ascertainment methods and missing confidence intervals in the source studies.
  • Considerable heterogeneity (I² up to 95.2% for pooled minor AEs) reflects real variation in study design, protocols, and clinical settings — this limits how confidently the pooled point estimates generalize to any single department's practice.
  • Dosage and concentration data were inconsistently reported and could not be analyzed — this review cannot tell you whether higher-dose peripheral norepinephrine carries materially more risk than lower-dose.
  • The authors are explicit: no high-quality RCT has directly compared PIV against CVC for AE outcomes, so this synthesis should not be read as license to categorically replace CVCs with PIVs — only as evidence that short-term, monitored short-PIV vasopressor use carries a low measured complication rate.
08

Bottom Line

Across the largest dataset assembled on this question to date, short peripheral IV catheters placed in proximal veins carried an essentially negligible major-complication rate (0.0%, one tissue necrosis event in ~29,600 catheters) and a low minor-complication rate (2.3% overall, mostly self-limited). The real caution flag in this whole dataset is specifically the midline catheter subgroup, where VTE risk was meaningfully higher (1.4%) — plausibly a dwell-time effect rather than a drug effect, but real enough to warrant deliberate duration limits and monitoring if midlines are your institution's chosen route. None of this replaces individualized judgment about when central access is still the right call (prolonged infusion, need for other central-line-dependent therapies, difficult peripheral access) — but for short-term, appropriately monitored pressor support via a short PIV catheter in a proximal vein, the evidence base for caution has gotten a lot thinner than the traditional teaching implies.
