Pediatric Appendicitis: Three Scores, One Diagnosis

Alvarado Score vs. Pediatric Appendicitis Score (PAS) vs. Pediatric Appendicitis Risk Calculator (pARC) — when each one actually earns its place at the bedside.

Original social summary: RebelEM — "MDCalc Wars: Pediatric Appendicitis" by Dr. Eric Steinberg (St. Joseph's EM, Paterson, NJ)

Verdict on the source post: the setting-specific framing is accurate and the pitfalls table is a genuinely useful addition most single-score explainers skip. The one number worth sharpening: pARC's discrimination advantage over PAS is even larger in real-world community-ED validation than in the original derivation cohort.
01

The Setup

Three tools, same diagnosis, three different jobs. Reaching for the wrong one in the wrong setting is the actual failure mode here — not any single tool being "bad."

Pediatric appendicitis is missed often enough, and imaged-for often enough, that risk-stratification tools carry real stakes in both directions: under-call it and a child perforates; over-image and a child gets an unnecessary CT. Alvarado was derived in adults and later applied downward to children by convention rather than pediatric-specific derivation. PAS was purpose-built for kids using pediatric-relevant exam findings. pARC is the newest and most different in kind — not a point-additive score at all, but a regression model outputting a continuous percent risk.

02

The Three Tools, Side by Side

ToolStructureBest suited for
Alvarado Score8 items, 0–10 points (adult-derived)Low-tech primary care/outpatient settings with limited labs
PAS8 pediatric-focused items, 0–10 pointsFast ED confirmation when clinical suspicion is already high
pARC7-variable logistic regression → continuous % risk (not a point score)Most individualized, accurate risk estimate; best for guiding the actual imaging decision in kids ≥5

pARC's confirmed variables: sex, age, duration of pain, guarding, pain migration, maximal right-lower-quadrant tenderness, and absolute neutrophil count (ANC) — note ANC specifically, not just a WBC count, which is what separates it from both Alvarado and PAS.

03

Performance, Confirmed and Sharpened

The carousel's "pARC is likely most accurate" claim undersells it slightly — the actual head-to-head numbers are stronger than "likely."

StudySettingpARC AUCPAS AUC
Kharbanda et al., 2018 (derivation/validation)9 pediatric EDs, academic0.85 (95% CI 0.83–0.87)0.77 (95% CI 0.75–0.80)
Cotton et al., 2019 (independent validation)11 community EDs0.89 (95% CI 0.87–0.92)0.80 (95% CI 0.77–0.82)
The number worth remembering: 49% vs. 23%

In the original validation cohort, pARC could confidently classify nearly half of patients into a clearly low-risk (<15%) or clearly high-risk (≥85%) category — where "confidently classified" means clinicians can act on the number directly. A comparably confident PAS cutoff (<3 or >8) only captured 23% of patients that decisively. That gap — not just the AUC difference — is the real practical case for pARC when it's available: it gives you a usable answer for twice as many kids.

04

The Pitfalls Slide Is the Most Valuable Part of the Post

Confirmed accurate, and worth restating because these are the specific ways each tool fails in practice, not generic caveats.

  • Alvarado: heavily weighted toward WBC, which is nonspecific — can overdiagnose appendicitis in patients with non-appendiceal inflammatory conditions. More accurate at the score extremes than for equivocal mid-range scores.
  • PAS: a score <4 does not reliably exclude appendicitis and can provide false reassurance. The cough/hop/percussion tenderness item is unreliable in young or uncooperative children. Should not be used as the sole decision tool in patients with known GI disease, pregnancy, or prior abdominal surgery.
  • pARC: validated only in children ≥5 years old with <96 hours of pain — appendicitis is rare and presents atypically below that age range, and the tool isn't designed to capture it. Requires ANC as an input, which not every point-of-care setting has readily available.
05

Bottom Line

This isn't a "which score is best" question — it's a "which score fits this setting" question, and the source post gets that framing right. Alvarado for low-resource, low-tech settings; PAS for a fast first-pass ED read, with its false-reassurance-at-low-scores caveat taken seriously; pARC, where ANC is available and the child is ≥5, for the most decision-actionable individualized risk estimate — and the real-world community-ED data makes an even stronger case for reaching for it than the original derivation numbers alone would suggest.
