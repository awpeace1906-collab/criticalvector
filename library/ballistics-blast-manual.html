Scope

This manual covers the physics and clinical consequences of penetrating ballistic trauma and blast overpressure injury, including range dependent wounding, forensic wound characterization, body armor interaction, regional anatomic trauma (neck, thorax, extremity), blast wave physics, blast traumatic brain injury, combined injury patterns, imaging workup, prehospital TCCC, damage control resuscitation, underwater blast, CBRN mass casualty triage principles, and pediatric blast and ballistic considerations. It is organized for rapid reference during trauma resuscitation, audition rotation review, or teaching. It is not a targeting, defeat, or weapons design document and contains no guidance on weapon construction, charge design, agent synthesis, or optimization. The CBRN section covers triage, decontamination, and clinical management principles only, not agent identification or effects detail. Pediatric content covers anatomic and physiologic considerations only and does not include weight-based dosing. All figures on wounding mechanics are drawn from open clinical, forensic, and military medical literature and are intended to inform diagnosis, triage, and surgical planning.

1  FUNDAMENTALS OF WOUND BALLISTICS

Energy, momentum, and what actually wounds tissue

A projectile's wounding capacity begins with kinetic energy, KE = ½mv². Velocity dominates this equation. Doubling mass doubles energy, but doubling velocity quadruples it, which is why rifle rounds at modest bullet weight outperform far heavier handgun bullets in tissue disruption. What matters clinically is how much of that energy is deposited in tissue along the wound track, not how much the bullet carried at the muzzle.

Wounding reduces to two physical mechanisms, and only two: tissue crush and tissue stretch. Crush produces the permanent cavity, the track of tissue destroyed by direct contact with the bullet and the crushing action of the stress field around it. Stretch produces the temporary cavity, a radial displacement of tissue outward from the bullet path from kinetic energy transferred during passage, which collapses back toward the permanent track within milliseconds.

Yaw, tumble, and why the bullet's path in tissue is not straight

Tissue density is roughly 800 times that of air, and the gyroscopic stability that keeps a bullet true in flight is overwhelmed almost immediately on impact. Most rifle bullets begin to yaw within the first several centimeters of tissue; once yaw exceeds roughly 90 degrees the bullet travels sideways or base first, presenting far greater frontal area and transferring energy faster. The point of maximum yaw, not the point of entry, is usually where the temporary cavity peaks.

Which tissues are hurt by stretch, and which are not

Temporary cavitation matters clinically only when it exceeds the elastic limit of the tissue involved. Elastic, low density tissue such as lung and skeletal muscle stretches and recoils with little lasting injury. Dense, low elasticity organs such as liver, spleen, kidney, and brain fracture or burst under the same cavity. Fluid or gas distended hollow structures behave like a hydraulic ram and can rupture at a distance from the bullet path.

Sectional density, ballistic coefficient, and penetration

Two related figures are often conflated with wounding potential but answer different questions entirely. Sectional density, a bullet's mass divided by its cross-sectional area, correlates with penetration depth for a given caliber: at equal velocity, a longer, heavier-for-caliber bullet retains momentum better as it crushes through tissue and tends to penetrate deeper than a shorter, lighter bullet of the same diameter. This is a property of the bullet itself and holds regardless of what it strikes. Ballistic coefficient, by contrast, describes how efficiently a bullet resists air drag in flight and governs velocity retention downrange (the basis for the range decay data in Section 2); it says nothing about behavior once the bullet reaches tissue, since air resistance and tissue resistance are governed by entirely different physics. A bullet optimized for a flat trajectory at long range is not necessarily optimized for penetration or cavitation once it arrives, and the two figures should not be used interchangeably when reasoning about wound severity.

Sectional density and ballistic coefficient concepts reflect standard exterior and terminal ballistics teaching, distinguished explicitly here because the two are frequently conflated in secondary sources.

Where the numbers come from: ballistic gelatin testing

Most of the cavitation and penetration data cited in this manual traces back to a standardized test medium rather than direct human observation. Ordnance gelatin mixed to a 10 percent concentration by weight, chilled to a specified temperature, closely approximates the density and low-velocity flow behavior of skeletal muscle and is the tissue simulant behind the modern "FBI protocol," developed after the 1986 Miami FBI shootout exposed how inconsistent ammunition testing had been across agencies. Before a gelatin block is used for testing, it must pass a calibration shot: a steel BB fired at a specified velocity must penetrate to a defined depth, or the block is discarded as improperly mixed. Standardized testing also fires through simulated clothing layers, since fabric measurably changes bullet expansion and can cause an otherwise reliable hollow point to fail to open. This matters clinically because a caliber's published "performance" is a laboratory figure obtained under controlled conditions; real wounds occur through variable clothing, at variable range, and against tissue that is not homogeneous gelatin, so gelatin test results bound the range of plausible wounding behavior rather than predicting any single patient's injury.

Ballistic gelatin protocol and FBI testing standard drawn from Hornady Manufacturing, Ballistic Gelatin Mixing Procedures Practiced by the FBI; Hornady Law Enforcement, FBI Test Protocol; and Brassfetcher, FBI Ammunition Protocol.

The hydrostatic shock controversy

Whether a bullet's pressure wave can injure tissue it never directly touches is a genuine, unresolved dispute in the wound ballistics literature, and this manual presents both positions rather than picking one. Martin Fackler, a Vietnam-era military surgeon and long-time head of the US Army's Wound Ballistics Laboratory, argued that so-called hydrostatic shock is not physically real: in his view, all clinically significant tissue damage traces back to direct crush or to temporary cavity stretch, and a review of Vietnam-era rifle wound data found no fractures or major vessel injuries that were not directly struck by the bullet. Michael and Amy Courtney, reviewing the same and additional literature, argue the opposite: that a bullet's pressure wave can produce measurable remote effects, including central nervous system and vascular injury at a distance from the direct path, particularly with higher energy rifle rounds, and they point to a later reanalysis of the same Vietnam data that documented indirect fractures and organ effects the original review had not emphasized. Both sides agree the effect, if real, requires substantial energy transfer and is far more plausible with high-velocity rifle wounds than with typical handgun rounds; the dispute is about magnitude and mechanism, not about whether energy transfer matters at all. Practically, this disagreement does not change acute management: the same thorough exam for occult injury near a high-energy wound track applies whether the mechanism is temporary cavity strain, a genuine pressure wave effect, or both.

Hydrostatic shock debate summarized from Courtney & Courtney, History and Evidence Regarding Hydrostatic Shock, and Scientific Evidence for "Hydrostatic Shock" (arXiv); Fackler's counter-position from Fackler, Wound ballistics: a review of common misconceptions (JAMA 1988); this manual takes no position on which side is correct.

Barrel length: why the same cartridge is not a fixed number

The velocity and energy figures above assume a specific test barrel length, and the same load can wound quite differently depending on what actually fired it. A 9mm load loses roughly 70–100 fps moving from a full-size duty pistol down to a 3-inch micro-compact, and gains 200 fps or more moving up to a 16-inch pistol caliber carbine; that carbine swing alone is enough to take a 124-grain load from roughly 360 ft-lb to over 500 ft-lb of muzzle energy, a difference that changes whether a hollow point expands reliably. Rifle cartridges are even more barrel-length sensitive: 5.56x45 loses velocity rapidly out of a short-barreled rifle in the 10–11 inch class, and a round that would fragment reliably out of a 14.5- or 20-inch barrel can leave the muzzle already below the fragmentation threshold discussed above. The practical point is that a wound's severity cannot be fully predicted from cartridge and range alone; the firearm that fired it is a third variable that belongs in the same mental model.

Range dependent wounding

Retained velocity falls continuously downrange, and many wounding effects depend on crossing a velocity threshold rather than on distance itself. As a rifle bullet slows, fragmentation at a cannelure becomes markedly less reliable. The velocity at which a given bullet stops fragmenting depends on its construction; no single figure is given here because no primary source for one was confirmed. The same cartridge that produced a fragmenting, high energy wound at close range can produce a comparatively simple through and through track at extended range.

The military ball convention

Military rifle ammunition intended for use by uniformed forces is generally built as FMJ ball, a design choice shaped by longstanding international convention against expanding projectiles in international armed conflict, which is why battlefield gunshot wounds and many civilian handgun wounds differ systematically in construction, independent of caliber.

6  BODY ARMOR AND BEHIND ARMOR BLUNT TRAUMA

Body armor that stops penetration does not stop all kinetic energy. Behind armor blunt trauma (BABT) results when the armor's rear face deforms rapidly and transmits a stress wave, plus shear deformation of the body wall, into the chest or abdomen beneath an intact vest.

  • Two combined mechanisms: a pressure/stress wave from rapid initial armor back face motion, and shear deformation of viscera from gross body wall deflection.
  • Reported injuries include rib fracture, pulmonary contusion, and rarely direct cardiac injury; systematic review found no confirmed BABT fatalities from properly rated soft armor, though severe case reports exist.
  • An intact vest with no penetration does not exclude significant intrathoracic or intra-abdominal injury; treat a reported high energy hit on worn armor as blunt trauma of unclear mechanism.
  • Experimental work implicates BABT as a possible transmission pathway for remote brain injury via a thoracic mechanism (Section 13).

Mechanism and injury pattern drawn from Cannon L, Behind Armour Blunt Trauma: An Emerging Problem, J R Army Med Corps 2001; NCBI Bookshelf systematic review of BABT (DARE); and Courtney & Courtney, A Thoracic Mechanism of Mild Traumatic Brain Injury Due to Blast Pressure Waves.

7  PENETRATING NECK TRAUMA

The neck concentrates airway, vascular, and aerodigestive structures in a small space with minimal protection, so a small entry wound can carry a large differential. Historically the neck was divided into three zones to guide surgical versus selective management, though a penetrating track can cross zones and the "no zone" approach (CT angiography driven regardless of zone) has gained favor at many centers.

Sign categoryFindings
Hard signsShock, pulsatile bleeding or expanding hematoma, audible bruit/thrill, and loss of pulse with a stable or evolving neurologic deficit. Mandates immediate operative exploration or urgent definitive intervention; do not delay for imaging.

CT angiography has sensitivity around 90–100 percent for major vascular injury and is now the default screening study in stable patients with soft signs or proximity findings; physical exam alone is highly sensitive for vascular injury but less reliable for aerodigestive injury, so maintain a low threshold for esophagoscopy, esophagography, or bronchoscopy when aerodigestive injury is suspected despite a negative CTA.

8  THORACIC TRAUMA AND REBOA ZONES

Tension pneumothorax and cardiac tamponade

Both are immediately treatable causes of obstructive shock from penetrating (and sometimes blunt) chest trauma, and both are clinical diagnoses that should not wait for imaging in an unstable patient. Tension pneumothorax presents with hypotension, absent or decreased unilateral breath sounds, distended neck veins, and often tracheal deviation away from the affected side; treatment is immediate needle or finger decompression followed by tube thoracostomy. Pericardial tamponade from a penetrating cardiac wound presents with hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad, often incomplete), and in a penetrating chest wound with cardiac box involvement and shock, resuscitative thoracotomy or emergent pericardiocentesis/sternotomy may be the only interventions fast enough to matter.

REBOA: zone selection

Resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta is a bridge to definitive hemostasis for non-compressible torso or junctional hemorrhage, used to preserve cerebral and cardiac perfusion while surgical or interventional control is arranged.

9  MANGLED EXTREMITY AND VASCULAR INJURY

Ballistic and blast trauma to an extremity raises the salvage-versus-amputation question early, often before full imaging is available. The Mangled Extremity Severity Score (MESS) remains the most widely applied bedside tool, though its predictive value is better established for identifying limbs that can be salvaged (low score) than for mandating amputation on a high score.

MESS componentScoring concept
Skeletal / soft tissue injuryGraded 1–4 from low energy (stab, simple fracture) to high energy or mass casualty crush/blast with gross contamination.
Limb ischemiaGraded 1–3 (doubled if ischemia duration exceeds 6 hours), from a pulse-reduced but perfused limb to a pulseless, cool, paralyzed, insensate limb.
ShockGraded 0–2 from normotensive to persistent hypotension in the field or on arrival.
AgeGraded 0–2, increasing with age given lower physiologic reserve.

A MESS of 7 or higher is the most commonly cited cutoff associated with amputation, but multiple validation studies, including combat casualty data, show only modest sensitivity for this cutoff and caution against using it as an automatic indication; the decision remains a clinical one weighing mechanism, ischemia time, associated injuries, and available reconstructive resources. Blast and crush mechanisms, polytrauma, and massive transfusion requirement are independently associated with higher amputation rates even when the MESS itself performs imperfectly as a predictor.

MESS components and validation limitations drawn from Kauvar et al., Utility of the Mangled Extremity Severity Score in Predicting Amputation in Military Lower Extremity Arterial Injury, Annals of Vascular Surgery; and EAST Landmark Papers, Mangled Extremity (Bosse et al. and Helfet et al.).

10  BLAST INJURY: MECHANISM TAXONOMY

Explosive injury is classified by the Department of Defense taxonomy into five mechanisms, primary through quinary. A single casualty commonly sustains more than one mechanism simultaneously, and the blast environment shifts which mechanism dominates.

MechanismCauseCharacteristic injuries
PrimaryDirect interaction of the blast overpressure wave with the body, concentrated at air-tissue interfaces.Tympanic membrane rupture, blast lung, bowel perforation, air embolism, globe rupture.
SecondaryEnergized fragments, casing shrapnel, or environmental debris propelled by the explosion.Penetrating and blunt trauma from projectiles; the most common cause of blast related death overall.
TertiaryDisplacement of the entire body by blast wind, followed by impact.Blunt trauma, traumatic amputation, closed head injury, fractures.
QuaternaryAll other direct effects of the explosion: thermal, toxic, or crush.Burns, inhalation injury, crush syndrome, exacerbation of chronic disease.
QuinaryPost-detonation environmental contaminants added to or released by the device.Toxic, chemical, radiologic, or biological contamination superimposed on the blast wound.

Taxonomy per DoD Directive 6025.21E, summarized in the Blast Injury Research Coordinating Office reference and Kim & Nguyen, Blast Injuries: A Review.

Open air detonations disperse the pressure wave quickly and produce proportionally more secondary fragmentation injury. Confined space detonations trap and reflect the overpressure wave, driving a higher proportion of primary blast lung and bowel injury. Structural collapse adds heavy quaternary crush injury and delayed extraction.

11  BLAST WAVE PHYSICS: OVERPRESSURE AND SCALED DISTANCE

A high explosive detonation produces a shock front that outruns the local speed of sound, a brief negative pressure phase, and a return to ambient. Peak overpressure at any point falls off steeply, not linearly, with distance. Blast engineering describes this with the Hopkinson-Cranz, or cube root, scaling law: scaled distance Z equals standoff distance divided by the cube root of charge mass. Because the relationship is a cube root, a tenfold increase in charge mass requires only roughly a 2.15 fold increase in standoff distance to reach an equivalent overpressure. The clinical takeaway: overpressure and injury severity fall off far faster with distance than most people intuitively expect, so relatively modest increases in standoff distance produce large reductions in primary blast injury risk, and casualty distribution from a mass casualty blast is not random, patients closest to the seat of the explosion carry a disproportionate share of primary blast injury.

Scaling law summarized from ScienceDirect Topics, Scaled Distance overview, and the Hopkinson-Cranz cube root scaling law reference (vCalc); this manual omits charge-specific coefficients and yield estimation tables, which are structural engineering and munitions safety planning tools outside clinical scope.

12  PRIMARY BLAST PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Barotrauma at density interfaces

The overpressure wave interacts most violently at interfaces between tissues of very different density, which is why air filled or air adjacent organs bear the brunt of primary injury. Three named mechanisms describe the damage: spalling (a stress wave tears the far side of a denser medium at a density boundary), implosion (compressed gas pockets collapse and rebound violently), and inertial shear (adjacent tissues of different density accelerate and decelerate at different rates and tear at their junction).

Blast lung

The pathognomonic primary blast injury and leading cause of immediate blast death among initial survivors. Alveolar hemorrhage, pneumothorax, and alveolar-capillary disruption allow air to enter the pulmonary venous circulation. Classic film findings are a bilateral, perihilar butterfly infiltrate developing within hours. A deceptively normal initial exam can deteriorate over the following minutes to hours, arguing for observation after any close range blast exposure with respiratory symptoms.

Arterial air embolism

Alveolar-venous disruption allows air to reach the left heart and systemic circulation, producing sudden focal neurologic deficit, myocardial infarction, or mesenteric ischemia with no direct injury to the affected organ. Suspect this in any blast patient with new focal neurologic or cardiac findings; consider left lateral, head down positioning and high flow oxygen while arranging transfer.

Tympanic membrane and blast bowel

TM rupture occurs at overpressures far below the threshold for lung or bowel injury and functions as a sentinel finding for meaningful exposure, though its absence does not exclude other primary injury. Blast bowel produces mucosal and submucosal hemorrhage, perforation, and mesenteric injury that can present with a delay of hours; a benign early abdominal exam does not exclude a perforation that will declare itself over the following day, supporting admission and serial exams after blast exposure with abdominal symptoms.

13  BLAST TRAUMATIC BRAIN INJURY

Blast induced traumatic brain injury (bTBI) has been the most common brain injury among survivors of recent military conflicts, yet the physical mechanism connecting an overpressure wave to neuronal injury is still contested. Three candidate mechanisms dominate the literature and are not mutually exclusive.

Proposed mechanismDescription
Direct transcranial transmissionThe pressure transient crosses the skull and propagates directly through brain parenchyma; skin and meninges may amplify the applied pressure, cranial air sinuses provide some attenuation.
Thoracic / vascular transmissionA pressure transient enters via the great vessels or a vagally mediated reflex originating in the thorax and reaches the brain through the vascular tree, also implicated in behind armor blunt trauma (Section 6).
Cerebrospinal fluid cavitationProposed formation and violent collapse of microbubbles within CSF and brain vasculature; a debated, actively researched hypothesis rather than an established mechanism.

Mechanisms summarized from Courtney & Courtney, The Complexity of Biomechanics Causing Primary Blast Induced TBI, Front Neurol 2015; Rosenfeld et al., Neurological Effects of Blast Injury; Marsh & Bentil, Cerebrospinal Fluid Cavitation as a Mechanism of Blast Induced TBI, Front Neurol 2021.

Mild to moderate blast TBI presents much like concussion from any other mechanism: transient loss of consciousness or altered mentation, followed by post concussive syndrome (headache, cognitive disruption, irritability, anxiety, fatigue) that can persist well beyond the acute period. Diffusion tensor MRI in symptomatic patients has demonstrated diffuse axonal injury signatures. A normal head CT does not exclude this injury pattern; the diagnosis remains clinical, based on mechanism and symptom trajectory.

14  COMBINED INJURY PATTERNS

Real world explosive events rarely produce a single blast mechanism in isolation. An IED casualty commonly presents with an overlapping combination of primary blast lung or TM rupture, secondary fragment wounds with their own cavitation physics, tertiary blunt trauma from being thrown, and quaternary burns or crush injury, all in the same patient at once.

  • Confined space or vehicle-based blast: heavier primary injury burden layered on lower extremity fracture and traumatic amputation from an under-vehicle blast pattern.
  • Open air device with fragmentation casing: secondary penetrating wounds dominate, primary injury limited to patients closest to the seat of the blast (Section 11).
  • Structural collapse: a shift toward quaternary crush injury and delayed extraction on top of injury at the moment of detonation.
  • Body armor plus blast or fragment impact: a casualty with no penetrating chest wound can still carry a significant primary-pattern thoracic injury from BABT; an intact vest should not lower suspicion.

Practical implication: do not let an obvious injury close the differential for a co-occurring primary blast injury with no external sign yet. Every blast casualty needs the same primary-injury screen (lung, ears, abdomen, neuro) regardless of how dramatic the secondary or tertiary injuries look.

Combined injury patterns synthesized from JMVH, Evolving mechanisms and patterns of blast injury; Halpern et al. mass casualty terrorist bombings implications, as adapted in Medscape Blast Injuries: A Review; Deranged Physiology blast injury reference.

15  IMAGING AND RETAINED FRAGMENT WORKUP

  • CT trajectory reconstruction: plot entry, retained fragments, and exit (if present) on axial and reformatted imaging; a nonlinear or discontinuous track signals yaw, ricochet off bone, or fragmentation, and should prompt a specific search for secondary bone or bullet fragments along a plausible deflected path.
  • e-FAST: extended FAST remains the fastest bedside screen for hemoperitoneum, hemothorax, and pneumothorax in both GSW and blast patients; a negative e-FAST does not exclude retroperitoneal, bowel, or delayed primary blast injury and should not by itself end the workup in a concerning mechanism.
  • Retained bullet fragments, general: the reflex to remove every retained fragment is outdated. Established indications for removal are progressive neurologic deterioration, sepsis referable to the fragment, lead toxicity, and (for spinal fragments) an intracanal position between roughly T12 and L4 with incomplete neurologic injury. Most retained fragments elsewhere can be left in place.
  • Retained fragments and MRI: long-standing avoidance of MRI in patients with retained ballistic fragments is being revised. Most bullets, shot, and fragments are non-ferromagnetic or too small to generate clinically significant RF heating; imaging composition on prior CT/X-ray (and dual-energy CT where available) can identify non-ferromagnetic fragments as MRI-safe. Known ferromagnetic fragments (some BBs, steel-core rounds) still warrant caution and case-by-case radiology consultation.

CT trajectory and e-FAST practice reflect standard trauma imaging teaching. Retained fragment removal indications drawn from SciELO, Civilian gunshot wounds of the spine: a literature review, and Wheeless' Textbook of Orthopaedics, Gunshot Injuries to the Lumbar Spine. MRI safety synthesized from mriquestions.com, MR Safety Bullets, and Marino et al., Magnetic Resonance Imaging of Patients With Retained Ballistic Projectiles, J Magn Reson Imaging 2026.

16  ANTIBIOTIC PROPHYLAXIS AND DAMAGE CONTROL SURGERY

  • Tetanus: address tetanus immunization status in all penetrating ballistic and blast wounds; give prophylaxis when status is unknown or incomplete, regardless of wound appearance.
  • Antibiotics for isolated soft tissue GSW: evidence for low velocity, soft tissue only gunshot wounds is inconclusive; base the decision on tissue destruction, contamination, time since injury, anatomic location, and velocity rather than reflexive prophylaxis for every wound. A bullet is not sterilized by firing; contamination from clothing and skin flora is well documented.
  • Antibiotics with viscus involvement: broad spectrum coverage is indicated when the track traverses bowel; roughly 48–72 hours is generally sufficient once there is no ongoing contamination, with duration guided by the degree of soft tissue destruction rather than a fixed course for every patient.
  • Damage control surgery sequencing: in the physiologically exhausted patient (acidotic, coagulopathic, hypothermic), the operative goal shifts from definitive repair to abbreviated surgery: control hemorrhage, control contamination, pack and temporarily close, and return to the ICU for ongoing resuscitation before a planned re-look and definitive repair once physiology has been restored. Attempting definitive repair in a patient still in the lethal triad is a recognized way to lose a salvageable patient on the table.

Antibiotic and tetanus guidance drawn from Antibiotic prophylaxis in injury: an AAST Critical Care Committee clinical consensus document, PMC11149135; SciELO gunshot wounds of the spine review; and Musculoskeletal Key, Surgical Management Principles of Gunshot-Related Fractures. Damage control surgery sequencing reflects standard trauma surgery teaching paired with the damage control resuscitation literature in Section 18.

17  PREHOSPITAL CARE: THE MARCH ALGORITHM

Tactical Combat Casualty Care reordered the trauma primary survey from ABC to MARCH after battlefield data showed hemorrhage, not airway compromise, was the dominant preventable cause of death: roughly 91 percent of preventable combat deaths were due to hemorrhage versus about 8 percent from airway obstruction and about 1 percent from tension pneumothorax. The same ordering has been adopted widely in civilian tactical and mass casualty response (TECC).

StepPriorityAction
MMassive hemorrhageLife-threatening extremity bleeding: hasty tourniquet placed high and tight over clothing during care under fire, converted to a deliberate placement 2–3 inches proximal to the wound against skin once behind cover. Junctional hemorrhage (groin, axilla, neck base): direct pressure plus a junctional tourniquet or hemostatic wound packing with sustained pressure. Time of application is recorded on the tourniquet and casualty card.
AAirwayAssess patency; a conscious, speaking casualty has a patent airway. Address obstruction from maxillofacial trauma or inhalation injury before moving on.
RRespirationAssess for tension pneumothorax and treat with needle or finger decompression; occlusive dressing for open chest wounds.
CCirculationReassess for additional non-compressible or previously missed hemorrhage; establish access; early TXA and blood product administration when indicated and available.
HHypothermia / head injuryActive warming to prevent potentiation of the lethal triad; assess for head injury and avoid hypotension/hypoxia which worsen secondary brain injury.

TXA is indicated for massive hemorrhage, hemorrhagic shock, or suspected significant intrathoracic or intra-abdominal hemorrhage, and early blood product use is favored over crystalloid whenever available in the prehospital setting, carrying the damage control resuscitation principles in Section 18 forward from the point of injury.

MARCH sequencing and battlefield mortality data drawn from NCBI Bookshelf, EMS Tactical Combat Casualty Care (StatPearls), citing Eastridge et al., Death on the Battlefield, J Trauma 2012; tourniquet and junctional hemorrhage technique from the TCCC Casualty Card guidance summarized in MED-TAC International's MARCH Algorithm reference and Rescue Essentials' TCCC MARCH overview.

18  CLINICAL MANAGEMENT: DAMAGE CONTROL RESUSCITATION

Damage control resuscitation (DCR) is the resuscitation framework built for exactly the injury pattern this manual describes: hemorrhagic shock with a high likelihood of trauma induced coagulopathy. Its aim is to break the lethal triad of coagulopathy, acidosis, and hypothermia before it becomes self-sustaining.

ComponentPractice point
Permissive hypotensionTitrate resuscitation to a systolic blood pressure in the 80–90 mmHg range until hemorrhage is controlled, rather than normalizing pressure with crystalloid. Not used in suspected traumatic brain injury, where SBP is kept above 90 mmHg.
Hemostatic resuscitationEarly, balanced blood product transfusion (roughly 1:1:1 PRBC:FFP:platelets) in place of large volume crystalloid, targeting correction of coagulopathy rather than volume alone.
Massive transfusion protocolActivated for anticipated need of 10 or more units of red cells in 24 hours; each 10 mmHg drop in presenting SBP is associated with roughly a 26 percent increase in MTP likelihood.
Tranexamic acid1 g IV within 3 hours of injury, followed by a second 1 g infusion over the following 8 hours, in patients with significant hemorrhage.
Hypothermia and acidosis correctionActive warming of the patient and of all administered fluids/blood products; a resuscitation priority, not an afterthought.
REBOAAdjunct for non-compressible torso or junctional hemorrhage (zone selection in Section 8); buys time to definitive hemostasis, complementary to, not a substitute for, permissive hypotension and rapid surgical control.

Practice points drawn from the AAST/ACS-COT Clinical Protocol for Damage Control Resuscitation for the Adult Trauma Patient; EAST Practice Management Guideline, Damage Control Resuscitation in Patients with Severe Traumatic Hemorrhage; and McGovern Medical School Controlled Resuscitation in Trauma Patients Clinical Practice Guideline.

Wound ballistics and blast injury share a common bedside principle: the visible wound is a floor, not a ceiling, on the injury actually present. Build the differential from mechanism and energy, then confirm or exclude with a deliberate, complete exam, the right imaging, and a resuscitation strategy matched to the physiology in front of you.

19  UNDERWATER BLAST INJURY

Water is roughly 800 times denser and far less compressible than air, and this single fact reshapes blast injury physics. An underwater shock wave travels faster, propagates further with less attenuation, and couples into the body far more efficiently than the same charge detonated in air. The practical result: underwater blast injuries occur almost exclusively through the primary mechanism, since fragments and blast wind lose most of their relevance once submerged, and a charge that would be a modest air blast at a given range can be lethal underwater at the same range.

Injury pattern

As with air blast, gas containing organs bear the brunt of injury, but the pattern in water differs in emphasis. Intestinal barotrauma, particularly to the colon, is more common with underwater than with air blast, reflecting how efficiently the pressure wave couples through torso tissue to the gas filled bowel. Pulmonary injury remains prominent, with alveolar hemorrhage, air embolism, and a vagally mediated bradycardia and apnea described in animal models as a reflex response to lung damage. Cerebral injury is thought to occur via compression of the thoracic and abdominal venous reservoirs, raising venous pressure and rupturing small cerebral veins, though human data here remains limited. The ear remains the single most sensitive organ to primary blast injury in water as in air.

Additional risk from disorientation

A diver or swimmer exposed to an underwater blast faces a hazard with no air blast analog: disorientation and vestibular disruption sufficient to prevent airway protection, which converts a primary blast injury into a drowning risk. Any diver recovered after a nearby underwater detonation warrants the same primary-injury screen as an air blast casualty (lung, ear, bowel) plus a specific assessment for aspiration and hypoxic injury from loss of airway control during the event.

Underwater blast physiology and injury pattern drawn from Axelsson et al., Underwater Blast Injury: A Review of Standards, and the related Annals of Biomedical Engineering feasibility study; SCUBADOC Diving Medicine Online, Underwater Blast Injuries; and Medscape, Blast Injuries: Background, Pathophysiology, Etiology (intestinal barotrauma comparison).

20  CBRN MASS CASUALTY FRAMEWORK

Quinary blast injury (Section 10) refers to clinical consequences of contaminants added to or released by a device: chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear (CBRN) material superimposed on a blast or ballistic wound. This section covers triage and management framework only; it intentionally omits agent identification, effects detail, or any content that could inform agent selection or device design.

Sequencing principle

Security and scene safety are coordinated first, since a CBRN event is by definition an ongoing hazard, not only a casualty event. Once safe to approach, the sequence is decontamination, then medical triage, in that order for any casualty with suspected external contamination: decontamination is itself a medical intervention, since it minimizes conversion of external contamination to internal dose, and a casualty with suspected liquid chemical exposure is triaged as immediate until decontaminated, then re-triaged on medical grounds.

Contamination typePractice point
Liquid chemical exposureDecontaminate before definitive medical triage regardless of initial symptoms; vesicant exposure is treated with particular urgency given delayed but progressive tissue injury.
Vapor-only chemical exposureCan generally tolerate a delay in thorough decontamination, unlike liquid or vesicant exposure.
Radiological / nuclear exposureInitial triage follows conventional trauma injury severity; a secondary triage pass incorporating estimated radiation dose occurs once dosimetry resources are available, with combined trauma-radiation injury bumped to a higher acuity category.
Wound contaminationExternal, internal, and wound contamination are each assessed separately; wound decontamination and, for certain radiological exposures, decorporation agents (e.g., chelation) may be indicated in coordination with radiation safety and toxicology expertise.

Standard mass casualty principles still apply on top of this framework: do the most good for the most people with the resources available, and expect iterative re-triage as the event unfolds and more information (agent identification, dosimetry, decontamination capacity) becomes available. A dedicated, experienced triage officer is recommended given the added complexity of combined trauma and CBRN casualties.

This section deliberately omits agent-specific detection, identification, or effects information.

21  PEDIATRIC BLAST AND BALLISTIC CONSIDERATIONS

Children are not small adults, and the same explosive device or projectile that wounds an adult can kill a child through anatomic and physiologic differences alone. This section covers those differences; it does not cover weight-based dosing, which should be drawn from a current pediatric reference or protocol at the point of care.

SystemPediatric difference and implication
HeadLarger head-to-body ratio, thinner skull, less myelinated brain. Head injuries are more common and more severe in children exposed to blast or ballistic trauma than in adults, and a child can sustain significant TBI without loss of consciousness; triage to further evaluation any child with altered behavior, irritability, or excessive sleepiness after a blast or head impact even if initially alert.
ChestMore compliant, cartilaginous rib cage transmits force to underlying lung and heart with less fracture than in adults; significant pulmonary or cardiac contusion can be present with few or no rib fractures, so the absence of chest wall injury does not exclude major thoracic injury.
AbdomenThinner abdominal wall and proportionally larger, less protected solid organs (liver, spleen); children are more prone to significant abdominal injury from both blunt and penetrating mechanisms at forces that a thicker adult abdominal wall would attenuate.
Ears / lungs (primary blast)Ocular trauma, tympanic membrane rupture, and primary blast lung injury occur at higher rates in children than adults exposed to comparable blast conditions.
SkeletonMore pliable bone produces plastic deformity and torus (buckle) fractures not seen in mature bone; standard adult fracture patterns and healing timelines do not directly apply.
Global physiologySmaller absolute blood volume means a given volume of blood loss represents a larger fraction of total circulating volume; children compensate for hemorrhage with tachycardia and vasoconstriction longer than adults before hypotension appears, so normal blood pressure can be falsely reassuring in a child who is already in compensated shock. Children also become hypothermic more easily given greater body surface area to mass ratio, compounding coagulopathy risk in the same lethal triad described in Section 18.

Practical implication: apply the same MARCH and damage control resuscitation framework used for adults (Sections 17 and 18), but read vital signs against pediatric norms rather than adult thresholds, maintain a lower threshold for occult head, chest, and abdominal injury given the anatomic differences above, and prioritize active warming given faster heat loss. Resource availability is a documented gap: pediatric-specific equipment and supplies are disproportionately unavailable across EMS and emergency department settings relative to the pediatric share of most populations, which argues for deliberate advance planning rather than improvisation in the moment.

Anatomic and physiologic differences drawn from Turkish Journal of Emergency Medicine, Pediatric blast injuries: Distinguishing features and unique challenges (2026); ACEP, Pediatric Blast Injury fact sheet; PMC, Disaster preparedness, pediatric considerations in primary blast injury, chemical, and biological terrorism; and the EXTRACCT Clinical Practice Guideline, Resuscitation of the Pediatric Blast Injury Patient.

22  QUICK REFERENCE SUMMARY CARD

Two wounding mechanisms

Crush = permanent cavity (what you must excise). Stretch = temporary cavity (hurts dense/inelastic organs, spares elastic ones).

Caliber is not destiny

Velocity and construction predict wounding better than bore diameter. Fragmentation depends on impact velocity, so range can change the wound pattern of the same round.

Forensic range of fire

Contact: soot, searing, stellate tears. Close: soot + stippling. Intermediate: stippling only (not washable). Distant: abrasion collar only.

Body armor

Intact vest ≠ no internal injury. BABT can fracture ribs and bruise lung/heart beneath unbroken armor.

Neck zones

I = thoracic inlet to cricoid. II = cricoid to mandible angle. III = mandible angle to skull base. Hard signs → OR now. Soft signs → CTA.

REBOA zones

Zone I (subclavian–celiac): abdominal/thoracic hemorrhage, profound shock. Zone III (renal–bifurcation): pelvic/junctional hemorrhage. Zone II: avoid.

MARCH

Massive hemorrhage → Airway → Respiration → Circulation → Hypothermia/Head injury. Hemorrhage kills fastest; treat it first.

Blast taxonomy

Primary = overpressure (lung, ear, bowel, air embolism). Secondary = fragments. Tertiary = thrown. Quaternary = burns/toxic/crush. Quinary = contamination.

Blast lung / TM rupture

TM rupture = sentinel finding, not the main injury. Blast lung can look fine early then decompensate — observe.

DCR core

Permissive hypotension (SBP 80–90, not in TBI) + 1:1:1 blood products + TXA within 3h + active warming + rapid hemostasis.

Underwater blast

Water couples pressure far more efficiently than air — almost pure primary injury. Colon barotrauma more common than in air blast. Screen for drowning/aspiration from disorientation.

CBRN sequence

Safety first → decontaminate liquid/vesicant exposures before medical triage → re-triage after decon → secondary radiation-adjusted triage once dosimetry is available.

Pediatric red flags

Normal BP can mean compensated shock, not "fine." Few rib fractures ≠ no chest injury. Altered behavior after blast = TBI until proven otherwise, even with no LOC.
