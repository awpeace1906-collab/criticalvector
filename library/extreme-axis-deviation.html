Extreme Axis Deviation (Northwest Axis)

v3, expanded. "No-man's land" on the hexaxial system — why it happens, what it means, and which cause to rule out first. Verified against Ann Intern Med, Circulation, JAHA, LITFL, StatPearls, and primary WCT-differentiation, congenital cardiology, and pacing literature. July 2026.

1. The Big Picture

Normal QRS axis runs roughly -30° to +90°. Right axis deviation is >+90°, left axis deviation is <-30°. Extreme axis deviation — the "northwest axis," or less politely, "no-man's land" — is the leftover quadrant: -90° to ±180°. It is rare enough that most clinicians see it only a handful of times in training, and disproportionately important when it shows up, because the differential is short and several entries on it are dangerous.

The one-line summary: both Lead I and aVF are negative. That's it — that's the entire recognition pattern, before you even calculate a number.

The most common cause of this finding is not cardiac at all — it's a cable swap. Section 3 covers why that should be the first thing you check, not the last.

Terminology trap: extreme axis deviation is often called an "indeterminate axis" in casual use, but the two are not actually the same thing, and mixing them up matters. Extreme axis deviation means Lead I and aVF are both clearly, discernibly negative — the axis is known, it just falls in the -90° to ±180° range. A true indeterminate axis is different: the QRS is isoelectric or equiphasic in all six limb leads, so the polarity can't be read at all and no direction can be assigned. One is a specific finding with a specific differential (this whole document); the other is a technical failure to determine axis in the first place. Some sources use the terms interchangeably — this guide doesn't.

2. The 10-Second Calculation

You don't need the hexaxial wheel to recognize this one — the quadrant method gets you there before you've finished looking at the strip.

Lead ILead aVFQuadrant
PositivePositiveNormal axis
PositiveNegativeLeft axis deviation
NegativePositiveRight axis deviation
NegativeNegativeExtreme axis deviation

Right axis deviation pattern: Lead I negative, aVF positive, Lead II/III positive, aVL negative — axis ≥ +120° at the extreme end.

Left axis deviation pattern: Lead I positive, aVF negative, Lead II/III negative, aVL positive — axis ≤ -30° at the extreme end.

If you want the actual number rather than just the quadrant, the classic shortcut still applies: the axis is roughly perpendicular to whichever limb lead is isoelectric, oriented toward whichever nearby lead is most positive.

3. Rule This Out First: Lead Reversal

Limb lead reversal is common and should be excluded before any pathologic explanation on this list. Right arm/left leg reversal in particular produces an apparent extreme axis. It's also easy to miss, because the tracing doesn't look obviously wrong at a glance.

What RA/LL reversal actually does to the tracing: Lead I becomes an upside-down Lead III. The limb leads effectively rotate, producing a pattern that reads as extreme axis deviation even though the heart's true electrical axis is normal.

ClueWhat it tells you
P wave negative in Lead IStrong signal for limb lead reversal — true extreme axis deviation from cardiac pathology essentially never inverts the P wave in Lead I this way
Precordial leads look normalReversal is a limb-lead-only problem; if V1-V6 are unremarkable and only the limb leads look bizarre, suspect cables before pathology
Clinically well patientAn asymptomatic, hemodynamically normal patient with a dramatic-looking extreme axis is a reversed-cable tracing until proven otherwise

The fix is free and takes ten seconds: check the cables and repeat the ECG before ordering an echo, consulting cardiology, or treating a patient for a rhythm they don't have.

4. The Main Event: VT vs. SVT with Aberrancy

Once lead reversal is excluded, extreme axis deviation in the setting of a wide complex tachycardia is one of the more heavily validated markers pointing toward ventricular tachycardia rather than supraventricular tachycardia with aberrant conduction.

StudyFinding
Akhtar et al., 1988Landmark analysis of 150 consecutive wide-QRS tachycardia patients (122 VT, 21 SVT with aberrancy, 7 accessory pathway) that verified a rightward superior QRS axis (northwest axis, -90° to -180°) as highly predictive of VT. Clinicians at initial presentation correctly diagnosed VT in only 32% of cases, despite VT being the far more common mechanism — this study is as much about how often VT gets mistaken for SVT as it is about the axis criterion itself.
SVT superior-axis rateRoughly 23% of SVT with aberrancy can still show a superior axis, which is why this is a criterion that raises suspicion rather than one that clinches the diagnosis alone.
Vereckei aVR algorithm, 2008A different route from axis-based reasoning: the aVR algorithm looks at the initial R wave and the initial-versus-terminal activation velocity in lead aVR. It is a morphologic criterion in a single lead, not a measurement of QRS axis.

Practical takeaway: a wide complex tachycardia with a northwest axis should be treated as ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise — hemodynamic stability doesn't change the underlying rhythm, and physicians are well documented to under-diagnose VT specifically because a stable-looking patient feels reassuring.

Axis alone is one data point, not a full algorithm. The two formal algorithms that incorporate it are worth knowing in outline, since a northwest axis is exactly the kind of finding that should push you to run one of them rather than eyeball the strip.

Brugada algorithm (1991), four sequential steps — stop at the first positive:

1. Absence of an RS complex in all precordial leads (V1-V6) → VT.

2. Longest R-to-S nadir interval >100 ms in any precordial lead → VT.

3. AV dissociation → VT.

4. Morphology criteria for VT met in both V1-V2 and V6 (classic RBBB/LBBB morphology rules, including the V1 "left rabbit ear taller than right" pattern) → VT. None of the above → SVT with aberrancy.

Vereckei aVR algorithm (2008) — lead aVR only, four sequential steps:

1. Initial R wave present in aVR → VT.

2. Initial r or q wave >40 ms in aVR → VT.

3. Notching on the initial downstroke of a predominantly negative QRS in aVR → VT.

4. Ventricular activation velocity ratio (Vi/Vt, comparing the initial 40 ms of activation to the terminal 40 ms) ≤1 → VT. None of the above → SVT with aberrancy.

AlgorithmReported accuracy
Brugada, original derivation (1991)98.7% sensitivity, 96.5% specificity — but a later community revalidation by emergency physicians and cardiologists found sensitivity as low as 79% and specificity as low as 43%, and inter-rater disagreement in roughly 1 of 5 applications.
Vereckei aVR, independent validationIndependent comparisons report the aVR algorithm as sensitive but with modest specificity, and no clearly better overall than Brugada. Every algorithm loses accuracy outside its derivation cohort.
AV dissociation specificallyDespite being a classic teaching point, visible AV dissociation appears on the surface ECG in only a minority of VT, commonly cited at about 20 to 30 percent. It's highly specific when present but absent far more often than trainees expect.

The gap between the original 1991 derivation numbers and later real-world revalidation is worth internalizing on its own: these algorithms are decision aids, not infallible rules, and interobserver disagreement is common even among specialists. Treat any positive VT criterion as reason enough to manage the rhythm as VT — the cost of mistakenly treating SVT as VT is far lower than the reverse.

One important exception worth knowing in the opposite direction: Belhassen (idiopathic fascicular) VT classically shows a right bundle branch block pattern with left axis deviation, not northwest axis — a reminder that axis criteria describe probabilities, not certainties, and a relatively benign, ablatable VT can still look electrically unusual.

5. The Rest of the Differential

Ventricular rhythms (non-tachycardic): accelerated idioventricular rhythm and ventricular escape rhythm can produce a northwest axis outside the tachycardia setting — same mechanism as VT, just at a normal or slow rate.

Ventricular pacing — the axis depends on where the lead actually sits.

Standard right ventricular apical pacing produces an LBBB-pattern QRS with a superior axis, usually in left-axis territory (roughly −60° to −90°) and only sometimes extending into the northwest quadrant. This is expected for that lead position, not pathology. Left ventricular epicardial pacing (via coronary sinus, as in cardiac resynchronization therapy) produces the mirror image: an RBBB pattern. Biventricular/CRT pacing from an RV apical lead plus a lateral coronary venous LV lead characteristically shows a dominant R wave in V1, a Q or q wave in leads I and aVL, and a right or left superior axis — recognizing this as the expected combined-pacing signature avoids mistaking normal CRT function for lead malfunction.

In a post-cardiac-surgery or device-clinic patient, an extreme axis on a paced tracing is frequently just confirmation the RV lead is doing what an RV apical lead does — correlate with the device interrogation and prior tracings before treating it as a new finding.

Hyperkalemia: severe hyperkalemia can shift the axis into the extreme range as part of its broader conduction-slowing effects, alongside the more familiar peaked T waves and QRS widening. An unexpected northwest axis with a wide, bizarre QRS is a reason to check a potassium before assuming a primary rhythm problem.

TCA or other sodium-channel blocker toxicity: the classic axis finding is a rightward shift of the terminal 40 ms of the QRS (terminal R in aVR, deep S in I), alongside QRS widening. A whole-QRS extreme axis is less typical and should prompt a search for another cause.

AV canal defect (atrioventricular septal defect) and ostium primum ASD: the classic pediatric cause of a superior QRS axis, and one worth knowing cold — this is a board-exam staple as much as a clinical one.

A superior axis, alongside RBBB-pattern conduction delay, RVH, LVH, and a prolonged PR interval, is a hallmark ECG finding in complete and partial AV canal defects. The mechanism is a displaced, malformed conduction system rather than a rhythm disturbance — the AV node and proximal conduction tissue are shifted by the underlying septal defect itself.

AV canal defects are strongly associated with Down syndrome (trisomy 21) and are the most common congenital heart defect seen in that population — a superior axis on a pediatric ECG, especially alongside a fixed split S2 or a holosystolic murmur, should raise this diagnosis specifically rather than getting filed under the adult VT/hyperkalemia differential above.

Structural and other congenital causes: tetralogy of Fallot (uncommonly — one series found right axis deviation in 87% of TOF patients but extreme right axis deviation in only about 3%), tricuspid atresia, single-ventricle physiology, right superior fascicular block, severe combined right ventricular hypertrophy with left anterior fascicular block, dextrocardia, and pulmonary emphysema round out the remaining structural causes.

Brugada syndrome: not a classic cause of extreme axis. The documented finding is a left-axis shift during ajmaline provocation, thought to reflect conduction slowing through the right ventricular outflow tract. Included only so it isn't mistaken for one.

Notice the pattern across this whole differential: nearly every cause here is either an artifact (lead reversal) or something that changes management immediately (VT, hyperkalemia, TCA overdose). This is not a finding to sit on while waiting for a cardiology read.
