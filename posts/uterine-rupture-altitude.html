Uterine Rupture at Altitude: When the Retrieval Is the Resuscitation

May 2026 · Air Medical Journal · DOI: 10.1016/j.amj.2026.01.005

There's a category of case that doesn't fit neatly into the prehospital box or the hospital box. The patient is sick enough to need intensive care, but the intensive care is happening in the back of a helicopter. The retrieval is the resuscitation. That's what a new case report in Air Medical Journal puts on the table — and it's worth working through carefully.

The Setup

A patient presents in extremis following catastrophic hemorrhage from uterine rupture — one of the rarer obstetric emergencies, but reliably devastating when it happens. The flight crew is managing simultaneous hemodynamic instability and refractory hypoxemia. Not one problem. Both, at the same time, in a confined space, with limited resources and a fixed destination still minutes away.

This is the scenario that makes prehospital critical care medicine genuinely different from everything that follows. In the ED, you can call for backup. In the OR, you have a perfusionist. In the ICU, you have the night attending a phone call away. In the back of an aircraft at cruise altitude, you have what you brought and the people next to you.

The Resuscitation

The crew initiates massive transfusion protocol — the cornerstone of damage control resuscitation in hemorrhagic shock. The core principle hasn't changed: balanced resuscitation with blood products in a ratio approximating whole blood, minimizing crystalloid, and targeting hemostasis rather than just pressure. What makes the transport environment uniquely challenging is that the tools for guiding that resuscitation — serial lactate, TEG/ROTEM, continuous hemodynamic monitoring — are compressed or absent.

Mechanical ventilation management adds another layer. The hypoxemia here isn't isolated — it's compounded by the physiology of hemorrhagic shock, potential transfusion-associated complications, and the altitude itself. The case highlights the tension between ventilator strategies optimized for oxygenation and those compatible with the cardiovascular state of a patient who is already losing the hemodynamic fight.

REBOA: On the Differential, Not on the Aircraft

Resuscitative Endovascular Balloon Occlusion of the Aorta gets a mention here, which is clinically honest. REBOA has expanded into the conversation for non-compressible torso hemorrhage, and uterine rupture checks that box. In a facility with the right team and equipment, aortic zone III occlusion buys time to hemorrhage control. In a transport environment, it's a different calculus entirely — the skill set, the wire access, the fluoroscopy, the commitment to a procedure that changes the management trajectory in ways that matter at the receiving end. The case treats it appropriately: considered, not dismissed, but also not performed in the air.

TACO at the End of the Line

Transfusion-associated circulatory overload surfaces as a complication — which is worth noting because it's easy to think of massive transfusion as a problem you solve rather than a process that creates new problems. Volume resuscitation sufficient to maintain perfusion pressure can push a compromised cardiopulmonary system into overload, particularly if the underlying cause of hemorrhage isn't controlled. The case makes this explicit, which is clinically useful.

Why This Belongs in The Continuum

The point isn't just that this is a dramatic case. It's that the decisions made in the first hour — what ratio to transfuse, how to manage the vent, whether to escalate toward a procedural intervention — have direct implications for what the ED team inherits, what the OR team walks into, and what the ICU manages overnight. The handoffs are consequential. The continuum is real, and it starts before the hospital doors open.

That's the thread this section of the blog will keep pulling: the places where prehospital, emergency medicine, critical care, and anesthesiology are not separate disciplines but sequential phases of a single encounter.
