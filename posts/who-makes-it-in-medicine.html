Who Actually Makes It in Medicine

June 2026 · From the Flight Deck

There is a student in my class who acquired the nickname "Supreme Leader" during an ice-breaker in first year. She said it as a joke. Somebody laughed. Then somebody else used it in a serious context: checking if she'd reviewed the pharmacology block, asking whether she thought the anatomy lab schedule made sense, treating her opinion like a benchmark. A staff member had a badge buddy made for her. It has her name on one side and "Supreme Leader" on the other. She wears it.

She is not the loudest person in the room. In the first weeks of school she was quiet the way some very capable people are quiet: paying attention, forming judgments, not yet ready to share them. By third year she is the person classmates route questions through, not because she demands it, but because she has been consistently right and consistently generous with her time when it mattered. She wants to be a surgeon. Her board scores suggest she will be.

There is another student in my class who is called "Med School Dad." He earned it the straightforward way: he checks on people. He shows up when classmates are struggling with something that has nothing to do with medicine. He brought food to a study group that had been in the library since six in the morning. He remembers things people mention once in passing, that someone was nervous about a parent's surgery or having a hard month, and follows up weeks later. He is also outspoken, opinionated, and socially magnetic in rooms full of people. He passes his boards without drama.

What I left out: both of them are non-traditional students. Both came through paths that admissions committees find harder to process: work histories that don't translate cleanly into academic metrics, backgrounds requiring more explanation than a straight-line premed trajectory. Both were rejected or simply never heard back from programs that screened them out before a conversation could happen. The question worth asking is what those programs thought they were protecting themselves from.

Neither of these people fits the template that medical school admissions committees are optimizing for. The research on personality in medicine has a hard time accounting for either of them.

What the research actually says

The personality literature in medical education keeps landing on the same finding: conscientiousness predicts performance in the preclinical years. Students who score high on self-discipline and methodical follow-through get better grades in the classroom years. This is not surprising. The first two years of medical school are, at their core, a very long memorization exercise. Conscientiousness is the engine that gets you through it.

What gets more complicated is what happens when the curriculum shifts to the wards. One study following Belgian medical students across all six years found that extraversion showed no grade advantage early on, then correlated strongly with interpersonal performance in the clinical years. Students who had been quieter academically caught up fast once patients were involved. A Western University study tracking 300 students through third-year clerkships identified social confidence and calmness as significant predictors of clinical performance, traits that standard admissions metrics do not touch.

There is also the introvert problem, which the literature documents with uncomfortable consistency. Reserved students who perform identically to extroverted peers on written exams are evaluated lower in clerkships. The Society of Teachers of Family Medicine has synthesized this research directly: more assertive students received lower grades less frequently, while reserved students were more likely to report lower clinical scores. Not because they knew less or did less. Because clinical evaluation is a performance in which confidence reads as competence. One Academic Medicine commentary put it plainly: reticence gets mistaken for lack of interest, and that misinterpretation ends up in the evaluation.

This is a structural problem masquerading as a personality problem. The clerkship environment was built by extroverts, for people who perform in it naturally, and then we measure performance in it as though the measurement is neutral.

What the research cannot measure

The Big Five personality framework — extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, openness, neuroticism — is a useful instrument with real predictive validity. It was not built to account for the kind of social capital that Supreme Leader accrued by being right about things quietly over time, or that Med School Dad generates by treating his colleagues as people worth caring about.

The emotional intelligence literature gets closer. A 2024 systematic review found that higher emotional intelligence (EI) was associated with lower burnout, better patient satisfaction scores, and longer careers. Residents with low EI leave the profession earlier. A three-year longitudinal study of medical and surgical residents found that emotional exhaustion rose sharply at the start of postgraduate year one (PGY-1) and stayed high throughout training; emotional intelligence was the clearest protective factor against it.

What emotional intelligence research is actually measuring is this: can you track what is happening in a room, including what is happening to you, and make good decisions anyway? In Supreme Leader that looks like earning trust so gradually that by the time people are routing their career questions through her, nobody can identify the moment they decided to trust her. In Med School Dad it looks like knowing when a colleague who says they are fine is not fine, and doing something about it without making it a project.

Neither skill appears on a board score. Both appear on every patient encounter, every code, every handoff where the team needs someone to make a call and the room is waiting.

The personality type that actually worries me

The medical student I worry about is not the introvert who struggles in clerkships. Those students are navigating a structural bias and most of them find ways through it. The ones who make it tend to compensate with precision: they may not command the room but they know the labs, they follow through, they do not miss things. That is a legitimate path.

The student who worries me has learned to present patients cleanly, answer pimping questions with the right vocabulary, and project confidence. They have not yet been put in a situation that requires them to actually care what happens next. Medicine has a long clinical training period in which a student can accumulate the surface behaviors of competence without accumulating the underlying judgment. The students who coast on that long enough tend to hit a wall in residency, when the margin for performance-without-substance disappears.

This is not an introvert-extrovert problem. Extroverted people can have genuine investment in their patients. Quieter people can run the same performance-without-substance play at lower volume. The tell is not personality type. It is what a person does when nobody is grading them.

What "larger than life" actually means

Both students get called "larger than life" by classmates, though the phrase means something different in each case. With Med School Dad it is literal: he takes up space, he is present, he generates warmth in rooms by occupying them. With Supreme Leader it works in the opposite direction. She became larger than life by being someone people measured themselves against without her asking them to.

The research label for what they share is probably agreeableness combined with high emotional intelligence. But that framing flattens something real. Agreeableness in the Big Five is a trait. What these students have is a series of sustained choices: to show up for people, to stay consistent, to not cash in accumulated trust for short-term advantage. Those choices compound over three or four years into a reputation that no admissions score predicted and no board exam confirmed.

Grit research adds one more piece. Students with higher grit scores were more likely to complete medical school in four years rather than five, and lower grit correlated with burnout and higher odds of considering leaving residency. Grit, as the research operationalizes it, is not toughness. It is sustained interest combined with perseverance toward a long-term goal. The students who stay genuinely interested in the work, rather than just clearing the next checkpoint, have an advantage that becomes visible in the third or fourth year of residency, when the students who were optimizing for checkboxes have run out of them.

The non-traditional tax

The research on non-traditional medical students has one finding consistent enough to treat as settled: the credential gap at admission closes. A University of Toledo study compared students who entered via Special Master's Programs (SMPs). These are typically students with lower incoming Medical College Admission Test (MCAT) scores who completed an additional year of graduate-level coursework to demonstrate readiness. The comparison group was traditional matriculants. MCAT scores differed significantly at entry. United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) Step 1 scores did not. When normalized for incoming MCAT, the SMP students outperformed traditional peers on boards. A separate study at institutions with less competitive undergraduate pipelines found the same pattern: significant differences in MCAT scores at admission, no significant differences in Step 1 or Step 2 performance once inside.

The osteopathic literature shows the same thing. A recent study examining post-baccalaureate versus non-post-baccalaureate students at an osteopathic school found first-attempt Comprehensive Osteopathic Medical Licensing Examination (COMLEX-USA) Level 1 pass rates of 97.5% versus 96.0% respectively, a difference that did not reach statistical significance. The students who took a longer or harder road to the door performed identically once they were through it.

The admission credential gap is not a performance gap. It is an artifact of a screening process that weights certain signals heavily and filters accordingly, then watches those filtered students arrive and perform at the same level as the students who were easy to admit.

The empathy data adds a different dimension. Research on empathy development in medical students identified prior work experience as a significant contributing variable, sitting alongside maturity and professionalism as factors that shaped how students developed and applied empathy with patients. A 2025 comparative study found that qualified professionals showed higher empathy than students, and that empathic capacity evolved in response to the relational demands of different clinical roles rather than formal training alone. Non-traditional students arrive having already been in those relational dynamics. They have managed people, absorbed bad outcomes, navigated institutions that did not care about their feelings. That is not the same as clinical training, but it is not nothing.

What the literature cannot cleanly capture is the thing both of my classmates actually demonstrate: that having a reason to want medicine that predates the application cycle by years produces something visible on the wards within weeks of the first clerkship. You can see it in how they talk to patients. You can see it in how they respond when something goes wrong and someone is looking for someone to follow.

What program directors are actually looking for

The 2024 National Resident Matching Program (NRMP) Program Director Survey found something that tends to get buried under the conversation about board scores. For interview selection, the top factors were USMLE Step 1 pass status, the Medical Student Performance Evaluation (MSPE)/Dean's Letter, and specialty-specific letters of recommendation. Clinical performance, in other words: not raw numerical scores, but performance in the environment where personality and judgment are on display for months at a time.

For ranking decisions, the picture shifts further. The top factors cited by program directors were interpersonal skills (rated 4.8 out of 5.0 in importance), interactions during the interview (4.8), and feedback from current residents (4.6). Board scores did not crack the top three. The ability to function with other people in a high-stress training environment is, by the data program directors generate about their own behavior, the primary driver of where applicants land.

This matters for non-traditional students in a specific way. The factors program directors weight most heavily in ranking are exactly the factors that accumulate through lived experience: how you carry yourself in a room, what current residents sense about whether you will be someone worth working alongside at 2 a.m. in February. Those qualities are not learnable from a test prep course. They come from having already been in hard rooms and having already had to figure out how to be useful in them.

A program director writing publicly about what he actually reads for in personal statements said he wants to know if someone failed at something miserably and how they overcame it. He wants to know about specific life events that changed a person. The non-traditional path is, by definition, a story about obstacles encountered and navigated. The problem is that the personal statement comes after the initial screen, and many non-traditional applicants never survive the screen long enough to tell it.

The thing worth saying plainly

Medical school admissions committees and residency program directors are optimizing for different things, and they largely know it. Admissions committees run a prediction model with the tools available: MCAT, grade point average (GPA), volunteer hours. Those tools predict first-year classroom performance with reasonable accuracy. Program directors, operating downstream where classroom performance has already been demonstrated by everyone still standing, weight interpersonal skills and observed behavior so heavily that board scores barely register in ranking decisions.

Non-traditional students get screened by a model that is a poor fit for their actual assets, then evaluated by a model that is a good fit for their actual assets. The transition between those two models is where many of them get lost. The ones who make it through tend to share one quality: they did not accept silence from programs that never responded as a verdict on whether they belonged. They found the one door that opened and walked through it.

The students I have watched succeed in ways that actually matter, not just survive boards or match somewhere good but become people I would want caring for me or someone I love, share qualities that no standardized instrument captures cleanly. They stay curious past the point where curiosity is rewarded. They treat their colleagues as people rather than competition. They are comfortable making decisions under uncertainty. And they seem to understand, without being told, that the patient in the bed is always the actual point.

Supreme Leader got her badge buddy because a staff member noticed. Med School Dad got his nickname because his classmates couldn't help but name what they were receiving. Both of those things happened because the people around them paid attention and found something worth naming.

That's the data the admissions process keeps missing.
