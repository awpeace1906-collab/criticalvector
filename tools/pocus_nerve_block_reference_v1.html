Procedural Framework
Phase-by-Phase Reference
01
Phase One
Pre-Procedure Preparation
1.1 Indication, Consent & Contraindication Screen

Confirm the clinical indication. US-guided peripheral nerve blocks are indicated for procedural analgesia, opioid-sparing analgesia, facilitation of extremity procedures, and acute pain management in the ED or perioperative setting.

Obtain informed consent. Discuss:

  • Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)
  • Nerve injury — neuropraxia, axonotmesis
  • Hematoma or vascular injury
  • Infection
  • Block failure or incomplete analgesia
  • Pneumothorax — for blocks in proximity to pleura (interscalene, supraclavicular)
  • Phrenic nerve palsy — bilateral blocks contraindicated

Absolute contraindications:

  • Patient refusal
  • Infection overlying the injection site
  • Known allergy to the selected local anesthetic class

Relative contraindications (risk-benefit analysis required):

  • Coagulopathy or anticoagulation — use ASRA institutional thresholds
  • Pre-existing neuropathy in the target distribution
  • Hemidiaphragm compromise (contralateral phrenic palsy) for C3–C5 blocks
  • Uncooperative or obtunded patient
1.2 Baseline Neurologic & Vascular Assessment

Document pre-procedure motor and sensory function in the planned block distribution. Pre-existing deficits must be recorded clearly — this protects both patient and provider if new symptoms arise post-block.

Confirm IV access is established and functional before any block is performed. This is non-negotiable. Record baseline vital signs: HR, BP, SpO₂, RR.

1.3 Local Anesthetic Selection & Dosing

Always calculate the total milligram dose — not just the volume.

Adjuvants:

  • Dexamethasone 4–8 mg perineural: extends block duration by 4–8 hrs; systemic dose may provide equivalent effect
  • Epinephrine 1:200,000 (5 mcg/mL): prolongs block, reduces vascular uptake, intravascular injection marker (HR rise >20 bpm = suspect IV injection)
  • Dexmedetomidine 0.5–1 mcg/kg perineural: emerging evidence for duration extension; not universally adopted
1.4 Resuscitation Readiness
Critical
LAST is the most feared complication of peripheral nerve blockade. Intralipid 20% and resuscitation equipment must be immediately accessible — not in a cabinet down the hall — before the first milliliter is injected.

Required at bedside:

  • Intralipid 20% (lipid emulsion) — confirmed present, drawn up or immediately accessible
  • Airway equipment: bag-valve-mask, laryngoscope, ETT or SGA
  • Supplemental oxygen and suction
  • Emergency medications: epinephrine, benzodiazepine, atropine
  • Crash cart within immediate reach

ASRA Intralipid 20% dosing for LAST:

  • Bolus: 1.5 mL/kg ideal body weight IV over 2–3 minutes
  • Infusion: 0.25 mL/kg/min; double to 0.5 mL/kg/min if cardiovascular instability persists
  • Repeat bolus once if no improvement at 5 minutes
  • Max cumulative dose: ~12 mL/kg in first 30 minutes
Note
Do not substitute propofol for Intralipid. Propofol contains lipid at insufficient concentration and carries its own hemodynamic risks at the doses required for emulsion therapy.
1.5 Positioning & Monitoring

Position for optimal operator access, patient comfort, and rapid conversion to airway management if needed.

  • Pulse oximetry (continuous)
  • Continuous ECG
  • NIBP cycling every 2–3 minutes during injection

Supplemental O₂ via nasal cannula for all moderate-to-high-risk blocks. Procedural sedation must not mask early CNS symptoms of LAST — use the minimum effective dose.

02
Phase Two
Ultrasound Setup & Target Identification
2.1 Transducer Selection
  • High-frequency linear (10–15 MHz): optimal for superficial nerves (<4 cm). Standard for most extremity blocks.
  • Low-frequency curvilinear (2–5 MHz): required for deep targets (>4–5 cm). Sciatic, obturator, deep lumbosacral plexus.
  • High-frequency curved / hockey stick (8–13 MHz): pediatric and superficial blocks in tight anatomical spaces.

General rule: use the highest frequency that provides adequate penetration. Resolution degrades linearly with depth. Downgrading prematurely sacrifices needle visualization unnecessarily.

2.2 Systematic Survey Scan

Before committing to an injection site, perform a full survey scan:

  • Identify the target nerve and surrounding structures
  • Map adjacent vasculature with color Doppler — do not skip this step even for "simple" blocks
  • Identify anatomical variants: bifurcations, aberrant vessels, accessory nerves
  • Confirm nerve identity by tracing proximally and distally — honeycomb (fascicular) pattern in short axis; parallel cable in long axis
  • Identify pleura, peritoneum, or other critical structures in the needle trajectory
Sonoanatomy
Peripheral nerves appear hyperechoic with internal hypoechoic fascicular bundles (honeycomb pattern). Tendons may mimic nerves — trace proximally; tendons attach to muscle, nerves do not.
2.3 Image Optimization

Maximize image quality before needle insertion. Chasing the needle tip on a suboptimal image is a common source of error.

  • Depth: center target nerve in the middle third of the image
  • Gain: over-gain washes out fascicular detail
  • Focus zone: position at the level of the target nerve
  • Anisotropy correction: heel-toe tilt to maximize nerve brightness — a nerve invisible at one angle becomes clearly visible with a few degrees of tilt
2.4 Needle Approach Planning

In-plane (IP): Needle inserted along the long axis of the transducer. Entire shaft and tip visible throughout. Preferred for most blocks.

Out-of-plane (OOP): Needle perpendicular to beam. Only the cross-section (a single hyperechoic dot) is visible — this is the tip ONLY when the beam intersects the tip precisely. Risk: the dot may represent the shaft. Always confirm tip position with hydrodissection.

  • Avoid crossing Doppler-confirmed vessels
  • Maintain the shallowest needle angle consistent with target access
  • Allow tip repositioning without full retraction
03
Phase Three
Sterile Technique & Needle Insertion
3.1 Sterile Preparation

Standard single-injection block:

  • Chlorhexidine-gluconate skin prep — allow full contact time (30 sec, air dry)
  • Sterile transducer cover with sterile coupling gel inside the cover
  • Sterile gloves and drape over the procedural field

Continuous catheter technique — additional requirements:

  • Full surgical skin prep of a wide field
  • Maximal sterile barrier: full gown, sterile gloves, mask, cap, large sterile drape
  • Follow ASRA catheter insertion aseptic technique guidelines
Note
Chlorhexidine is preferred over povidone-iodine. Allow complete drying before needle insertion — pooled chlorhexidine on the needle can be introduced into tissue.
3.2 Skin Wheal & Needle Selection

Raise a subcutaneous lidocaine wheal (0.5–1 mL of 1% lidocaine) at the planned entry point. This reduces pain on insertion and allows a moment to confirm transducer position before committing to the block needle.

  • Gauge: 21–22G standard — balances injectability with procedural control
  • Length: match to target depth plus approach angle; a shallow angle requires a longer needle
  • Echogenicity: use echogenic needles (blunt-tip, Stimuplex, texture-surface) — most significant for OOP and deep targets
  • Blunt-tip (pencil-point): may reduce nerve penetration risk; preferred at some centers for high-risk blocks
  • Short-bevel: better tactile feedback when traversing fascial planes
3.3 Needle Insertion & Tip Confirmation
Rule
Never advance the needle if the tip is not visible.

IP approach: Advance slowly with the transducer stationary; watch for tissue movement ahead of the tip. If the tip is lost — do not advance. Tilt or slide the transducer until the tip reappears. Tip appears as a bright hyperechoic dot with posterior acoustic artifact.

OOP approach: Track the hyperechoic cross-section through successive tissue planes using the "walking" technique. Always confirm final tip position with hydrodissection.

Hydrodissection:

  • Inject 0.5–2 mL of NS or D5W (D5W preferred — superior echogenicity; isosmotic and non-neurotoxic)
  • Correct position: fluid spreads in the expected tissue plane and separates the nerve from surrounding fascia
  • If fluid disappears without visible spread (intramuscular absorption): reposition and repeat
04
Phase Four
Aspiration, Injection & Spread Confirmation
4.1 Aspiration Before Every Injection

Aspirate for 3–5 seconds before each injection and after any needle repositioning. Positive aspiration (blood return) mandates repositioning — do not inject.

Critical Caveat
Negative aspiration does NOT exclude intravascular placement. False-negative rates of up to 2% have been reported. The needle tip may be against a vessel wall or within a small vessel that collapses on aspiration. Negative aspiration is a screening step, not a guarantee. The true safety margin comes from incremental dosing and epinephrine markers.
4.2 Injection Technique
  • Inject in 2–3 mL aliquots
  • Aspirate between each aliquot
  • Pause 15–30 seconds between aliquots to allow time for early CNS toxicity symptoms to manifest
  • Total injection time should be at least 60–90 seconds regardless of volume
  • Target injection pressure <15 psi — high resistance suggests intraneural/subfascial placement, stop and reposition
  • A well-positioned perineural injection flows easily with minimal resistance
Stop Sign
If the patient reports paresthesia or pain during injection — stop immediately. Paresthesia on injection may indicate intraneural placement. Reposition before continuing.

Epinephrine intravascular marker: A 3 mL test dose will produce >20 bpm HR increase within 60 seconds if injected intravascularly. Unreliable with beta-blockers or significant baseline tachycardia. Absence of HR response does NOT exclude intravascular injection.

4.3 Real-Time Spread Visualization
  • Correct perineural: hypoechoic fluid surrounds the target nerve ("donut sign" in short axis)
  • Intramuscular: fluid spreads diffusely through muscle planes without organizing around a structure
  • Intraneural: nerve appears to swell and increase in cross-sectional area — CRITICAL WARNING SIGN, stop immediately
  • Subfascial: fluid tracks along fascial planes without reaching the nerve

If spread is suboptimal, redirect the needle before continuing. Do not attempt to compensate with increased volume.

4.4 Needle Repositioning

Multiple repositioned aliquots deliver better circumferential coverage than a single large bolus. If spread is unilateral or incomplete:

  • Pause injection
  • Withdraw needle partially under US visualization
  • Redirect to the opposite aspect of the nerve
  • Aspirate, re-confirm tip position with hydrodissection, continue
05
Phase Five
LAST Surveillance & Complication Monitoring
5.1 Pathophysiology of LAST

Local anesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels. In the CNS, this inhibits inhibitory interneurons first, producing an excitatory prodrome before global CNS depression. In the cardiovascular system, sodium channel blockade prolongs conduction and depresses contractility.

Bupivacaine is the most cardiotoxic agent — it dissociates slowly from cardiac sodium channels ("fast in, slow out"), and its R(+) enantiomer additionally blocks calcium channels and mitochondrial metabolism.

  • Plasma concentration rise driven by: total mg dose, injection site vascularity (intercostal > caudal > paracervical > brachial plexus > femoral/sciatic), presence/absence of epinephrine, rate of injection, intravascular injection
5.2 Clinical Presentation of LAST

Early CNS excitation:

  • Perioral/tongue numbness or tingling
  • Metallic taste
  • Tinnitus or auditory changes
  • Visual disturbances (nystagmus, diplopia)
  • Anxiety, agitation, or feeling of impending doom
  • Confusion, disorientation

CNS depression (severe): Drowsiness → unconsciousness, seizures (tonic-clonic or focal), respiratory arrest.

Cardiovascular: Bradycardia, QRS widening, PR prolongation, ST changes, VT/VF, cardiovascular collapse.

Important
LAST can present as cardiovascular collapse WITHOUT antecedent CNS symptoms — particularly with bupivacaine, rapid injection, or in sedated/anesthetized patients. Onset window: within 60 seconds of intravascular injection, but can be delayed up to 30 minutes with perineural absorption. Maintain monitoring for the full 30-minute window.

06
Phase Six
Post-Procedure Assessment & Documentation
6.1 Block Onset Assessment

Assess at 5-minute intervals for 30 minutes:

  • Sensory: cold (alcohol swab), sharp (pinprick), light touch — compare to contralateral side
  • Motor: strength testing in the distribution of the blocked nerve
  • Compare all findings against pre-procedure baseline documentation

Onset typically 10–25 minutes for long-acting agents. Absence of sensory change at 30 minutes suggests block failure — reassess with US before proceeding with the primary procedure.

6.2 Procedure Documentation
  • Indication; consent confirmed; timeout completed
  • Patient position and monitoring applied
  • Transducer used and approach (IP vs OOP)
  • Needle type, gauge, and length
  • LA agent, concentration, volume, and total milligram dose
  • Adjuvants added (if any)
  • Aspiration results (negative × number of aliquots)
  • Real-time US spread pattern observed
  • Onset assessment at 10, 20, and 30 minutes
  • Patient-reported symptoms during injection
  • Any complications or unplanned events; post-procedure plan
6.3 Patient Instructions
  • Expected duration of sensory and motor blockade for the agent used
  • Limb protection: sling (upper extremity), ambulation restrictions (lower extremity)
  • Fall precautions — mandatory for any lower extremity block
  • Return precautions: prolonged paresthesia, new weakness, signs of infection, severe pain on block resolution
  • Contact information for questions
Part II
Step-by-Step Instruction Set
Sequential Action Checklist — Use During Training & Pre-Procedure Run-Through

This section translates the procedural framework into a sequential, action-oriented checklist. Steps A–H constitute the LAST emergency sequence and apply at any point in the procedure.

Pre-Procedure — Before entering the room or at the bedside
1
Confirm the clinical indication and that the appropriate nerve(s) are targeted for the procedure or pain syndrome.
2
Review: patient weight, allergies (local anesthetic class), current anticoagulation status, and any pre-existing neuropathy in the target distribution.
3
Calculate the maximum allowable dose in milligrams (weight-based). Determine the volume-concentration combination that achieves the target dose without exceeding the ceiling.
4
Confirm IV access is patent and functional. If none: obtain it before proceeding.
5
Obtain and document informed consent. Discuss: LAST, nerve injury, hematoma, infection, and block-specific risks (pneumothorax, phrenic nerve palsy where applicable).
6
Confirm Intralipid 20% is physically present and accessible before injecting any local anesthetic.
7
Confirm at bedside: airway equipment, BVM, supplemental O₂, epinephrine, benzodiazepine.
8
Apply monitoring: pulse oximetry, continuous ECG, NIBP cycling every 2–3 minutes.
9
Position the patient for optimal target access. Confirm comfort and ability to maintain position for the procedure duration.
10
Perform pre-procedure timeout: patient identity, procedure, site, allergies, consent, emergency equipment confirmed.
Ultrasound Setup
11
Select the appropriate transducer:
  • Superficial (<4 cm): high-frequency linear (10–15 MHz)
  • Deep (>4–5 cm): low-frequency curvilinear (2–5 MHz)
12
Apply coupling gel to the transducer (non-sterile at this stage — survey scan only).
13
Perform a systematic survey scan. Identify: target nerve, adjacent vessels, fascial planes, and any critical structures in the planned needle path.
14
Activate color Doppler to map vessels in the intended needle trajectory.
15
Confirm nerve identity by tracing in both long and short axis. Look for the honeycomb (fascicular) pattern in short axis.
16
Optimize the image: depth, gain, focus zone, and transducer tilt (anisotropy correction) to achieve the clearest possible nerve visualization.
17
Determine and confirm needle approach: in-plane (IP) vs. out-of-plane (OOP). IP is preferred. Identify the skin entry point.
Sterile Setup
18
Open sterile supplies to the field: block needle, syringes, extension tubing, sterile transducer cover, sterile gel.
19
Perform hand hygiene. Don sterile gloves.
20
Prepare skin with chlorhexidine-gluconate. Apply with friction in concentric circles from center outward. Allow to fully dry.
21
Apply sterile cover to the transducer with sterile coupling gel inside the cover.
22
Apply sterile gel to the patient's skin at the imaging site.
23
Draw up the local anesthetic. Verify agent, concentration, and volume against the pre-calculated dose. Label the syringe.
24
Prepare a separate syringe with 2–3 mL of normal saline or D5W for hydrodissection.
Needle Insertion & Positioning
25
Re-image the target with the sterile transducer cover in place. Confirm the nerve is clearly visible and the trajectory remains free of vessels.
26
Raise a skin wheal with 0.5–1 mL of 1% lidocaine at the planned needle entry point.
27
Insert the block needle at the planned entry point, maintaining the chosen approach (IP or OOP). Keep the needle in the plane of the ultrasound beam at all times.
28
Advance the needle slowly, tracking the tip continuously on US. Do not advance if the tip is not visible.
  • IP: confirm the bright hyperechoic needle shaft and tip are advancing toward the target
  • OOP: track the hyperechoic cross-section dot advancing through tissue layers
29
Once the needle tip is in the anticipated target zone — pause advancement.
30
Confirm tip position with hydrodissection:
  • Inject 0.5–2 mL of saline or D5W
  • Confirm fluid spreads in the correct tissue plane adjacent to the target nerve
  • If fluid disappears without visible spread: reposition the needle and repeat
31
If paresthesia is reported on needle advancement: withdraw 1–2 mm. Do not inject at a position that causes paresthesia.
32
Reconfirm tip position with hydrodissection if the needle has been moved.
Injection
33
Aspirate for 3–5 seconds. Check for blood or air return.
  • Blood return: withdraw needle, apply pressure, reassess and replan
  • Air return (rare): may indicate pleural proximity — stop and reassess
  • No return: proceed to Step 34
34
If epinephrine is included: inject a 3 mL test dose first. Monitor ECG for 60 seconds for HR increase >20 bpm. Absence of response does NOT exclude intravascular injection.
35
Begin injection in 2–3 mL aliquots:
  • Inject the first aliquot slowly over 15–20 seconds
  • Watch the US image for hypoechoic fluid spread around the nerve
  • Watch the patient for CNS symptoms: agitation, metallic taste, confusion
  • Watch the ECG for arrhythmia
36
After each aliquot: aspirate again before continuing.
37
Pause 15–30 seconds between aliquots to allow time for early toxicity signs to manifest.
38
STOP AND REASSESS if:
  • Patient reports paresthesia, burning, or unusual sensation
  • Resistance is high or increases unexpectedly
  • Nerve cross-sectional area increases on US (possible intraneural injection)
  • Patient reports metallic taste, tinnitus, dizziness, or anxiety
  • Heart rate change on ECG consistent with intravascular injection
  • Any hemodynamic change
39
If spread is unilateral or incomplete:
  • Pause injection
  • Withdraw needle partially under US visualization
  • Redirect to the opposite aspect of the nerve
  • Aspirate, confirm tip position, and continue injection
40
Once the full planned volume is injected, withdraw the needle smoothly.
41
Apply gentle manual pressure over the injection site for 30–60 seconds to reduce hematoma risk and encourage local anesthetic spread.
Post-Procedure Monitoring & Assessment
42
Maintain continuous monitoring (SpO₂, ECG, NIBP) for a minimum of 30 minutes after injection.
43
Assess the patient every 5 minutes for:
  • CNS symptoms: perioral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus, visual changes, anxiety, confusion
  • Hemodynamic changes: heart rate, blood pressure, rhythm
  • Respiratory changes: rate, effort, oxygen saturation
44
Assess block onset at 10 and 20 minutes:
  • Sensory: cold discrimination, pinprick, light touch in the target distribution
  • Motor: strength testing appropriate to the blocked nerve
  • Compare to baseline documentation from Step 2
45
At 30 minutes, perform a final onset assessment.
  • Adequate block: proceed with planned procedure
  • Incomplete block: reassess anatomy with US, consider supplemental injection or alternative strategy
  • Block failure: document, assess for technical cause, pursue alternative approach
46
Document the procedure note (see Phase 6.2 for required elements).
47
Counsel the patient on:
  • Expected block duration
  • Limb protection requirements (sling, non-weight-bearing, eye patch as indicated)
  • Fall precautions if lower extremity blocked
  • Return precautions and contact information
LAST Recognition & Response — Emergency Sequence

If LAST is suspected at any point during or after the procedure:

A
STOP injection immediately.
B
Call for help. Announce "possible LAST" to all team members.
C
Ensure airway patency. Apply 100% O₂ via face mask. Prepare for intubation.
D
Treat seizures if present: midazolam 2–5 mg IV or lorazepam 2 mg IV. Avoid propofol as primary seizure management.
E
Administer Intralipid 20%:
  • Bolus: 1.5 mL/kg IBW IV over 2–3 minutes
  • Begin infusion: 0.25 mL/kg/min
  • Repeat bolus once if no improvement at 5 minutes
  • Increase infusion to 0.5 mL/kg/min if instability continues
F
If cardiac arrest: initiate CPR. Use reduced epinephrine (10–100 mcg IV boluses). Avoid vasopressin, CCBs, beta-blockers.
G
Prepare for prolonged resuscitation — cardiac recovery from bupivacaine toxicity can require 60+ minutes. Consider ECMO early if available.
H
Transfer to monitored setting for minimum 4–6 hours post-event resolution. Risk of recurrence from redistribution.
