19.1 Upper Extremity Blocks

Brachial plexus blockade at four levels, proximal to distal. Choice of level trades block extent against risk (phrenic involvement, pneumothorax).

BlockTarget / LevelTransducer & ApproachKey Landmarks & Pearls
InterscaleneC5–C7 roots/trunksLinear, transverse, lateral neck at cricoid levelRoots stacked between anterior and middle scalene (“traffic light” sign) lateral to carotid/IJ. Covers shoulder, proximal humerus. Near-universal ipsilateral hemidiaphragm paresis — avoid in significant pulmonary disease or contralateral phrenic palsy.
SupraclavicularTrunks/divisionsLinear, supraclavicular fossa, probe parallel to clavicle“Bunch of grapes” lateral/superior to subclavian artery, superficial to first rib; pleura immediately deep. Dense, fast-onset block for the whole arm — highest pneumothorax risk of the four levels.
InfraclavicularCordsLinear (or curvilinear if deep), parasagittal deltopectoral grooveLateral/posterior/medial cords clustered around the axillary artery, deep to pec major and pec minor. Stable catheter position for continuous infusion; needle trajectory steeper and deeper than supraclavicular.
AxillaryTerminal branches (median, ulnar, radial, musculocutaneous)Linear, arm abducted 90°, axillary creaseMedian/ulnar/radial nerves circle the axillary artery; musculocutaneous nerve separates early, sits within coracobrachialis — block it separately. No pneumothorax risk; compressible site.
Phrenic Nerve

Interscalene block causes ipsilateral hemidiaphragm paresis in the large majority of patients. Avoid bilateral interscalene blocks and use caution with pre-existing contralateral phrenic palsy or significant respiratory compromise — consider supraclavicular or infraclavicular as an alternative when the surgical field allows.

19.4 Needle Visualization & Technique
In-Plane (IP)
  • Needle inserted along the long axis of the transducer — entire shaft and tip visible throughout
  • Preferred approach for most blocks
  • If the tip is lost: stop advancing, tilt or slide the transducer until it reappears
Out-of-Plane (OOP)
  • Needle perpendicular to the beam — only a cross-section (bright dot) is visible
  • That dot is the tip only when the beam intersects it precisely; it may represent the shaft instead
  • Always confirm final tip position with hydrodissection before injecting
Sonoanatomy

Peripheral nerves appear hyperechoic with internal hypoechoic fascicular bundles (“honeycomb” pattern in short axis; parallel cable in long axis). Tendons can mimic nerves on a static image — trace proximally: tendons attach to muscle, nerves do not.

Hydrodissection: inject 0.5–2 mL of saline or D5W (D5W is more echogenic, isosmotic, and non-neurotoxic) to confirm tip position before committing to local anesthetic. Correct placement shows fluid spreading in the expected tissue plane and separating the nerve from surrounding fascia; if fluid disappears without visible spread, reposition and repeat.

Needle selection: 21–22G, length matched to target depth and approach angle. Echogenic (textured/blunt-tip) needles improve tip conspicuity, most valuable for OOP and deep targets. Short-bevel needles give better tactile feedback crossing fascial planes.

19.5 LAST Protocol — Recognition & Management

Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) is the most feared complication of peripheral nerve blockade. Bupivacaine is the most cardiotoxic agent (slow dissociation from cardiac sodium channels, additional calcium-channel and mitochondrial effects of the R(+) enantiomer). Onset is typically within 60 seconds of intravascular injection but can be delayed up to 30 minutes with perineural absorption — LAST can present as cardiovascular collapse without antecedent CNS symptoms, particularly with bupivacaine, rapid injection, or in a sedated patient.

Early CNS Excitation
  • Perioral/tongue numbness, metallic taste
  • Tinnitus, visual disturbance
  • Anxiety, agitation, confusion
  • Progresses to seizure, then CNS depression and respiratory arrest
Cardiovascular
  • Bradycardia, QRS widening, PR prolongation
  • Ventricular arrhythmia (VT/VF)
  • Cardiovascular collapse — can occur without preceding CNS signs
StepAction
1Stop injection immediately; call for help — announce “possible LAST”
2Secure airway: 100% O₂, prevent hypoxia and acidosis (both potentiate toxicity); intubate if needed
3Treat seizures: midazolam 2–5 mg IV or lorazepam 2 mg IV — avoid propofol at seizure-suppressing doses in a hemodynamically unstable patient
4Intralipid 20%: bolus 1.5 mL/kg ideal body weight IV over 2–3 min, then infusion 0.25 mL/kg/min (increase to 0.5 mL/kg/min if instability persists); repeat bolus once if no improvement at 5 min; max ≈12 mL/kg in the first 30 min
5Cardiac arrest: CPR + reduced-dose epinephrine (10–100 mcg IV boluses); avoid vasopressin, calcium-channel blockers, beta-blockers, and propofol
6Prolonged resuscitation may be required — bupivacaine cardiotoxicity can take 60+ minutes to resolve; consider ECMO early if available
7Transfer to a monitored setting for a minimum of 4–6 hours post-resolution — recurrence can occur from redistribution
